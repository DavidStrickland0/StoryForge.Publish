# Chapter 6: The Weight of Water

The sun did not rise so much as it asserted itself, a slow, bruised spreading of orange and yellow that turned the stone walls of the pen from cold gray to a suffocating, dusty amber. The heat was already building, a physical weight pressing down on Caelen’s chest. He had not slept. In the Marines, sleep was a skill you flipped on when the adrenaline faded; here, it was a luxury that required a negotiation he was not yet qualified for.

He sat against the wall, his legs stretched out, his back rigid. The boy was nearby, further away than he had been at night. The boy had moved during the dark hours, shifting closer to the center of the pen where the shadows pooled, then retreating again as the first light touched the ground. Caelen watched the movement in the corner of his eye. The boy’s shoulders were still hunched, a defensive posture that had become second nature. Caelen did not reach for him. He simply existed in the space, a fixed point in the shifting anxiety of the other captives.

The gate clanked open. The sound was sharp, violent in the stillness of the early morning. Two guards entered. They were not the same two as the previous night, or perhaps they were; the faces were blurred by the dust and the low light, but the uniforms were identical. Coarse brown tunics, leather belts, staffs that looked heavy and blunt. One of them carried a large, wooden bucket. The water inside sloshed, a dark, murky liquid that caught the light.

The guard with the bucket did not look at Caelen. He walked to the center of the pen, where a small, stone basin sat. It was dry, cracked, and stained with old algae. The guard poured the water. The sound was a dull, rhythmic splash. He then turned and walked to the gate, where a pile of large, jagged stone blocks sat. They were not decorative. They were heavy, rough-hewn, and clearly meant to be moved.

Caelen’s eyes narrowed. He watched the second guard, the one who had held the canteen the night before. This guard walked over to the stone blocks. He placed a hand on one. It was a massive slab, easily as long as Caelen’s arm and twice as thick. The guard strained, his face reddening, his boots slipping in the packed dirt. He managed to shift it a few inches. Then he stopped, wiping his forehead with the back of his hand. He looked at the pile, then at the other guard, who was still by the basin, watching the other slaves drink.

The other slaves were drinking. They huddled around the basin, cupping their hands, their faces twisted in desperate greed. The water was scarce. The guard did not stop them. He did not encourage them. He simply stood there, a silent observer of their desperation.

Caelen stood up.

The movement was slow, deliberate. He walked past the basin, past the huddled group of slaves, and stopped beside the guard who was struggling with the stone block. The guard looked up, his eyes hard, his grip on the stone tightening. He did not raise his staff. He did not back down. He was a man doing a job, and the job was difficult.

Caelen looked at the stone. Then he looked at the guard.

He stepped forward and placed his hands on the stone.

The guard’s eyes widened. He released his grip, stepping back a half-step. The stone was still heavy, but it was not immovable. Caelen could feel the density of it, the way the gravity of this world pulled at it with a strength that felt almost personal. He braced his feet, digging his toes into the dirt. He did not use a technique. He did not use leverage. He simply used mass.

He lifted.

The stone rose. The sound it made was a low, grinding groan, the friction of rock on rock. Caelen’s muscles burned, a familiar, sharp ache that he had learned to ignore. He held the stone for a moment, letting the guard see the stability of his hold. Then he moved it. He placed it neatly on top of the other pile, a precise, controlled motion that suggested he had done this a thousand times.

The guard stared at him. The other guard, by the basin, had turned. The slaves had stopped drinking. The silence in the pen was absolute, broken only by the dripping of water from the basin.

Caelen did not look at them. He looked at the guard who had been struggling. He pointed to the stone. Then he pointed to the basin. Then he pointed to himself.

*Work. For water.*

The guard’s mouth twitched. It was a calculation. He looked at the pile of stones. There were at least ten more. If he moved them, it would take him an hour. If Caelen moved them, it would take him ten minutes.

The guard reached for his belt. He pulled out the canteen. He did not offer it. He held it out, the leather worn smooth, the metal cap glinting in the harsh light. He held it for a long, agonizing second. Then he tossed it.

Caelen caught it.

The weight of it was solid, real. He uncapped it. The water inside was cold, clean, and tasted of iron and stone. He drank. He did not gulp. He drank slowly, savoring the relief, the cooling of his throat, the settling of his stomach. He drank until the panic in his chest subsided, replaced by a cold, clear focus.

When he was finished, he capped the canteen. He did not throw it back. He held it in his hand, a small, tangible thing that he had earned.

The guard watched him. Then he pointed to the next stone.

Caelen looked at the stone. He looked at the guard. He picked up the stone.

He worked.

The pen was quiet. The other slaves watched, their eyes wide, their hands still cupped around the basin. They did not speak. They did not move. They watched Caelen move stone after stone, a rhythmic, efficient dance of strength and control. He did not rest. He did not pause. He moved them with a precision that was almost beautiful, a stark contrast to the chaos of the pen.

The boy watched from the shadows. His eyes were fixed on Caelen, on the way he moved, on the way the guard accepted his labor without a second thought. There was a new quality to the boy’s gaze. It was understanding.

Caelen finished the last stone. He stood back, his breathing steady, his muscles humming with exhaustion. He felt strong. He felt useful.

The guard walked over. He did not look at Caelen. He looked at the pile of stones. He nodded, a sharp, decisive movement. Then he reached into his belt again. This time, he pulled out a small, flat piece of cloth. He placed it on the ground, near the basin. It was a rag, dirty and stained, but it was a place to lie. A designated spot.

Caelen looked at the rag. Then he looked at the guard.

The guard pointed to the rag. Then he pointed to the canteen. Then he walked back to the gate.

Caelen picked up the rag. He picked up the canteen. He walked to the spot by the wall, away from the basin, away from the other slaves. He lay down. He placed the rag under his head. He held the canteen in his hand.

The boy was there. He had moved closer, but he was still a few feet away. He did not speak. He did not ask for water. He simply sat there, watching Caelen.

Caelen looked at him. He did not offer the canteen. He simply held the boy’s gaze for a moment. Then he closed his eyes.

The heat was still there, a heavy, suffocating blanket. But the panic was gone. He had traded his strength for his survival. It was a fair trade. It was a bounded trade. He was still a slave. He was still in the pen. But he had a place. He had water. He had a routine.

And in this place, that was enough.

The boy stayed. He did not leave. He sat in the shadow of the wall, his back straight, his eyes open. He watched the gate. He watched the guards. He watched Caelen.

Caelen slept.

The sun moved across the sky, casting long, distorted shadows across the pen. The other slaves came and went, drinking, resting, enduring. The guards patrolled the perimeter, their staffs resting on their shoulders. The world continued.

And Caelen, in his designated spot, with his canteen in his hand, dreamed of nothing.

He woke as the light began to fade, the orange and yellow of the sunset bleeding into the dark. The boy was still there. He had not moved. He was watching the stars, which were beginning to appear, one by one, through the square of sky above the pen.

Caelen sat up. The canteen was still full. He had not drunk it all. He had saved some.

He looked at the boy. The boy looked back.

Caelen uncapped the canteen. He offered it.

The boy hesitated. He looked at the guards. They were distant, their attention focused on the gate. They were not looking at this small transaction in the center of the pen.

The boy reached out. He took the canteen. He drank.

He did not gulp. He drank slowly, savoring the coolness, the relief. He drank until the panic in his chest subsided, replaced by a cold, clear focus.

When he was finished, he capped the canteen. He handed it back to Caelen.

Caelen took it. He looked at the boy. Then he looked at the numbers in his vision.

**1**
**1**

They were still the same. But the left one felt lighter. The right one felt heavier.

He had secured his survival for the day. He had obtained the water he needed. And he had done something else. He had chosen to share the surplus. In the cold, dark pen, that choice felt like a spark in the void.

He leaned back against the wall, closing his eyes. The thirst was gone, replaced by the dull ache of a full stomach and tired limbs. The panic was gone. He had bought himself a little time. And in this place, time was the only currency that mattered.

The boy sat down, clutching his empty pot. He did not look at Caelen anymore. He looked at the ground. But his shoulders were less hunched. He was breathing easier.

Caelen watched the stars appear, one by one, through the square of sky above the pen. They were bright, cold, and distant. He watched them, and for the first time since he had woken in this world, he did not feel alone. He was not a Marine. He was not a slave. He was Caelen. And he was going to play this game to the end.

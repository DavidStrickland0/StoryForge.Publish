The mud in the trench was thick enough to swallow a boot, and the smell of cordite hung in the air like a physical weight. Kaelen lay prone, his cheek pressed against the cold, wet earth, his rifle barrel resting on the parapet. His fingers were slick with sweat, a stark contrast to the chill creeping up his spine. He wasn’t thinking about the war, or the cause, or the flag pinned to his chest. He was thinking about the promotion board back in the States, and how much easier the paperwork would be if he kept his head down and his kill count high.

"Easy, easy," Sergeant Miller’s voice crackled through the static of the radio, a distant, tinny whisper. "Don’t let ’em see the line. Keep it tight."

Kaelen adjusted his breathing, forcing the panic out of his lungs. He was eighteen, the youngest in the squad, and he knew it. He also knew that being the youngest meant being expendable, a statistic to be tallied in a report that no one would read twice. He had joined the Marines for the benefits, the structure, the way the uniform made his shoulders look broader and his voice sound older. It was a transaction. He gave them his time, his body, and his obedience; they gave him a way out of the small, suffocating town where he had grown up. It was a fair deal, or so he told himself.

A burst of fire snapped the air above his head, kicking up a spray of dirt and debris. Somewhere to his left, Corporal Vance swore, the sound sharp and frustrated. Vance was a good soldier, reliable and steady, but he had a weakness that Kaelen had learned to exploit. Vance cared about the rules. Vance cared about the mission in the abstract. Kaelen only cared about the mission in the immediate, physical sense: staying alive and getting credit.

"Vance, hold your position," Kaelen said, his voice flat. "I’ve got eyes on the ridge."

It was a lie. He didn’t have eyes on the ridge. He had eyes on the enemy’s secondary line, where they were regrouping. He could see the gaps in their armor, the hesitation in their movement. He wanted to take them out, not to protect the trench, but because it would look good on the after-action report. It would show initiative. It would show aggression. It was the kind of behavior that got noticed by the officers who actually had the power to move a man up the ladder.

He raised his rifle, peering through the scope. The world narrowed to a circular view, a tiny, manageable slice of chaos. He lined up a target, a soldier crouching behind a makeshift barricade. The man was moving, reloading, vulnerable. Kaelen felt a surge of satisfaction, a cold, sharp pleasure that he didn’t quite understand. It was the feeling of having the upper hand, of knowing something the other man didn’t. He pulled the trigger.

The recoil was a familiar shock against his shoulder. The target dropped, and the satisfaction deepened. He didn’t check to see if the shot had been precise, or if it had been clean. He just knew he had hit, and that was enough.

"Contact front, two hundred meters," he called out, his voice steady. "Taking them down."

It was a small victory, a drop in the bucket of the war, but it felt like a step forward. It was the kind of step that mattered. It was the kind of step that separated the men who survived from the men who didn’t. And in that moment, lying in the mud with the taste of dust in his mouth, Kaelen felt more powerful than he had in years.

The radio crackled again, this time with a warning. "Incoming, everyone! Hold your positions!"

Kaelen didn’t move. He stayed prone, his eyes still on the scope, waiting for the next opening. He was calculating the odds, weighing the risks. He knew that staying still was the safest bet, but he also knew that staying still was boring. And boredom was a luxury he couldn’t afford.

He shifted his weight, feeling the mud seep into the fabric of his uniform. It was cold, uncomfortable, and real. It grounded him. It reminded him that he was alive, that his body was still a thing that could feel, could hurt, could die. And that was the problem with the job. You had to be willing to let your body be a tool, a thing to be used and discarded. Kaelen had learned that lesson early. He had learned that the only way to survive was to be better than the other guys, to be smarter, faster, more ruthless.

He looked to his right, where a young Private named Harris was shaking. Harris was new, still had the look of a kid about to throw up. He was terrified, and his fear was contagious. Kaelen resented it. He resented the way Harris’s fear made him look weak, made the whole squad look weak. If Harris had joined the Marines for the same reasons Kaelen had, he would know better. He would know that fear was a luxury, a distraction.

"Hey, Harris," Kaelen said, his voice low. "Quit shaking. It’s making me nervous."

It was a cruel thing to say, but Kaelen said it anyway. It was the kind of thing that kept the hierarchy clear. It reminded Harris of his place in the world. And if it made Harris shake more, well, that was his problem, not Kaelen’s.

Harris didn’t respond. He just kept shaking, his eyes wide and darting around the trench. Kaelen turned his head away, focusing on the scope again. He didn’t want to look at the boy. He didn’t want to see the fear in his eyes. It was a reminder of what he was, what he had become. A man who used other people’s weakness to make himself feel strong.

He adjusted his aim, his fingers moving with practiced ease. The war was a game, and he was winning. Or so he thought. He didn’t know about the mother, or the child, or the shot that was coming. He only knew that he was ready, that he was strong, that he was the one who would survive.

The next round of fire was closer, the sound deafening. Kaelen pressed his helmet down, his heart pounding in his chest. He was afraid, but he hid it. He hid it behind the scope, behind the calculation, behind the cold, hard logic of survival. And in that moment, he felt more alive than he ever had in his life.

The return of sensation was violent.

It did not arrive as a gentle tide, as the void had promised, but as a physical assault. Caelen gasped, a ragged, wet sound that tore from a throat that felt too tight, too raw. His eyes snapped open, and the world struck him with the force of a sledgehammer.

Light. Blinding, amber-colored light.

He was lying on his back, staring up at a sky that was wrong. The atmosphere hung heavy and thick, smelling of sulfur, crushed pine, and distant rain. The air was humid, clinging to his skin like a second layer of sweat. He tried to sit up, and his muscles responded with a strength that felt alien and intoxicating. There was no stiffness, no ache, no fatigue. He was eighteen, perhaps nineteen, in a body that had been forged in a furnace of perfect efficiency.

He pushed himself up onto his hands and knees. The ground beneath him was cool, dark earth, packed tight in a shallow depression. He looked down at his hands. They were large, the knuckles prominent, the skin tanned and unblemished. He flexed his fingers, feeling the tendons slide with hydraulic precision. He was at peak condition. Every instinct screamed that this was a trap, a hallucination, a fever dream in the void, but the grit of the soil beneath his palms and the weight of his own limbs told a different story. He was real. He was here.

He stood, his legs steady, and looked around.

He was in a pit. The walls of the depression were smooth, carved from the local stone, rising perhaps ten feet above his head. Beyond the rim, the world was a blur of alien vegetation. Trees with bark like twisted iron, leaves that shimmered with an oily sheen. The sky above was a deep, bruised purple, streaked with clouds that moved with sluggish, heavy grace.

Caelen’s mind raced. *Where am I?*

He knew nothing. He had no map, no compass, no knowledge of the language, the customs, or the dangers of this place. He was a Marine, trained to assess a situation, identify threats, and formulate a course of action. But he had no data. No intelligence. He was blind in a land of monsters.

He took a deep breath, forcing himself to calm the panic that was spiking in his chest. *Think. Observe. Survive.*

He checked his body again. No wounds. No scars. He was wearing nothing but a rough, coarse tunic that had materialized on his skin, the fabric stiff and smelling of dye. It was a prisoner’s cloth, or a laborer’s, he guessed. It offered no protection, no tools, no weapon.

He was alone.

For a moment, the stillness of the pit was absolute. Then, the sound of heavy boots crunching on gravel broke the silence.

Caelen spun around, his hand moving instinctively to his hip, finding nothing but empty air. He was unarmed.

Three figures emerged from the tree line, descending into the pit with the casual confidence of men who owned the ground they walked on. They were dressed in leather armor that looked expensive, reinforced with metal plates. They carried staffs topped with curved blades, and their eyes were hard, calculating. They did not look like soldiers in a combat zone. They looked like merchants.

The leader, a man with a shaved head and a scar running through his left eyebrow, stopped at the edge of the pit. He looked down at Caelen, his gaze sweeping over the young man’s body with the detached appraisal of a butcher inspecting a side of beef.

Caelen held his ground. He did not raise his hands. He did not back away. He met the man’s eyes, letting the cold, dead stare of a predator meet his.

The leader spoke.

The words were a string of guttural, clicking sounds, rapid and sharp. Caelen didn’t understand a single syllable. But he understood the tone. It was not curiosity. It was transaction.

The man gestured to his companions. They moved forward, not with aggression, but with purpose. One of them, a woman with close-cropped hair and a scar on her cheek, produced a length of rope from her belt. It was thick, braided, and reinforced with wire.

Caelen’s heart rate remained steady. *They want to take me.*

He knew what this was. He had seen this dynamic a thousand times in the videos, the reports, the declassified files. This was capture. This was inventory.

The woman approached him. Caelen did not move. He watched her hands, watched the way she coiled the rope, watched the subtle shift in her weight as she prepared to strike. He was a Marine. He knew how to fight. But he was alone, unarmed, and outnumbered three to one. He was also a stranger in a hostile environment, with no allies, no reinforcements, and no way to know if the ropes were simple bindings or something worse.

Resistance was not an option. Not yet.

As the woman lunged, Caelen relaxed his muscles. It was a tactical choice. He would not struggle. He would not scream. He would let them bind his hands behind his back, the wire biting into his wrists, the rough rope digging into his skin. He kept his face blank, his eyes fixed on the leader.

*Assess,* he told himself. *Remember. Everything.*

The leader watched Caelen’s stillness with a flicker of surprise, then respect. A wild animal that did not thrash was more valuable than one that broke its own legs in the struggle.

The leader spoke again, this time pointing to Caelen’s chest, then to his own. Then he made a gesture that seemed to indicate value, or perhaps weight.

Caelen nodded slowly.

It was a lie. He didn’t know what they were saying. But he knew the rhythm of the exchange. He was being assessed. He was being priced.

The leader turned and spoke to his companions. They began to lead Caelen out of the pit. The ground was uneven, the rocks sharp under his bare feet. He followed, his steps measured, his breathing controlled. He was no longer a Marine. He was stock. He was a commodity.

As they climbed out of the pit, the world expanded. The forest was dense, but there was a path, worn smooth by the traffic of many feet. And beyond the trees, in the distance, he saw it.

A city.

It rose from the earth like a tumor of stone and metal. Towers of black rock pierced the purple sky, connected by bridges that seemed to defy gravity. The architecture was brutal, imposing, designed to intimidate. There were no windows, only slits for light and arrows. The streets below were a hive of activity, a swarm of people moving in organized, fearful lines.

Caelen’s stomach churned. He recognized the structure. He had seen it in the dreams, in the fragments of memory that had surfaced in the void. This was a place of order. A place of control.

The slavers walked him toward the city, their staffs resting easily on their shoulders. They did not guard him tightly. They did not need to. He was alone. He was confused. He was weak.

Or so they thought.

Caelen walked with them, his mind working. He was in a society built on hierarchy. He could see it in the way the people in the streets parted for the slavers, in the way they kept their eyes downcast. This was a world of Dominion. A world where power was the only currency that mattered.

And he had something they wanted. He had strength. He had youth. He had a body that had been engineered for peak performance.

He would survive this. He would adapt. He would learn their language, their laws, their weaknesses. And when the time was right, he would strike.

But for now, he was just another piece of meat in the market.

The slavers led him into the city, the noise of the streets rising to a deafening roar. Caelen kept his head down, his eyes open, his mind sharp. He was no longer in the void. He was no longer a dream.

He was in the crucible.

And he had a long way to go.

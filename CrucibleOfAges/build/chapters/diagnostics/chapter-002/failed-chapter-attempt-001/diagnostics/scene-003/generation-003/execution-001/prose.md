The silence in the void had a texture, or at least that is how Caelen’s mind tried to translate the absence of sound into something comprehensible. It was not the muffled quiet of a hospital room, nor the heavy stillness of a held breath. It was a flat, infinite expanse where noise simply did not exist to be drowned out. He had been drifting in this state for what felt like hours, or perhaps moments, for time had lost its rigid structure here. The panic that had initially gripped his consciousness—that frantic, clawing need to locate a body, to feel the cold air against his skin or the ache of a broken bone—had faded into a distant, dull thrum. He was still afraid, but it was a cold fear now, the kind that settled into the marrow of his thoughts rather than spiking in his chest.

He turned his attention back to the interface. It hung there in the periphery of his mind, a faint, geometric lattice of red and silver lines that he could perceive without looking, a sensation more like a change in pressure than a visual image. The red was deep and visceral, a color he associated with dried blood and the heat of a rifle barrel. The silver was pale and distant, like moonlight on steel. Both were at Level 1. The numbers were identical, a perfect, balanced symmetry that made his stomach churn with a vague, inexplicable unease.

Caelen focused on the red lines. He tried to push against them, to feel for a handle or a lever, to demand an explanation. *What are you? Why are you here? How do I use this?* The interface did not respond. It did not shift, glow brighter, or offer a menu of options. It simply remained, a passive record of his existence. He probed the silver lines next, searching for a connection to the mother in the courtyard, to the weight of her dead infant, to the final, shattering moment of his own heart stopping. The silver was just as inert as the red. It was a ledger, a receipt for a transaction he had not asked to make.

The cognitive dissonance was exhausting. Caelen had always operated on a simple, brutal logic: take what you need, leave no trace, and if someone got in the way, remove them. He had used Private Davis’s ignorance to secure a spot in the squad he wanted. He had let Sergeant Miller believe in his blind obedience to maintain the image of a loyal, efficient tool. Those actions had been selfish, calculated, and entirely successful. They had been the stuff of his identity, the proof that he was more than just a scared kid from a small town who had joined the Marines to escape his own insignificance. And yet, here was the red line, Level 1, standing beside the silver line, Level 1, as if the two were equal in weight and value.

He had given his life for a stranger. The thought felt absurd, a glitch in his programming. He had not calculated it. He had not weighed the moral value of a civilian against his own survival. He had simply moved, a reflex born of a sudden, violent rejection of the geometry of the moment. He had wanted to be seen as something other than a weapon, and in that split second, he had chosen to be human. Now, he was dead, and the universe had stamped a price tag on his soul.

"Alright," he thought, though the word did not exist in the void. It was just a concept, a mental marker. "What do I do?"

There was no answer. The interface did not provide instructions. It did not offer a tutorial or a path forward. It simply existed, a permanent fixture of his new reality. Caelen felt the weight of his own insignificance settle over him. He was not a hero. He was not a chosen one. He was an eighteen-year-old boy who had died in a ditch in a country whose name he would probably never learn, and he was stuck in a waiting room with no door and no clock.

He let his attention drift from the interface. The red and silver lines faded slightly, receding into the background of his awareness like a song playing in another room. He did not force them away; he simply stopped focusing on them. The exhaustion that had been building in his mind began to take hold, a heavy, drowsy fog that made the edges of his thoughts blur. He could still see the shapes, still feel the presence of the two paths, but the urgency to understand them had dissipated. There was no point in fighting a force that did not care about his struggle.

His memories of the last life were starting to soften. The sharp, metallic taste of blood was gone. The sound of Miller’s rifle was no longer a deafening crack but a distant, muffled report. The face of the mother, the hollow of her eyes, the bundle in her arms—these details remained, but the frantic, desperate intensity that had accompanied them was fading. He remembered the act, but the emotion behind it was becoming a story he told himself rather than a trauma he lived through. It was a strange, peaceful sensation. For the first time in his life, he was not fighting for something, not trying to prove himself, not scheming or surviving. He was just existing.

Caelen settled into the void. He did not close his eyes, because he did not have them. He did not breathe, because he did not have lungs. He simply allowed his consciousness to spread out into the nothingness, accepting the suspension of animation. He was waiting for something, though he did not know what. Perhaps it was another chance. Perhaps it was another world. Perhaps it was just the end of it all. But for now, in the colorless, boundaryless expanse, there was only the quiet hum of the interface, the faint whisper of red and silver, and the slow, inevitable softening of the man he had been.

The silence stretched, thick and viscous, like cold tar settling in a hollow. Caelen did not move, for there was no body left to move, yet the sensation of stillness remained, a heavy anchor dragging through the gray mist. He floated in the suspension, the interface hovering in his peripheral awareness like a second pair of eyes that refused to blink.

In the void, time was a strange, malleable thing. It did not tick or chime; it pooled. Moments could last for what felt like hours, or hours could collapse into a single, sharp intake of breath that he no longer possessed. Caelen had been waiting, or so he told himself, though the distinction between waiting and existing had begun to blur. The gray mist swirled around him, indifferent and cold, offering no comfort and no judgment. It was merely the space between the ending of one story and the beginning of another, a vast, empty stage where the actors had gone home and the lights had not yet been turned off.

He turned his attention back to the interface. It was still there, sharp and geometric against the soft, amorphous background of his consciousness. The two bars, one marked with a symbol that suggested weight and control, the other with a symbol that suggested release and loss, remained fixed at their single, solitary unit. One level. Just one. It was an absurdly small number, a rounding error in the grand ledger of the universe, and yet it sat in his mind with the weight of a verdict.

Caelen tried to push the interface away, to shelve it in some back corner of his mind where he could ignore it, where he could pretend it was a hallucination born of dying trauma. He focused on the memory of the courtyard, the dust, the heat of the sun on his neck, the smell of cordite and sweat. He tried to reconstruct the feeling of the ground beneath his boots, the solid reality of the world he had left behind. But the interface did not budge. It was not a thought he could dismiss; it was a fact, as concrete as the stone of the wall he had hidden behind, as undeniable as the blood drying on his skin.

He stopped trying to push it away. Instead, he looked at it with the detached curiosity of a soldier assessing a new piece of equipment. He examined the sharp lines of the symbols, the clean edges of the bars. There was no warmth in it, no divine radiance, no comforting voice to guide him. It was a tool, cold and precise, and it was waiting for him to use it.

The realization settled into him with a slow, creeping dread. He was not special in the way he had once imagined, not a hero chosen by fate, not a savior sent by the gods. He was a data point. A variable in an equation he could not solve. The System did not care about his intentions, his sacrifices, or his regrets. It simply recorded them, quantified them, and offered him the ability to direct the flow of his own soul.

Caelen felt a strange, hollow laugh rise in his throat, a sound that died before it could fully form. It was a dark, bitter thing, devoid of humor, a reflection of the absurdity of his situation. Here he was, dead in a gray mist, holding a cheat code for the universe, and the only thing he wanted was to go home. He wanted to sit in the mess hall, eat a cold meal, and argue with Private Davis about the price of cigarettes. He wanted to feel the sting of the wind on his face and the ache of muscle fatigue in his legs. He wanted to be human, flawed and fragile and temporary.

But he was none of those things now. He was a soul in the void, suspended in the gap between lives, equipped with a power that most people would kill for and a level of progress that would take a lifetime to earn. And he was alone.

The mist continued to swirl, indifferent to his despair. The interface remained, bright and sharp, a beacon of cold logic in the dark. Caelen looked at the two bars, Dominion and Sacrifice, and felt the weight of the choice that lay ahead. He could use this power to dominate, to control, to seize what he wanted by force or by cunning. He could use it to give, to serve, to sacrifice himself for others until nothing of himself remained. Or he could do both, balancing the scales, walking the tightrope between power and purpose.

He did not know which path he would choose. He did not know if he was capable of either. He was Caelen, an eighteen-year-old Marine who had spent his life running from who he was, and now he was dead, and the universe was asking him to decide who he wanted to be.

The question hung in the air, heavy and unanswered. Caelen did not have an answer. He did not have a plan. He had only the interface, the mist, and the long, silent wait for the next time he would be forced to choose.

He closed his eyes, or the mental equivalent of closing them, and let the silence of the void fill the space where his thoughts used to be. The interface remained, a fixed point in the darkness, a promise of power and a warning of cost. He would wait. He would think. He would survive, in this way, until the void released him and the next life began.

The mist swirled, and the lights in the distance remained fixed, a constellation of unknown significance. Caelen was alone with his numbers, and for the first time in his life, he had nothing to do but wait.

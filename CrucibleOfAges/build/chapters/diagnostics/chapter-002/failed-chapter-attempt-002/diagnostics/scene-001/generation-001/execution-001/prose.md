The silence was not an absence of sound, but a presence of stillness.

Caelen opened his eyes.

There was no ceiling. There was no courtyard. The dust that had coated his lips and filled his lungs was gone, replaced by a cool, dry air that tasted of nothing at all. He tried to sit up, expecting the grinding protest of his shattered chest, the fire in his thigh that had turned his blood to hot slush. He moved his fingers. He moved his legs. He moved his torso.

There was no pain.

He looked down at himself. He was wearing his uniform, the same olive drab fatigues he had worn for the last six months of his life. The fabric was clean. The mud that had caked his boots was gone. His body was intact. He could feel the weight of his own limbs, the steady rhythm of a heartbeat that was not frantic, not ragged, but slow and deep, like the pulse of a sleeping animal.

He was standing. Or rather, he was in a state where the concept of standing applied, though his feet did not seem to touch a floor. He was surrounded by a pale, diffuse light that had no source. It was the color of early morning on a winter day, the gray-white of a sky just before the sun breaks the horizon.

Caelen took a step forward. The ground, if there was one, felt solid but indistinct, like pressing his hand against a sheet of stretched silk. He walked. The movement was fluid, unburdenened by gravity or fatigue. He turned in a slow circle, his head tilting to listen.

Silence.

Not the silence of a held breath, or the silence of a library, or the silence of a tomb. This was the silence of a space where sound had never existed. It was a vacuum, but a gentle one. It did not press against his eardrums. It simply was.

He stopped walking. He looked at his hands. They were young hands, eighteen years old, but they did not look like the hands of a soldier. They looked like the hands of a boy who had just finished a long run and was waiting for his mother to call him home. The calluses on his palms, the scars from training accidents, the roughness of his knuckles—they were there, but they felt distant, like details in a painting he had seen years ago.

He tried to remember the courtyard.

The memory came, but it did not come with the visceral shock of the moment. It came as a scene. He saw the mud-brick walls, the way the sunlight hit the dust. He saw the mother crouched behind the rubble. He saw the rifle in his hand, then the rifle on the ground. He saw the flash of Miller’s muzzle.

But the sensation of the bullet hitting him was gone. The heat, the shock, the sudden, terrifying cold that had rushed up his spine—it was not there. He could recall the fact that he had been shot. He could recall the fact that he had died. But the experience of it was like reading about a stranger’s accident. He knew it had happened to him, but he could not feel it.

He tried to remember his mother’s face.

The image was there, but it was soft. The sharp edges of her worry, the specific crease between her eyebrows, the way her voice had cracked when she called his name—these details were fading, becoming blurred. He could remember that he had loved her. He could remember that he had been afraid of her disappointment. But the specific, burning pain of that fear was gone. It had been smoothed over, like a rough stone in a river.

He tried to remember his father.

The memory was even fainter. He knew his father was a drinker. He knew his father had left when Caelen was twelve. He knew he had resented him. But the resentment was gone. What remained was a vague sense of loss, a hollow feeling in the chest that did not hurt, but simply was.

Caelen stood in the pale light and watched the memories drift through his mind. They were like leaves falling from a tree, drifting down to the ground and settling there. Some were bright, some were dark. Some were sharp, some were dull. He watched them fall. He did not try to catch them. He did not try to hold them. He simply watched.

He realized that he was not afraid.

This was the most surprising thing. He had been afraid his whole life. Afraid of being ordinary, afraid of being weak, afraid of being forgotten. He had joined the Marines because he thought it would make him strong, make him important, make him real. He had killed because he thought it was necessary, because he thought it was what a man was supposed to do. But now, in this place of silence and light, the fear was gone.

He was not a Marine. He was not a son. He was not a killer. He was a consciousness, suspended in a space that had no time, no place, and no need.

He looked at his hands again. They were still there. He flexed his fingers. They moved. He clenched his fists. They tightened. He had a body. He had a self. But the self was different. It was stripped of its armor, its rank, its history. It was just a mind, aware of itself, aware of its own existence.

He thought about the mother.

He did not know if she was alive. He did not know if she had survived the firefight. He did not know if she had looked at his body and wept, or if she had looked away. He did not know if she would remember him, or if he would become a ghost in her life, a shadow on the edge of her memory.

But it did not matter.

The importance of her survival, the weight of his sacrifice, the glory of his death—these things had been heavy, pressing down on him, defining him. Now, they were just facts. They were part of the story of his life, but they were not the story. They were just a chapter, and the chapter had ended.

Caelen felt a strange sense of peace. It was not the peace of relief, or the peace of victory. It was the peace of a book being closed. The peace of a door being shut. The peace of a journey reaching its destination.

He stood in the void, watching the last of his memories settle like dust in the quiet air. He was alone. He was empty. He was full.

And he was waiting.

He did not know what he was waiting for. He did not know how long he would wait. He did not know if he would wait for days, or years, or centuries. But he knew that he was no longer in a rush. He was no longer running. He was no longer trying to prove anything.

He was simply there.

And in the silence, in the light, in the absence of pain and fear and time, Caelen began to dream.

The waiting was the hardest part. It wasn’t like sleeping, where the mind drifts into pleasant, nonsensical dreams, nor was it like being awake in a quiet room, where the silence eventually becomes a companion. It was a static hold, a suspended animation of the soul. Caelen found himself replaying the final moments of his life on Earth with the detached curiosity of a man reviewing a video of an accident he hadn’t caused.

He saw Miller’s face, the grim, set jaw, the way the sergeant’s eyes had narrowed not with malice, but with a cold, procedural certainty. Miller hadn’t hesitated. He hadn’t looked at the mother with horror or confusion. He had looked at Caelen with the same expression he used when checking a sight picture or logging ammunition counts. In that flash of memory, Caelen felt a spike of irritation that seemed entirely out of place for a dead man. He had been the one to break the line, the one to introduce the chaos, the variable that had ruined the sergeant’s clean geometry. And yet, looking at the silver thread of his progression, Caelen felt a strange, hollow pride. He had been the one to save the woman. He had been the one to stop the bullet.

He tried to summon the feeling of heroism, the swelling music that usually accompanied such moments in the movies he’d watched during downtime at the base. He tried to feel noble. Instead, he felt only the lingering taste of copper and the dull, throbbing ache in his chest that had faded into a phantom sensation long ago. He hadn’t done it because he was good. He had done it because the alternative—a world where a man like Miller got to decide who lived and who died based on a whim—was intolerable. It was a selfish act of preservation, a refusal to let the world become a place that didn’t align with his internal sense of order.

The System interface hovered at the edge of his perception, a faint geometric lattice of red and silver. Caelen focused on the red line. Dominion. Level 1. He remembered the weeks before the deployment, the way he had manipulated Private Davis into taking the worst watch shifts, the way he had subtly leaked information to Sergeant Miller about a suspected leak in the unit, framing a corporal who had crossed him. He had enjoyed it. He had liked the feeling of being the pivot point around which other people’s lives rotated. He had liked the power. And the System had acknowledged it, just as it had acknowledged the saving of the mother.

It was a ledger. That was the only word that fit. It didn’t judge. It didn’t praise. It simply recorded the exchange of value. Caelen had given his life, a finite resource, to prevent the death of a stranger. That was a transaction. He had used his social standing and information to manipulate his peers for personal comfort and status. That was another transaction. The System saw both, and it saw nothing wrong with the fact that they were equal.

He tried to push the interface away, to deny its existence, to pretend that he was just a soul in the void, waiting for whatever came next. But the grid remained, persistent and indifferent. It didn’t require his belief. It didn’t require his understanding. It was simply there, a tool he possessed without having earned the right to use it.

Caelen let out a breath that didn’t exist, a mental exhalation of frustration. He was eighteen years old. He had been a Marine. He had been a liar, a manipulator, and, in the end, a martyr. And now he was a point of data in a cosmic spreadsheet. The absurdity of it was almost funny, if he had a mouth to smile with.

He looked at the silver line again. Level 1. It was so thin, so fragile, that it felt like a lie. One act. One single, desperate lunge across a courtyard had given him this. What would it take to get to Level 2? Would he need to save another person? Would he need to give up something he wanted? Or would the System wait for a greater cost?

He didn’t know. And that was the problem. He was a soldier. He was used to clear objectives, defined boundaries, and measurable outcomes. Here, in the colorless expanse of the void, there were no orders. There was no mission. There was only the waiting, and the slow, inevitable softening of the edges of his memory.

The fear of dying was gone. The fear of the unknown was fading. What remained was a quiet, persistent doubt. He had saved the mother because he had to, because he couldn’t stand the idea of a world where that was allowed. But did that make him a good man? The System said yes, in its own way. It had awarded him the silver path. But Caelen knew the truth. He knew that if he had been in a different position, if he had been alone, if the cost had been higher, he might have stayed behind the wall. He might have let Miller fire. He might have chosen survival over principle.

The doubt was a heavy stone in his chest, heavier than the bullet. It was the part of him that had survived the crucible of his first life, the part that had learned to be selfish, to be ruthless, to be effective. That part of him was still there, watching, waiting, ready to take control the moment the void gave him a body and a world to conquer.

Caelen closed his eyes, though he had no eyelids. He let the interface fade further into the background, until it was just a faint hum in the back of his mind. He was tired. He was exhausted by the sheer effort of trying to process a reality that defied every law he had ever known.

He floated there, suspended in the nothing, his consciousness sharp and clear. The memories of the battlefield were still there, but the emotional intensity was already beginning to dull, the sharp edges of the fear and the pain softening under the weight of the void. He was waiting. He didn’t know for what, or how long. He just knew that the clock had started, and he had no way to stop it.

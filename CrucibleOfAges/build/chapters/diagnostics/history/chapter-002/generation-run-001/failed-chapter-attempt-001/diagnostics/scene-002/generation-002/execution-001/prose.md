The grid did not offer itself. It waited.

Caelen pushed harder, a mental pressure that felt like squinting against a glare that wasn't there. The faint lines sharpened, resolving from abstract suggestion into something with weight and definition. They were not decorative. They were structural. A skeleton of light, or perhaps of logic, laid over the featureless dark.

And then, as if the grid had been the cover, the interface bloomed.

It did not announce itself with thunder or divine proclamation. It simply appeared, projected onto the inner surface of his awareness, clear and cold and utterly devoid of personality. Two columns of text hung in the empty space before him. To the left, a label: **DOMINION**. Below it, a number: **1**. To the right, a label: **SACRIFICE**. Below it, a number: **1**.

Caelen stared at them. Or rather, he perceived them with the part of his mind that had learned to process threat assessments, intel reports, and order of battle diagrams. The military mind, the part of him that had kept him alive and respected and utterly empty of joy, tried to categorize the data.

Dominion. Sacrifice.

He knew the words. He knew what they meant in the abstract, in the philosophical lectures he’d skipped, in the sermons his mother had dragged him to when he was too young to argue and too tired to run. But seeing them quantified, reduced to a single digit each, felt like a glitch in the universe. A coding error in the afterlife.

He focused on the left column. Dominion. Level 1.

The memory surfaced, unbidden and cold. It wasn’t the mother. It wasn’t the infant. It was the moment before. The moment he had looked at the woman in the rubble and seen not a person, but an obstacle. A variable in the equation of his survival. He had calculated that if she were eliminated, the threat to the squad was removed, and his chances of living to see another paycheck increased. He had pulled his trigger, aimed at the bundle in her arms, confident in his own righteousness. He had used her weakness—her helplessness, her fear, her child—to secure his own safety.

That was Dominion. Using another’s vulnerability for personal gain.

And he had done it. He had always done it. He had climbed the ranks by stepping on others, by taking credit that wasn’t his, by letting colleagues take the fall for his mistakes because it was easier than admitting fault. He had been selfish, aggressive, ambitious. He had been good at it. And now, the universe was handing him a scorecard that said, *Yes, you did that. It counts.*

A bitter laugh echoed in the void, though there was no air to carry it. It was a dry, cracking sound, the laugh of a man who had just been told his life’s work was a minor infraction.

Then he looked at the right column. Sacrifice. Level 1.

This one felt wrong. Deeply, fundamentally wrong.

The memory of the shielding was there, yes. The visceral shock of the bullet tearing through his chest, the sudden, absolute silence of his own heartbeat. But the interface presented it with the same sterile detachment as the other side. *Sacrifice. 1.*

He had not intended to save her. That was the part that gnawed at him, the part that refused to let him settle into the comfort of the story he wanted to tell himself. He hadn’t lunged forward out of sudden, blossoming love. He hadn’t been a hero. He had been a Marine who had been ordered to kill a civilian and had, for a fraction of a second, hesitated. And in that hesitation, something else had taken over. A reflex? A guilt? A refusal to let Miller have the satisfaction?

It didn’t matter. The interface didn’t care about his motives. It cared about the result. He had given up his life. Or, more accurately, his body had been destroyed in the attempt to save another’s. And the System had stamped it with a level.

Caelen tried to look for a third column. A balance sheet. A final judgment. *Good. Evil. Holy. Damned.* But there was nothing. Just the two numbers, identical in value, staring back at him with the blank, indifferent gaze of a machine.

The dissonance was physical, even without a body. It felt like two opposing forces pulling at different parts of his soul, neither winning, both anchored.

He was a selfish man who had done a selfless act. And the universe had decided that both were true, and both were real, and both were worth exactly one point.

He wanted to scream. He wanted to demand an explanation. He wanted to ask why he was here, what this was, who was watching. But the void remained silent. The grid pulsed once, a slow, rhythmic thrum that felt less like a heartbeat and more like a clock ticking down to a moment that hadn’t happened yet.

There was no one to ask. There was no one to blame. There was only the data.

Caelen looked at the number **1** under Dominion. It was a small number. A starting point. He knew, with a cold certainty that felt like a gut instinct, that this was not the ceiling. He had seen the potential in the word itself. Dominion. Control. Power. He had spent his life chasing the shadow of it, and he was good at the chase. He could see, with a clarity that was almost painful, how easy it would be to climb. To use this. To turn this interface into a weapon, a tool for advancement, a way to ensure that the next time he was in a situation like that courtyard, he would be the one holding the rifle, and he would be the one deciding who lived and who died.

Then he looked at the **1** under Sacrifice.

It was smaller, in a way. Less promising. It required him to give something up. To lose. To be the one who bled. It was the path of the mother in the rubble, clutching her dead child, waiting for the world to end. It was a path that felt foreign, uncomfortable, and deeply, terrifyingly honest.

He didn’t know which one he wanted. He didn’t know which one he was.

The void did not judge. It did not comfort. It simply held the two numbers in the air, balanced and bright, waiting for him to choose. Or perhaps, waiting for him to fail.

Caelen let his mind drift from the interface. The grid faded back into the periphery, becoming a faint hum rather than a sight. The numbers remained, but they were quieter now, a background noise in the silence of his existence.

He was dead. He was here. And he had one point to show for it.

He waited.

The pulse did not arrive as a voice. It arrived as a pressure, a sudden tightening in the space where his chest had been, a phantom echo of the wound that had no body to hold it. Caelen tried to flinch, a reflex so deep in his bones that it predated thought, but there were no muscles to tense, no skin to bristle. The sensation remained, hovering in the silence like a note struck on a bell in an empty room.

It was not pain. Pain required a boundary, a self that could be hurt. This was something else. It was a recognition.

He waited for the sound to follow. In his last life, every signal had a source. A radio crackle meant a transmission. A boot on gravel meant a footstep. A heartbeat meant blood moving through veins. Here, the signal had no origin. It simply *was*. It expanded, filling the stillness with a hum that vibrated against the edges of his consciousness, and with it came the first flicker of structure.

The void, which had previously been a formless expanse of potential, began to resolve. It did not become a room, or a sky, or a landscape. It became a grid.

Caelen stared, or performed the mental equivalent of staring, as two lines of light drifted into focus before him. They were not written in any language he knew. They were not symbols he could read, yet their meaning pressed against his mind with the weight of iron. They were absolute.

One line was red, deep and saturated, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic intensity. Beside it, a counter ticked up. **Level 1.**

The other line was white, pale and steady, burning with a quiet, unwavering heat. Its counter also read **Level 1.**

Between them, a third element hovered, a faint, shimmering frame that contained both. It looked like a window, or perhaps a cage. It was private. It was his. And it was undeniable.

Caelen’s first instinct was to reject it. He was a Marine. He understood logistics, supply chains, and the tangible weight of ammunition. He did not understand glowing numbers floating in the void of his own soul. He tried to look away, to dismiss the display as a hallucination, a final trick of a dying brain. But the image did not waver. It did not fade. It simply existed, waiting for him to acknowledge it.

He looked closer.

The red line felt heavy. It carried the memory of every time he had taken more than he needed. It remembered the extra rations he had hoarded during the hike to the wire, the way he had let Private Davis carry the heavy gear while he rested his shoulders, the cold calculation of how best to position himself so that the credit for the successful breach would land squarely on his name. It was the architecture of his survival, built brick by brick from the weaknesses of others. It was efficient. It was effective. And it was labeled, clearly and without judgment, as **Dominion.**

The white line felt lighter, but no less real. It carried the memory of the courtyard. It held the image of the woman’s face, the terrible, silent grief of her eyes, and the weight of the dead infant in her arms. It remembered the moment he had dropped his rifle. It remembered the choice. It was not a reward for heroism, because he had not been a hero. It was a record of a transaction. He had given his life, and the void had accepted the payment. It was labeled **Sacrifice.**

One level in each.

Caelen let out a breath that did not exist. The dissonance was painful. He had spent eighteen years believing that his actions were driven by self-interest, by ambition, by the need to be the best, the strongest, the most competent. He had built his identity on the premise that he was a predator, a survivor, a man who took what he needed to stay alive.

And yet, here was the white line. Here was the proof that he had, in the end, chosen to give.

He tried to reconcile the two. He searched for the logic, the tactical advantage, the hidden benefit that would justify the sacrifice as another form of dominion. Had he saved her to save himself? No. He had been dying. Had he saved her to gain status? He was dead. There was no one to impress.

The numbers did not care about his confusion. They did not offer explanations. They did not provide a moral framework. They simply displayed the truth of his progression, cold and neutral, stripped of all narrative and justification.

Caelen stared at the interface, and for the first time in his life, he felt truly seen. It was a terrifying sensation. He had always been the observer, the one who analyzed, calculated, and controlled. Now, he was the observed. His every action, every choice, every moment of selfishness and every flash of altruism had been recorded, weighed, and categorized.

He was not a Marine. He was not a soldier. He was a data point.

The realization settled over him, cold and heavy. He had died to save a stranger, and the universe had responded by giving him a spreadsheet. It was absurd. It was ridiculous. And it was the most honest thing that had ever happened to him.

He looked at the red line again. **Dominion: Level 1.**

He looked at the white line. **Sacrifice: Level 1.**

He was balanced. He was divided. He was a man of two halves, standing on a precipice that had no bottom.

The pulse in the void faded, leaving only the quiet hum of the interface. The grid remained, steady and waiting. Caelen did not move. He did not try to turn it off. He did not try to understand it. He simply let it be, a new fact of his existence, as immutable and undeniable as death itself.

He was suspended in the silence, a soul with a score, waiting for the world to call him home.

The dust in the trench tasted like copper and old ash. Caelen wiped it from his lip with the back of his hand, the grit grinding against his teeth as he chewed. Around him, the rest of Fire Team was huddled behind the low concrete parapet, their breathing ragged and wet in the humid air. The sun was a white disc in a sky choked with smoke, offering no shade, only a relentless heat that turned their camo uniforms into sweat-soaked rags.

Caelen checked the magazine in his rifle for the third time in the last minute. Full. He didn’t need to check again; he had counted the rounds himself after the last reload. He looked up at the sky, then down at his boots, where a puddle of mud mixed with oil and something darker had collected around his toes. He was eighteen, and he had been fighting in this godforsaken hole for six weeks. In that time, he had learned more about the geometry of death than he had in two years of college, which he had dropped out of because the tuition was a joke and the degrees were worse. He had joined the Marines because he wanted to be someone. He wanted the uniform to mean something more than just a way to get out of his father’s house without owing him an apology. He wanted the respect that came from being the one who could handle the worst of it, the one who didn’t flinch when the radio crackled with coordinates for a kill.

"Two minutes to rotation," Sergeant Miller said, his voice flat and bored. He was sitting on an empty ammunition crate, cleaning his fingernails with a knife. He was fifty, and he looked like he had been made of stone and bad decisions. He didn’t look at Caelen. He never looked at Caelen unless Caelen had made a mistake or needed to be told to shut up.

Caelen didn’t answer. He was watching the street ahead. The buildings were crumbling skeletons of brick and rebar, their windows blown out, their doors hanging off their hinges. It was a typical urban battlefield, a place where the enemy lived in the shadows and the allies lived in the fear of what was coming out of them. Caelen had a clear line of sight to the intersection three streets down. He had a clear line of sight to the window on the second floor of the building directly across. He had a clear line of sight to the enemy sniper who had been picking off their rotation team since Tuesday.

He had a name for the sniper. He didn’t know who it was, but he knew he was there. He could see the glint of the scope in the window, a tiny point of light that moved with the sun. Caelen adjusted his scope, his thumb finding the familiar click of the elevation turret. He was good at this. He was good at the math of it, the physics of it. He was good at the way a bullet felt when it left the barrel, the way it arced through the air, the way it found its mark. He was good at the feeling of power that came from knowing he could kill a man a thousand yards away with a single shot, and that no one else on the team could do it as well.

It wasn’t just about being good. It was about being the best. It was about being the one who got the promotion, the one who got the medal, the one who got to go home with a story that made him sound like a hero instead of just another grunt. He had seen what happened to the guys who weren’t the best. They got stuck in the mud, they got stuck in the dark, they got stuck in the fear. Caelen didn’t want to be stuck. He wanted to move. He wanted to climb. And the only way to climb was to be the one who got the kill.

"Hey, Caelen," Miller said, still looking at his fingernails. "You gonna sit there all day, or you gonna do something?"

Caelen looked at him. "I’m watching the window, Sergeant."

"Good," Miller said. "Because if that sniper gets another one of us, I’m putting you on the next rotation. And I don’t care if you’re the best shooter in the unit. I don’t care if you can hit a coin at a mile. If you’re not moving, you’re dead. And I don’t want you dead. I want you useful."

Caelen didn’t respond. He knew what Miller meant. He knew that in the Marines, being useful was the only thing that mattered. Being good was just a byproduct. Being useful was the point. And right now, Caelen was useful. He was the one who could kill the sniper. He was the one who could keep the team alive. And that was enough.

He looked back at the window. The glint of the scope was still there. He could see the sniper’s face, a blur of shadow and skin. He didn’t know what the man looked like, but he could feel him. He could feel the tension in the air, the way the sniper was waiting, the way he was calculating, the way he was deciding who to kill next. Caelen felt a surge of adrenaline, the kind that came with the knowledge that he was about to do something important. He wasn’t just a Marine anymore. He was a weapon. He was the one who would end this.

He raised his rifle, his cheek pressing against the stock. The world narrowed down to the crosshairs. He could see the sniper’s hands, the way they were gripping the rifle, the way they were steady, the way they were ready. Caelen could see the fear in the sniper’s eyes, the way they were wide, the way they were darting around the room. He could see the desperation, the way the sniper was trying to figure out how to get out of this. And Caelen knew, with a certainty that felt like a physical weight in his chest, that he was going to win.

He wasn’t sure why he felt that way. He wasn’t sure why he felt so sure that he would kill the sniper. He wasn’t sure why he felt so sure that he would be the one to save the team. But he felt it, and that was enough. He was going to be the one. He was going to be the hero. And he was going to do it his way.

The sun moved across the sky, and the glint of the scope disappeared. The sniper had moved. Caelen lowered his rifle, his heart pounding in his ears. He had missed. He had missed the shot. And that was the problem. He had missed. And he knew, with a cold clarity that cut through the adrenaline, that he was going to have to do it again. And this time, he wouldn’t miss. He would be the one who killed the sniper. He would be the one who saved the team. And he would be the one who got the promotion.

He looked at his hands. They were steady. They were strong. They were the hands of a Marine. And they were going to do what they were supposed to do.

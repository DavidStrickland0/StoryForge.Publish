The radio crackled, a burst of static that sounded like sand being poured over gravel. I clicked the transmit button, my thumb hovering over the switch. “Eagle One, this is Eagle Two. Status?”

Silence.

I waited until the carrier wave died, then clicked again. “Eagle One, repeat, status. Do you copy?”

Nothing. Just the wind whistling through the shattered window frames of the building I had just exited. The air smelled of cordite and wet dust, a metallic tang that coated the back of my throat. I adjusted my grip on the M4, the polymer stock cold against my cheek. Seven rounds. A number that felt precarious, a math problem I hadn’t wanted to solve.

“Diaz,” I said, turning back toward the doorway.

He was still there, pressed against the collapsed beam, his face a mask of pale shock. His breathing was ragged, a wet, hitching sound that grated against my nerves. I had told him to breathe. I had told him to control it. He hadn’t listened.

“Move,” I ordered.

He flinched, his eyes wide and darting. “Extraction’s—”

“Extraction is late. We are moving to the rally point. Now.”

He scrambled to his feet, his legs shaking visibly. He was young, younger than me by a year, and he still had the look of a boy who had just learned that the world did not care about his potential. I didn’t have time to fix that. I had a job to do, and I had a body count to maintain.

We stepped out into the street. It was a narrow, choked artery of rubble, the asphalt buckled and split by the earlier mortar impacts. The sun was high and bleaching, turning the dust into a fine, white fog that hung in the air. It was beautiful, in a sterile, dead way. It was the kind of beauty that didn’t ask anything of you.

I moved first. Always first. I scanned the left, then the right, my eyes tracking the shadows between the piles of concrete. Diaz followed, his boots crunching on the debris. He was loud. He was always loud. I wanted to tell him to be quiet, but I knew he was trying. That was the problem with Diaz; he tried so hard it made him clumsy.

We reached the corner of the intersection. A bus lay on its side, its windows shattered, the interior a tangle of twisted metal and torn fabric. I raised my rifle, checking the corner. The corner was clear. I signaled for him to follow.

As we rounded the bend, the world seemed to tilt.

A scream cut through the air. It was high, thin, and desperate. It came from behind us, from the direction of the building we had just left.

I froze. My body locked, every muscle tensing. I turned slowly, my rifle sweeping back toward the source of the sound.

Diaz was gone.

The space where he had been standing was empty. The dust swirled in the spot where his shadow had been a second ago. There was no blood, no body, no sign of him. Just a faint, fading trail of disturbed dust in the air.

I stared at the empty space. My mind tried to process the data. Diaz was gone. Diaz was not there. The equation was broken.

“Diaz!” I shouted, the word tearing out of my throat before I could stop it.

The room fell into silence.

I ran back toward the building. My boots slammed against the asphalt, the impact jarring my knees. I reached the doorway, peering inside. The hallway was dark, the light from the street cutting a sharp, angular slice across the floor.

There was nothing.

I checked the rooms. The kitchen, the bathroom, the bedroom. The rooms were empty. I checked the windows. The windows were broken. I checked the roof. I climbed up, my heart hammering against my ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage.

He was gone.

I stood on the roof, looking out over the city. The smoke rose in thick, gray columns. The distant sound of gunfire was a constant, rhythmic pulse. I was alone.

I looked down at my hands. They were steady. That was the worst part. They should have been shaking. They should have been trembling. But they were steady, gripping the rifle with a precision that felt unnatural, mechanical.

I was the Marine. I was the one who made the decisions. I was the one who kept the others alive.

But the others weren’t there.

I climbed back down, my movements efficient, controlled. I returned to the street, my eyes scanning the horizon. The rally point was two blocks away. I needed to get there. I needed to report. I needed to find a way to make sense of this.

I walked. I didn’t run. Running would have been a mistake, a display of weakness. I walked with a steady, purposeful stride, my rifle held at the ready. The dust settled on my shoulders, my face, my hair. I felt the weight of the world settling onto me, a heavy, oppressive burden that I had no choice but to carry.

I was alone.

And I was ready.

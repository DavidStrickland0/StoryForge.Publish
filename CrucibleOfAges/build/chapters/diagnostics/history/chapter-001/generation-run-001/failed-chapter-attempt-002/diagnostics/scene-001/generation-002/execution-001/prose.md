The dust in the room tasted like copper and old sweat. I wiped my forearm across my mouth, trying to clear the grit from my teeth, and checked the magazine in my M4. Seven rounds left. Not enough for a fight, but enough for a conversation.

"Contact left," I said. My voice was steady. It was always steady. That was the first thing they taught us at Parris Island, and it was the last thing they took away.

Private Diaz flinched. He was nineteen, green in a way that had nothing to do with his skin and everything to do with his eyes. They were wide, darting around the ruined hallway, looking for a door, a window, a mother, anything that would tell him where to go. I didn’t have that luxury. I had a sector, a weapon, and a job to do.

"Cover me," I ordered.

"I can't see—"

"I said cover me."

I moved. The hallway was a mess of shattered drywall and twisted rebar, the remnants of a building that had once been a family home and was now a corridor of death. The enemy was somewhere ahead, pinned down by the mortar fire from the support team, but they weren’t dead. Not yet.

I saw the flash of muzzle fire from the corner. Three shots. Two missed. The third hit the wall above my head, showering me in plaster. I dropped, rolled behind a collapsed beam, and returned fire without looking. I heard the body hit the floor. I didn’t need to see it. I knew the sound.

"Another one," I called out. "Stay down, Diaz."

"I'm trying, sir."

"You're breathing too loud."

He stopped breathing. Or at least, he tried to. I could hear the rapid intake of air in his throat, the wet click of his tongue against his teeth. He was scared. Scared people made mistakes. Mistakes got people killed.

I stood up. The hallway was dark, lit only by the intermittent flare of explosions in the distance and the faint, orange glow of burning debris. I moved forward, step by step, my boots crunching on broken glass. I was eighteen. I was a Marine. I was the best thing in that building, and I knew it.

There was another figure in the shadows. A insurgent, maybe, or a local who had gotten in the way. He was holding a rifle, his hands shaking. He looked at me, and I saw the fear in his eyes. It was the same fear I saw in Diaz. The same fear I had seen in every person who had ever stood in my line of fire.

I raised my weapon.

"Drop it," I said.

He didn’t drop it. He raised it.

I didn’t hesitate. I didn’t think. I didn’t feel a surge of righteousness or a pang of sorrow. I simply pulled the trigger.

The rifle shot rang out. The man fell.

I walked over to his body. He was dead. I checked his hands to make sure he wasn’t holding a grenade. He wasn’t. I looked at his face. He was young. Younger than me, maybe.

"Threat eliminated," I said into my radio. "Sector clear."

"Copy, Six-Bravo. Stand by for extraction."

I exhaled. My heart rate was elevated, but I was in control. That was the thing about control. It was a feeling. It was a warm, solid thing that lived in your chest, and it was the only thing that mattered when the world was falling apart.

I turned back to Diaz. He was still huddled behind the beam, his face pale.

"Nice shot," he said. His voice was quiet, almost a whisper.

"Yeah," I said. "Next time, don't let them get a bead on you."

I didn’t wait for his reply. I moved toward the exit, where the air was cleaner and the noise was louder. The sounds of the battle were deafening. Mortars thumping in the distance, the whine of bullets passing through the air, the shouts of men who were no longer in control of their own lives.

I was in control.

That was the lie I told myself. That was the story I had built for myself, brick by brick, bullet by bullet. I was the Marine. I was the one who made the decisions. I was the one who kept the others alive.

But as I stepped out into the street, I saw the bodies. There were three of them, lying in the dirt. Two were enemy combatants. The third was a woman. She was wearing a dress, torn and bloody. Her eyes were open, but they were empty.

I looked at her for a second. Then I looked away.

There was no time for that. There was never time for that.

I kept moving.

The dust tasted like copper and old concrete. I spat it out into my mask, the grit sticking to the filter paper, and checked my magazine. Fourteen rounds. Enough to keep a conversation going with the locals, provided they were polite enough to wait their turn.

The street was a graveyard of broken glass and twisted rebar. Somewhere to the north, the distant thud of mortar fire continued its lazy, rhythmic count, a metronome ticking away in the background while we dealt with the immediate, messy problem in front of us. It was a good kind of noise. It meant the big guns weren’t looking at us. It meant we could do this the quick, ugly way without having to worry about a tank round turning our squad into a modern art installation.

"Stay on your marks," I said into the radio. My voice came out flat, calm, the way it was supposed to. "We’re pushing the corner. Diaz, you’re up. Get that door open and clear the room. I want eyes on the hallway before I move."

I watched Diaz through the scope. He was young. Nineteen, maybe twenty. He had that look in his eyes that I used to have before I learned that hesitation was a luxury I couldn’t afford. He was shaking. Not from cold, and not from fear, at least not the kind that made your hands useless. It was the adrenaline, the high-pitched whine of survival instinct screaming at him to run, to hide, to let someone else do the dirty work.

I didn’t have time to be his mentor. I didn’t have time to be his friend. I had a mission, a timeline, and a body bag waiting for whoever was behind that door.

"Diaz," I repeated, my tone sharpening just enough to cut through the static. "You heard me. Get in there. If you hesitate, you’re dead. If you’re dead, I’m dragging your body out over the enemy’s head, and that’s a lot more work than I’m willing to do right now."

It was a cruel thing to say. I knew it was cruel. But it was the truth, or at least the version of the truth that kept people moving. People needed a reason to be afraid that was worse than the thing they were about to face. I gave them that.

Diaz took a breath, his chest heaving under the weight of his gear. He moved. He was good. Quick, disciplined, the kind of soldier who memorized the manual because he knew that rules were the only thing standing between him and chaos. He kicked the door inward, the wood splintering with a sound that was almost satisfying in its finality.

We moved in.

The room was dark, smelling of stale sweat and rusted pipes. My flashlight cut through the gloom, a beam of white light that exposed the dust motes dancing in the air. I swept the corners, left to right, covering the space with the cold precision of a machine. There was no one there. Just a broken bed frame and a pile of clothes that looked like they hadn’t been touched in months.

"Clear," Diaz said. His voice was steady now, the shaking gone. He was doing the job. He was doing it well.

"Good," I said. "Hold position. Watch the window. I’m moving to the next room."

I stepped back into the hallway, my boots crunching on the debris. I liked the feeling of the rifle in my hands. It was an extension of my will, a way to impose order on a world that desperately wanted to be chaotic. I thought about the mission briefing. The intel was thin, the stakes were high, and the pay was enough to keep my head above water for another six months. It was a good job. A dangerous job, but a good one.

I reached the next door. It was slightly ajar, a gap of darkness inviting me in. I pushed it open with the barrel of my rifle, the metal sliding against the wood with a soft scrape.

The room was larger than the last one, a living space that had been abandoned in a hurry. A table, a chair, a television that was dead and silent. And in the corner, sitting on the floor with her back to the wall, was a woman.

She was young. Maybe her mid-twenties. She was wearing a dress that had been torn at the hem, and her hair was matted with dust. She was holding a small child in her arms, a boy who was no older than three or four. He was asleep, his face pale and thin, his eyes closed.

The woman’s eyes were open. They were wide, terrified, and fixed on me.

I raised my rifle.

The instinct was immediate. A threat. A civilian in a combat zone. The protocol was clear: assess, neutralize, secure. She was in the way. She was a variable I hadn’t accounted for. She was a risk.

I looked at Diaz, who was moving up behind me. He saw her too. He lowered his weapon slightly, his eyes flickering between me and the woman. He was reading the situation, trying to decide if this was a trap or a mistake.

I didn’t wait for him to decide.

"Move her," I said, my voice flat. "Get her out of here. Now."

Diaz hesitated. "Sir, she’s a non-combatant. The rules—"

"The rules say we secure the area," I interrupted. "She’s in the area. That makes her a priority. Get her out. If she’s hostile, put her down. If she’s not, get her out of my sight before I have to think about her."

Diaz looked at me, then at the woman. She was trembling, the child stirring in her arms. She didn’t speak. She didn’t beg. She just looked at me with that same wide, terrified stare.

I felt a flicker of something in my gut. A discomfort. A small, annoying itch that I usually ignored. It was the part of me that knew I was being a bastard. But I was also a Marine, and I was doing my job. The discomfort was a luxury. I didn’t have time for it.

"Move," I said again, louder this time.

Diaz nodded. He approached the woman slowly, his hands visible, his posture open. He spoke to her in a language I couldn’t understand, a series of soft, rapid consonants. She flinched, pulling the child tighter against her chest.

I watched them. I watched Diaz. I watched the child. And I watched the clock in my mind, the one that was ticking down toward the next phase of the operation.

I realized then that I was waiting. I was waiting for them to be gone so I could finish the job. I was waiting for the world to clear itself out so I could continue with my life.

And in that moment, standing in that dusty, broken room, I felt a strange, cold clarity settle over me. I knew exactly who I was. I knew what I wanted. And I knew that I would do whatever was necessary to get it.

I lowered my rifle slightly, my eyes never leaving the woman. I was ready to move. I was ready to continue.

And then, I saw the shadow move in the window.

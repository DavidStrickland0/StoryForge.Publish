The shadow in the window was a mistake. My first thought, sharp and immediate, was that it was nothing. A piece of debris. A trick of the light filtering through the broken glass. I held my breath, my finger resting lightly on the trigger guard, watching the frame of the window like a hawk watching a field mouse. The figure did not reappear. It didn’t vanish into the darkness of the street, either. It just stopped moving, or perhaps it had never moved at all.

I let out a slow exhale through my nose, the mask damp and warm against my face. The silence in the room was heavy, pressing against my eardrums, broken only by the rhythmic, distant thud of mortar fire that had been a constant companion for the last hour. It was a steady, metronomic beat, a reminder that the world outside this shell of concrete was still violent and indifferent.

I keyed my radio. "Diaz. Status."

Static hissed in my ear, a thin, electronic whine that cut through the quiet. I tapped the side of the mic, a small, futile gesture. "Diaz. Come in. Status."

The radio remained silent. No crackle. No voice. Just that endless, white noise.

My jaw tightened. In the last room we cleared, Diaz had been right behind me. I could still see the back of his helmet, the way his rifle was held with a tense grip that spoke more of nervousness than confidence. He had followed my orders without question, his eyes downcast, his movements precise and efficient. He was good at his job. He was useful. And now he was gone, or he was broken, or he was dead. The possibilities were numerous, and none of them were helpful.

I checked my magazine. Fourteen rounds. I had counted them at the start of the room, and I had not fired a single shot. My hands were steady. My training was holding. But the connection to my unit, the invisible web of voices and orders that had supported me since I put on the uniform, was severed. I was alone.

I moved toward the door, my rifle sweeping the hallway. The dust swirled in the air, catching the pale light from the broken windows. I could see the hallway stretching out, empty and dark. There was no one there. No Diaz. No backup. No command voice in my ear telling me where to go or what to do.

I paused, listening. The mortar fire continued its steady rhythm. Thud. Thud. Thud. It was a lull, a pause in the storm, but it was deceptive. It gave me a false sense of security, a window of opportunity to think, to plan, to survive. But it was also a trap. A lull is just the breath before the scream.

I checked my weapon again. The bolt was locked back. I raked it forward, the sound sharp and metallic in the quiet hallway. I checked the chamber. A round was seated. I racked the charging handle, clearing the chamber, then racked it again, loading a fresh round. My hands moved automatically, muscle memory doing the work that my mind couldn't quite keep up with.

I was a Marine. I was trained for this. I was trained to be efficient, to be deadly, to be a tool that worked as intended. But being a tool required someone to hold the handle. And right now, no one was holding it.

I stepped out of the room, my boots crunching on the debris. The hallway was narrow, the walls scarred with bullet holes and shrapnel. I moved quickly, my eyes scanning the shadows, my mind calculating the angles, the exits, the threats. I was looking for a way out, a way back to my unit, a way to re-establish the order that I had relied on for so long.

But the hallway was empty. The door at the end of the hall was open, leading to a street that was bathed in the grey light of a sky choked with smoke. I could hear the distant screams of people, the rattle of automatic weapons, the crash of glass. The battle was still raging, but it had moved on. It had moved on without me.

I stood at the edge of the doorway, looking out at the chaos. The world was a mess. It was a place of violence and destruction, of loss and grief. And I was standing in the middle of it, alone, with my rifle and my mask and my fourteen rounds of ammunition.

I took a deep breath, the air tasting of dust and ash. I was afraid. The fear was a cold, hard thing, sitting in the pit of my stomach. But I pushed it down. I shoved it into the dark corner of my mind where it belonged. I was a Marine. I didn't show fear. I didn't let it control me.

I stepped out into the street, my rifle raised, my eyes scanning the buildings on either side. I was looking for a target, a threat, anything to focus on. But there was nothing. Just the ruins of the city, the smoke, the silence.

And in that silence, I heard my own heartbeat. It was loud, pounding in my ears. It was the only sound that mattered. It was the sound of my life, my reality, my isolation.

I was alone. And I was scared.

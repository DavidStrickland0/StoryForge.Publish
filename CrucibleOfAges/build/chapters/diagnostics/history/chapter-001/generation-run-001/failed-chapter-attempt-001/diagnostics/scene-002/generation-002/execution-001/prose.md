The order came through the radio with a static crunch that made my teeth ache. "All elements, advance on signal. Contact the ridge. Hold no ground. Push through."

I killed the radio. The silence was louder than the noise, a sudden vacuum that sucked the air out of the trench. Miller looked up from his rifle, his eyes wide and wet, waiting for me to translate the bureaucracy of war into something human. I didn’t. I just checked the magazine, felt the brass cold against my palm, and waited for the whistle.

The whistle blew. It was a thin, high-pitched scream that cut through the artillery rattle like a knife through wet paper.

I moved first. Always move first. It’s the only rule that matters when the ground tries to swallow you. I climbed the ladder, my boots finding the rungs by muscle memory, and hauled myself over the lip of the trench. The air outside was thick with dust and the smell of cordite, a dry, metallic taste that coated the back of my throat.

Miller was right behind me. I could see the top of his helmet, the green paint peeling off in flakes. He was shaking. I could see it in the way his rifle stock jittered against his shoulder. I wanted to tell him to stop it. I wanted to tell him that trembling made a bad target, that fear was a luxury for people with backup, and that I was the backup. I wanted to tell him that if he died, I’d have to find a new pair of hands to hold the line, and I didn’t have the time or the patience for a replacement.

Instead, I just walked.

The ground was a mess. Trenches cut through the earth in a jagged, chaotic web, some deep, some shallow, all filled with mud and debris. We were supposed to be moving in a coordinated fire-and-maneuver pattern. My left, Miller’s right. My cover, his advance. It was a simple equation. Two variables. One solution.

The equation broke.

A burst of automatic fire snapped from the left, tearing into the earth where we had been standing just seconds before. Dirt sprayed up in a geyser, coating my face in gritty, chalky dust. I dropped to one knee, my rifle up, scanning the ridge. Nothing. Just the grey sky and the distant, hazy outlines of the enemy position.

"Contact left!" I yelled. My voice sounded thin, swallowed by the wind.

Miller didn’t move. He was frozen, his rifle pointed at the sky, his hands still trembling so badly the stock blurred. He wasn’t looking at the enemy. He was looking at the dirt. At the holes. At the reality of what was trying to kill him.

"Miller!" I roared, grabbing his shoulder. "Look at me! Where is it?"

He blinked, his eyes unfocusing, drifting back to the present. "I... I don't know."

That wasn't an answer. That was a failure. I shoved him back, hard. He stumbled, his boots slipping in the mud. I turned my gaze back to the left. The fire had stopped, but the silence was worse. It meant they were reloading. It meant they were adjusting their aim. It meant I was about to be hit by something bigger than a rifle round.

I checked my watch. We were supposed to be at the next objective in three minutes. We had been moving for two. I was the leader. I was the one who made the calls. I was the one who kept the squad alive. Or so I told myself.

The truth was simpler and uglier. I was the one who didn’t want to die. Miller was the one who was going to die first because he was slow, and his death would buy me time to find a way out. It was a cold calculation, but it was the only one I could make. I had moved the lieutenant to the front line yesterday because he was nervous. I had taken his high ground because I wanted it. I had used Miller’s fear to keep my own perspective sharp, to keep me from feeling the cold creep of my own mortality.

Now, I was alone. Or as close to it as I could get with a shaking idiot for a wingman.

I stood up. The wind hit me, cold and dry. I looked at Miller. He was staring at me, his face a mask of confusion and terror. He didn't know what to do. He was waiting for me to tell him.

I didn't. I turned away.

I moved left, into the open, exposing my flank to the ridge. It was a mistake. I knew it was a mistake. But I needed to see. I needed to know where the fire was coming from. I needed to control the situation.

I moved fast, low, sliding along the ground, my rifle scanning the trees, the rocks, the shadows. Nothing. Just the dust and the debris. I was the only one moving. The rest of the squad was back in the trench, or dead, or frozen like Miller.

I was alone.

The realization hit me harder than the bullet that should have hit me. I was alone. My rank, my training, my equipment, my squad—they were all just props. They were all just ways to keep me from feeling the void. And the void was opening up around me.

I stopped. I stood still. I listened.

The wind. The distant artillery. The sound of my own breathing.

I was here. I was alive. I was alone.

And I was afraid.

I didn't like it. I didn't want to admit it. But it was there, a cold knot in my stomach, a tightness in my chest. I was afraid of being alone. I was afraid of making a mistake. I was afraid of dying.

I looked at Miller. He was still there, still frozen, still waiting.

I walked back to him. I didn't say anything. I just looked at him. He looked back at me, his eyes wide, his hands still trembling.

I put a hand on his shoulder. I didn't squeeze it. I just rested it there. A reminder. A connection.

We were both here. We were both afraid.

And we were both alone.

I picked up my rifle. I checked the magazine. I checked the safety. I was ready. Or as ready as I could be.

I looked at the ridge. I looked at the sky. I looked at the dust.

I was here. I was alive. I was alone.

And I was ready to move.

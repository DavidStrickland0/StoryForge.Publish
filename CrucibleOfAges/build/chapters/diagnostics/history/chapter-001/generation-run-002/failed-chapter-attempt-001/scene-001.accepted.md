I was going to be a captain before I was twenty-five. It was a reasonable goal, considering I had spent the last two years treating my squad like a collection of expensive, fragile tools that needed constant maintenance and frequent replacement. The mud in the trench was cold, slapping against my boots with every small adjustment I made to my footing. I didn’t mind the cold. Cold was honest. It didn’t have opinions.

"Corporal," Private Vance whispered, his voice cracking under the weight of his own terror. He was huddled behind me, his face pressed into the back of my flak jacket. I could feel the heat of his breath through the Kevlar, a wet, frantic vibration that reminded me of a dog waiting for a bone it wasn’t sure it would get. "I don't think we can hold this sector. The intel was wrong. They’re everywhere."

I adjusted my rifle, checking the chamber for the third time in ten minutes. "The intel is what it is, Vance. You’re a Marine. You hold your position, or you don’t. There’s no third option where you get to negotiate with the physics of a bullet."

He flinched. I liked that. Fear was a useful commodity. It made people listen. It made them follow. And right now, following was the only thing keeping my left flank from collapsing into a crater of shrapnel and regret. I had been the one who suggested we use the drainage culvert as a natural choke point. It was a good tactical choice. It forced the enemy into a narrow lane where their numbers mattered less and their individual skill mattered more. It was also the reason Vance was currently using my spine as a pillow.

I glanced over my shoulder. The dirt wall was only six inches away, but I didn’t look at it. I looked at the sky, which was a bruised purple, swollen with the smoke of our own artillery. The sun was hidden, turning the world into a gray, muddy tableau where the only things that moved were the dead and the desperate.

"Hey, Sarge," another voice came from the left. It was Miller, a kid who looked like he hadn’t slept since the invasion began. His eyes were wide, bloodshot, and he was chewing on a piece of cactus stem he’d found in the supply drop. "You think they’re going to push here?"

"Everything gets pushed eventually, Miller," I said, keeping my voice flat, bored. I wanted them to think I was indifferent. Indifference was armor. If I seemed scared, they would start looking for exits, and I didn’t have the manpower to cover every exit. "But right now, we’re the wall. Walls don’t get pushed. They get rebuilt. Usually with more cement."

Miller smiled, a thin, trembling thing. "Cement. Right. We’ve got plenty of that in our rations."

"No, we don't," I said. "We’ve got protein bars and dust. But the point is the same. You hold."

I turned back to the front. The enemy was about two hundred meters out, moving in a loose formation that looked surprisingly organized. That was the problem with this engagement. It wasn’t the chaotic, suicidal charge the generals had promised in their briefings. It was a methodical advance, a grinding pressure that suggested someone on the other side was thinking, planning, and calculating just as hard as I was. And I was tired. I was so tired that my bones felt like they were made of wet sand.

I thought about my apartment back in San Diego. The one I’d rented with the bonus I’d made in the last tour. It had a view of the harbor. The water was calm, almost black, and the city lights reflected off the surface like spilled glitter. I used to go out there in the evenings, just to stand on the balcony and watch the boats come in. I liked the predictability of it. Boats came in. Boats went out. The world made sense when you could see the edges of it.

Here, there were no edges. Just a horizon that went on forever, and a horizon that was full of things that wanted to kill me.

"Vance," I said, without turning around. "How’s your magazine count?"

"Two," he said.

"Good. Save them. If you see something you can’t handle, you shoot. If you see something you can handle, you wait. Do you understand the difference?"

"I think so."

"You don’t think so. You’re guessing. And guessing gets you killed. I’ll handle the handling. You handle the waiting."

I felt a strange surge of satisfaction at that. I was managing them. I was directing the flow of their fear into something useful, something that kept me alive. It was a simple equation. I was the brain, they were the muscle. And brains don’t bleed. Brains just think.

The first shell landed close. The sound wasn’t a bang; it was a tear, a ripping of the air that seemed to come from inside my own skull. The ground jumped, and I felt the vibration travel up through my legs and into my chest. Dirt rained down on my helmet, gritty and cold.

"Contact!" someone shouted. I didn’t know who. It didn’t matter. The shouting was a good sign. It meant we were still awake.

I raised my rifle, scanning the tree line. The enemy was still there, still moving. They weren’t taking cover. They were advancing.

"Miller," I said, my voice cutting through the noise. "Stop chewing. Start watching."

I saw a flash in the distance, a muzzle report that looked like a star being born and dying in the same instant. A bullet whizzed past my ear, close enough that I felt the displacement of the air against my cheek. It was a warning. Or maybe just a coincidence. In this war, the difference was academic.

I checked my own magazine. My magazine was full. I had the luxury of full magazines because I had the luxury of being the one in charge. I could trade my comfort for their safety, and I did it without hesitation. It was the deal. I took the risk, they took the orders. It was a fair trade, even if it wasn’t a fair price.

I watched the enemy line. They were getting closer. One hundred meters. The distance closed to ninety meters. The distance closed to eighty meters.

I thought about the woman I’d met in the mess hall last week. She was a logistics officer, a civilian contractor who had been assigned to our unit to manage the supply chain. She was smart, efficient, and completely out of her depth. She had tried to explain to me that our ammunition consumption was inefficient, that we were wasting rounds on targets that weren’t high value. I had listened to her, nodding along, while mentally calculating how many of her supplies I could divert to my personal stash.

She had been so certain of her data. So sure that the numbers would protect her. I had told her that numbers were just tools, and tools could break. She had looked at me with this mixture of pity and fear, as if I were a wild animal she hadn’t quite tamed.

I liked that she was afraid. It made her useful.

The enemy reached the fifty-meter mark. I could see them now, individual figures, faces obscured by dust and masks. They were moving with a grim determination that made my stomach churn. They weren’t retreating. They weren’t hesitating. They were coming for us.

"Vance," I said, my voice low. "Get ready."

"I’m ready," he said, but his voice was shaking.

"Good. Now wait."

I tightened my grip on the rifle. The wood was smooth under my hands, familiar and solid. It was the only thing in this world that felt real. Everything else was mud, smoke, and the distant, indifferent roar of the artillery.

I was a Marine. I was a leader. I was the best there was. And I was about to die.

The thought was absurd. I was eighteen years old. I had a future. I had a life. I was the one who was supposed to be the one who survived, the one who came home, the one who wrote the book about how I did it.

But the enemy was closing in, and the sky was falling, and the only thing I could do was wait.

I waited.

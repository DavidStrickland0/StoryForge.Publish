The mud was cold. That was the first thing my brain latched onto. It wasn't the fear, or the adrenaline, or the bizarre, floating sensation of knowing I was about to die. It was the cold. It soaked through my boots, seeping into the fabric of my trousers, and it felt like every drop of water in the universe had decided to become my personal enemy. I stood there, ankle-deep in the slurry, my knees aching from the impact of the earlier shelling. My rifle hung heavy on my back, the strap cutting into my shoulders, a dead weight that I had no intention of using.

I tried to breathe. That was the second thing. Breathing used to be automatic, a background process that required zero conscious effort. Now, it felt like a chore. I had to remember to pull the air in, to push it out. In, out. The air tasted of sulfur and wet earth and the metallic tang of blood that wasn't mine. I could hear the enemy moving. They were close. Five meters, maybe four. I could see the edges of their uniforms, the way the dust clung to their shoulders. They were young. I could tell by the way they moved, the slight tremor in their arms. They weren't monsters. They were just boys, like me, wearing different colors and following different orders.

And then there was the woman. She was standing behind them, just beyond the reach of their formation, watching me with an expression I couldn't read. It wasn't fear. It wasn't hope. It was curiosity. She looked at me the way one might look at a dog that had wandered into the wrong yard, wondering what it was going to do next. Her hair was pinned up, her clothes clean. She belonged to a world where people didn't stand in the mud and wait to be killed. I envied her. I envied her clarity. I envied the fact that she didn't have to make a choice. She just had to survive.

I looked down at my hands. They were shaking. Not the violent, jagged shakes of panic, but a low, constant vibration, like a phone on a table. I flexed my fingers, trying to steady them, but they kept trembling. I could feel the grit of the mud under my fingernails, the roughness of my gloves. I was eighteen years old. I was a Marine. I was supposed to be the one who made the hard choices, the one who held the line. But standing here, in the open, with no cover and no plan, I felt incredibly small.

The enemy stopped. They were right in front of me now. I could smell them. Sweat, and gun oil, and the faint, sweet scent of the rain that had just stopped falling. One of them, a boy with a scar running through his eyebrow, raised his weapon. I didn't flinch. I didn't brace myself. I just stood there, letting the mud settle around my boots, letting the weight of the world settle onto my shoulders.

I thought about Miller. I thought about Vance. I thought about the way they had laughed in the mess hall, the way they had argued about music, the way they had looked at me with that mix of respect and suspicion that I had come to rely on. They were gone. They were dust. And I was still here, standing in the mud, waiting for the bullet that would end it all.

It was absurd. That was the third thing. The sheer, unadulterated absurdity of it all. I had joined the Marines to escape my father's shadow, to find a place where my worth was measured in something other than money or status. And now, here I was, measuring my worth in the number of strangers I could shield with my body. It was a trade I hadn't made a deal for. It was a price I hadn't agreed to pay. But here it was, sitting in the mud, cold and wet and waiting.

The boy with the scar took a step forward. His boot squelched in the slurry. He raised his rifle, aiming at my chest. I could see the glint of the barrel in the dusty light. I didn't close my eyes. I didn't pray. I just watched. I watched the way his finger hovered over the trigger, the way his breath fogged in the cool air. I watched the woman behind him, her face still calm, her eyes still curious.

I realized, with a strange, detached clarity, that I wasn't afraid. I wasn't even excited. I was just... I was just tired. Tired of running, tired of hiding, tired of pretending that I was someone I wasn't. This was the end of that. This was the moment where the mask slipped off and fell into the mud. And for the first time in my life, I didn't care who saw what was underneath.

The boy's finger tightened. The air seemed to hold its breath. The world narrowed down to a single point, a single moment, a single choice. I stood in the mud, cold and wet and alive, and I waited for the darkness to come.

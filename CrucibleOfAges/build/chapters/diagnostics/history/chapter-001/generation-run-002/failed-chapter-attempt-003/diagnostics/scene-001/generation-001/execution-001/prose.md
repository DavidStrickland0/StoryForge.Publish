The sandbags were damp. That was the first thing I noticed, a cold, gritty slickness against my cheek that smelled of wet clay and old sweat. The second thing I noticed was that the world had turned into a kaleidoscope of dust and screaming, and I was in the middle of it.

"Move! Move, you lazy sons of bitches!" Sergeant Miller’s voice cracked over the din, distorted by the static and the sheer volume of the artillery barrage shelling the perimeter. "Get off the net! Get to the fallback line!"

I didn’t move. I shifted my weight, finding a more stable footing on the packed earth behind the hastily stacked barriers. My rifle was already raised, the stock pressed firmly against my shoulder, the iron sights aligned with the treeline where the enemy was massing. I liked the weight of the weapon. It grounded me. It was a simple, clean power.

Around me, the squad was unraveling. Private Vance was on his knees, vomiting into the dirt, his helmet hanging off the side of his head. He looked like a child, pale and shaking, his eyes wide with a terror that I recognized but refused to share. Beside him, Corporals Diaz and O’Malley were arguing, their voices rising in a frantic, disjointed chorus of orders that meant nothing.

"Left flank is gone!" O’Malley shouted, pointing a finger that trembled violently. "They’ve got us flanked, Sarge, we’re pinned!"

"Shut up and shoot!" Miller roared back, his face a mask of grim determination that looked, to me, like a bad mask in a movie. He was trying to hold the line, trying to be the rock, the leader, the reason we were here. But the rock was cracking. I could see it in the way he gripped his rifle, the way his knuckles were white, the way he kept glancing back at the treeline as if expecting it to swallow him whole.

I watched them with a detached, clinical interest. This was the moment I had always expected, the moment where the training kicked in and the system worked. But the system was leaking. The institutional support, the invisible web of rank and protocol that held us together, was fraying under the weight of the chaos. And I was just a node in the network, a component waiting to be replaced.

A mortar round landed close. The ground jumped, sending a spray of dirt and pebbles over the sandbags. I didn’t flinch. I adjusted my aim, tracking the movement of the shadows among the trees. My finger hovered over the trigger, relaxed but ready.

"Miller!" I called out, my voice calm, cutting through the noise. "We need to fall back. Now."

Miller turned to me, his eyes wide. "We hold the position, Marine. We have orders."

"Orders are for people who want to die for a piece of paper," I said, keeping my gaze on the trees. "I’m not doing that. I’m doing what I’m good at. Surviving."

Vance looked at me, a flash of hope in his eyes. "Right, yeah, let’s go. I’m with you."

"No," I said, and the word came out sharper than I intended. I didn’t look at him. I didn’t need to. I could feel the heat of his fear, the desperate need to be part of something, to be saved by the group. But the group was failing. And I wasn’t a savior. I was a survivor.

"Stay down," I told him. "Don’t move unless you’re told to. And when you’re told to move, run."

He blinked, confused by the directness. "But… the Sarge…"

"I’ll deal with the Sarge," I said, and turned back to my target.

I fired three rounds, rapid and precise. The first hit a branch, showering leaves and dust. The second struck the earth, kicking up a cloud of dirt. The third found its mark, a shadow that flinched and disappeared into the undergrowth. I didn’t know who it was, or what they were. It didn’t matter. It was a problem solved.

That was the thing about being a Marine. It wasn’t about the mission. It wasn’t about the flag, or the country, or the noble cause. It was about the problem. And I was very, very good at solving problems.

The battle raged on around me, a symphony of gunfire and explosions that I tuned out like background music. I was isolated in my own little bubble of competence, a bubble made of steel and discipline and the cold, hard logic of survival.

Miller was still shouting, still trying to hold the line. Vance was still on his knees, still looking to me for guidance. Diaz and O’Malley were still arguing, still trying to find a solution in the chaos.

And I was just watching. Waiting.

Because I knew something they didn’t. The battle was already lost. The only question was how long it would take them to realize it.

I checked my magazine. Full. I checked my watch. 14:02. I checked the sky. Clear, blue, indifferent.

I was ready.

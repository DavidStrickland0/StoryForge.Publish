The sand was hot enough to blister skin if you sat on it too long, but right now, I was crouched behind a stack of sandbags that felt like they were made of brick, so I didn’t much care. My knees were buried in the dirt, my rifle resting against my shoulder, and the muzzle brake was pointed at a patch of open ground that looked suspiciously empty.

“Keep your head down, Davis,” I muttered, though Davis was three meters to my left, and his head was already down, pressed flat against the sandbag with the intensity of a man trying to fuse himself with the earth.

Davis was a good soldier. He was also a twenty-two-year-old kid from Ohio who had joined the Corps because he thought he could outrun his dad’s disappointment, and he was currently sweating through his uniform in a way that suggested his internal organs were trying to stage a mutiny. I checked my watch. We’d been sitting here for forty minutes. The heat was a physical weight, pressing down on the back of my neck, and the smell of sweat and diesel fuel was a constant companion.

I liked this. I liked the stillness. I liked knowing that the world could be reduced to a simple equation: if they come, I shoot; if they don’t, I wait. I liked the fact that I was the best at this specific job in my platoon. I had the fastest draw time, the cleanest aim, and the least amount of time spent worrying about whether God was judging my soul. God, for the record, seemed to be taking a long lunch.

“Sergeant,” Davis whispered. His voice was thin, reedy. “I can hear them. Or I think I can. Is that a truck?”

I didn’t answer immediately. I listened. The silence of the desert was a tricky thing. It wasn’t empty; it was full of potential. I heard birds calling in the distance. I heard the wind. The distant hum of a generator. And yes, a low, rhythmic thrum that vibrated through the sandbags and into my bones.

“Probably,” I said. “Or a goat that’s had too much caffeine.”

Davis let out a breath that sounded like a deflating tire. “You’re a hell of a comfort, Sarge.”

“I’m the best you’ve got. Save the pep talks for the post-combat debrief. Right now, just keep your eyes open and your finger off the trigger unless you see something that looks like a threat. And if you see it, you tell me, and I shoot it. That’s the job. That’s all I care about.”

He nodded, his helmet bobbing. He looked at me with that look, the one that said he expected me to say something inspirational, something about brotherhood or the flag. I didn’t have it in me. I had a rifle, a full magazine, and a distinct lack of interest in anyone who wasn’t me.

A few meters to my right, Corporal Miller was cleaning his fingernails with a combat knife. He was a big man, built like a refrigerator that had been welded shut, and he had the personality to match. He didn’t talk much, didn’t joke, and didn’t seem to care about anything except getting paid and going home. I respected that. Miller was efficient. He did what he was told, he kept his head down, and he didn’t ask questions.

“Miller,” I said, without looking at him. “You see anything?”

He shrugged. “Just sun.”

“Good. Keep it that way.”

I shifted my weight, trying to find a more comfortable position for my hip. The rifle was slung across my back, the weight of it a familiar anchor. I liked the weight. It grounded me. It reminded me that I was the one with the power, the one who could decide who lived and who died. It was a simple, clean power. I didn’t need to understand why we were here, or what the mission was, or who the enemy was. I just needed to be the one who didn’t get shot.

That was the game. It was a game I was very good at.

The thrumming grew louder. It was definitely a vehicle. And it was coming closer.

“Contact front,” Davis whispered, his voice tight. “Two hundred meters. Maybe less. It’s a truck. A white truck. There’s… there’s people in the back.”

I raised my rifle, bringing it to my shoulder with practiced ease. My eye slid into the scope, the world narrowing down to a crosshair. The heat shimmer distorted the view, but I could make out the shape of the vehicle. It was a pickup, modified, with a canvas cover over the bed. And yes, there were people under the canvas. They were moving.

“Civilians,” I said, the word tasting like ash. “Jesus.”

“Civilians?” Miller asked, his voice flat.

“Yeah. Looks like it. Women and kids, maybe. I can’t tell. The resolution is poor.”

Davis was quiet for a moment. I could hear his breathing, ragged and fast. He was staring at me, waiting for orders. Waiting for me to do the right thing.

I looked through the scope again. The truck was still moving, heading straight for our position. There was no one else around. No support. No air cover. Just us, the sand, and the truck full of people who had no business being on a firefighting line.

“Sarge,” Davis said. “What do we do?”

I looked at the crosshair. I looked at the people in the truck. I thought about the mission. I thought about my career. I thought about the fact that if I opened fire, I would be hitting civilians. And if I didn’t open fire, the people in that truck might be carrying weapons, and they might kill us.

It was a simple calculus. One life was worth more than the other. But whose life?

I lowered the rifle. The weight of it felt different now. The rifle felt heavy. The rifle felt unforgiving.

“Wait for my mark,” I said. My voice was steady. My voice was steady and professional. “If they open fire, we return fire. If they don’t, we let them pass. We don’t engage unless we’re forced to.”

Miller shrugged. “Your call, Sarge.”

Davis looked at me, his eyes wide. He wanted me to say something else. He wanted me to be the hero. He wanted me to be the man who would sacrifice himself for them, or at least for the mission, in a way that would make him feel like he had meant something.

But I wasn’t that man. I was the man who had the rifle. And the rifle was the most important thing in the world.

The truck slowed. The thrumming engine pitched down, and the canvas cover rippled in the wind. A hand reached up, waving. A small, frantic gesture.

I watched it. I watched the hand. I watched the fear in the eyes of the people behind the canvas. And I felt nothing.

Not because I was a monster. But because I was a Marine. And Marines don’t feel things that get in the way of the job.

The truck stopped. The engine cut. Silence rushed back in, filling the space where the noise had been.

Then, the canvas was pulled back. A face appeared. A woman. She was young, maybe twenty, with dust in her hair and a baby strapped to her chest. She looked at us. At the sandbags. At the rifles. At us.

And she screamed.

It was a high, thin sound, a sound of pure, unadulterated terror. It cut through the heat, through the dust, through the silence. It was the most human sound I had ever heard.

I raised my rifle again. My finger hovered over the trigger. The crosshair was on the center of her chest.

Davis was looking at me. Miller was looking at me.

I waited. I waited for the order. I waited for the instinct. I waited for the part of my brain that was supposed to say *don’t do it* to kick in.

It didn’t.

I was the Marine. I was the one with the power. And the power was mine to use.

I didn’t pull the trigger. But I didn’t lower the rifle, either. I just held it, steady and cold, while the woman screamed and the dust settled around us.

That was the moment. That was the choice. And I had made it.

I was still holding the rifle.

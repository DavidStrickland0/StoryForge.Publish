The dust in the trench tasted like copper and old pennies. It coated the back of Cale’s throat, gritty against his teeth, a reminder that he was still alive, still breathing, still here. The air vibrated with a low, constant hum, the sound of artillery rounds punching holes in the sky a mile back, followed by the delayed *thump* that shook the fill dirt loose from the parapet and rained down onto his helmet.

Cale adjusted his rifle, checking the chamber one last time. The action felt slick under his gloved fingers, a reassuring weight. He was an eighteen-year-old Marine, a junior enlisted grunt with a uniform that fit better than it should have, and a reputation he had spent the last three months carefully cultivating. He had earned it. Not through bravery, or at least, not the kind of bravery that people wrote songs about. He had earned it by being the first to spot the sniper, the first to suggest the flank route that saved the squad from a kill zone, and the first to point out when the food rations were being shorted. He had earned it by noticing things others missed and by making sure that when he did notice, he got a cut of the credit.

It was a simple system. The world was a machine, and he had found the levers.

"Relax, kid," Sergeant Vance said, sliding down the muddy slope of the trench to sit beside him. Vance was a man composed of gray stubble and permanent exhaustion, his eyes sunken into hollows that spoke of sleep he didn’t have. "You’re twitching."

"I’m ready," Cale said. It was the truth, technically. He was ready to fight. He was ready to shoot. He was ready to survive. He was not ready to be the one who fell, but that was a luxury he couldn’t afford to think about.

Vance grunted, pulling a crumpled cigarette from his pocket. He didn’t light it, just chewed on the end, a nervous habit that never seemed to go away. "They’re pushing the eastern ridge again. Second wave. If we hold the line, they break. If we break, they’re in the valley by nightfall."

"And if we break?" Cale asked.

Vance looked at him, a flicker of something unreadable in his tired eyes. "Then we’re dead. Or worse, we’re prisoners. And in this war, there’s no difference."

Cale nodded, his gaze fixed on the tree line where the enemy was massing. He could see the glint of rifles, the huddle of bodies moving with the slow, deliberate purpose of men who knew they had numbers on their side. He felt a surge of adrenaline, cold and sharp. He liked this. He liked the clarity of it. There was no ambiguity in a battlefield. There was the threat, and there was the response. There was the weakness, and there was the exploitation.

He looked to his left, where Private Miller was huddled in the dirt, his face pale, his hands shaking so badly he could barely keep his grip on his weapon. Miller was nineteen, a farm boy from Ohio who had lied about his age to get in. He was terrified. Cale could see it in the way Miller’s eyes darted, in the way his lips moved as if he were praying to a god he wasn’t sure existed.

Cale felt a familiar contempt rise in his chest. It was a cold, hard feeling, comfortable in a way that fear was not. Miller was useless. Not because he was a bad soldier, but because he was weak. He was a liability. In a firefight, Miller would freeze, or he would panic, or he would make a mistake that would get them both killed. And yet, here he was, taking up space, eating rations, breathing air that Cale needed to stay alive.

Cale didn’t hate Miller. Hatred was too emotional, too messy. Cale simply valued efficiency. And Miller was inefficient. He had already pointed this out to Vance, casually, during a rest break. *He’s slowing us down, Sarge. If we’re going to hold this line, we need every man firing, not just standing there shaking.* Vance had listened. Vance had understood. That was why Cale was still alive, and why Miller was still in the trench.

The enemy opened fire.

It started as a crack, then a roar, then a continuous hiss of lead tearing through the air. The world turned into a blur of motion and noise. Bullets kicked up dirt around them, spraying Cale’s face, his neck, his uniform. The sound was deafening, a physical pressure that made his ears ring and his vision blur.

Cale didn’t think. He didn’t plan. He reacted.

He dropped to his stomach, his rifle coming up with a fluid motion that had become second nature. He fired three rounds, feeling the recoil shove against his shoulder, and saw a figure in the tree line go down. He didn’t know if he’d hit him. He didn’t care. He kept firing, his aim steady, his breathing controlled.

Beside him, Miller was screaming. It was a high, thin sound, lost in the chaos of the battle. Cale glanced over and saw the boy firing wildly, his muzzle flash strobing in the dusty air. He was missing everything. He was wasting ammunition. He was a target.

Cale’s mind worked. He could move. He could grab Miller, push him down, take control of the situation. He could protect the boy. He knew that. He had the training, the strength, the will.

But he didn’t.

Instead, he pulled his rifle back to his eye and focused on the enemy. He saw a glint of metal, a head poking out from behind a boulder. He lined up the crosshairs, held his breath, and fired. The recoil was a familiar punch. The head vanished.

He felt a surge of satisfaction. It was clean. It was effective. It was his.

The battle raged on. The air was thick with smoke and the smell of gunpowder. Cale’s hands were steady, his mind clear. He was in the zone, the place where the noise faded and the world narrowed down to the target in his sights. He was a machine, precise and lethal.

And then, the screaming stopped.

Cale blinked, the smoke stinging his eyes. He looked to his left. Miller was still there, but he was silent now. His hands were still on his rifle, but his eyes were wide, his mouth open in a silent scream. He was frozen.

Cale looked back at the enemy. They were still coming. The line was holding, barely. He could feel the weight of it, the precarious balance of power. One wrong move, one moment of hesitation, and it would all come crashing down.

He looked at Miller again. The boy was useless. He was a dead weight. He was a threat.

Cale made a decision. He didn’t think about it. He didn’t weigh the options. He simply acted.

He reached out and grabbed Miller’s rifle, twisting it away from the boy’s hands. Miller flinched, his eyes widening in shock. Cale held the rifle up, his finger on the trigger, his aim steady.

"Shoot," he said. His voice was calm, flat, devoid of emotion. "Or I will."

Miller stared at him, his face a mask of terror and betrayal. He didn’t shoot.

Cale lowered the rifle, a small smile touching his lips. He had secured the weapon. He had neutralized the threat. He had done the right thing.

He looked back at the enemy, his eyes hard, his mind clear. The battle was far from over. He had more to do. He had more to prove.

He was ready.

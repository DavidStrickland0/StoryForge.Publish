Carter’s rifle barrel was still smoking when the next burst hit the dirt two inches from his left boot. He didn’t flinch. Flinching was for recruits who hadn’t yet figured out that fear was just a biological error, a glitch in the software that could be patched with enough adrenaline and enough bodies between you and the muzzle flash. He adjusted his breathing, counting the seconds between the crack of the enemy’s automatic weapon and the thud of the round impacting the earth. Three seconds. Maybe four. The man across the street was working from a static position, likely a window, likely a balcony. Carter liked static positions. Static positions meant predictable geometry, and predictable geometry meant he could solve the equation with a single, clean headshot.

He liked the simplicity of it. In the real world, people were messy. They lied, they cheated, they cried over things that didn’t matter, and they expected the universe to owe them a break because they’d had a bad week. Here, in the gray ruin of the city block, the universe was honest. Bullets traveled in straight lines. Gravity pulled things down. And if you were slow, or stupid, or soft, you died. It was a fair system, as long as you were the one holding the gun.

"Move up! Move up!" Sergeant Miller’s voice crackled through the radio, static-heavy and urgent.

Carter rolled to his knees, scanning the debris field ahead of him. Concrete chunks, shattered glass, the twisted metal of a collapsed vending machine. He could see the muzzle flashes from the enemy position, bright orange sparks against the dim morning light. Two shooters. Maybe three. He counted the flashes: one, two, one, two. Two shooters. Good. That meant the room was smaller, the angles tighter. He could work them down.

He checked his magazine. Twelve rounds. He had plenty. He always had plenty, as long as he kept moving and kept his head on a pivot. The guy to his left, a kid named Evans who had a jaw like a wet paper bag and hands that shook every time he lifted his weapon, was crouching behind a broken tire, staring at the ground as if he expected it to offer him cover.

"Evans," Carter said, his voice low and flat. "You’re in my lane. Get off the line."

Evans looked up, his eyes wide and watery. "I’m... I’m holding, sir."

"You’re holding your breath and your fear. Get out of the way." Carter didn’t look at him. He was watching the window, calculating the angle of elevation. If he fired from here, he’d have to jump the gap. If he jumped, he was exposed for maybe two seconds. Two seconds was a lifetime. Two seconds was enough time for someone to put a round in his chest if he hesitated. He didn’t hesitate. He never hesitated. That was the difference between a Marine and a civilian. Civilians thought in paragraphs; Marines thought in bullet points.

He signaled to Miller, a quick two-finger gesture. *Flank left.* Miller nodded, a grimace twisting his face, and began to move. Carter waited until Miller had cleared the corner, then he kicked the broken tire aside, using it as a shield while he sprinted the five meters to the next cover point, a shattered storefront window. He slid into the frame, his rifle up, his eyes locked on the target.

The shooter in the window was a man in a black jacket, his face obscured by a bandana. He was young, maybe twenty-five, and he was firing with a steady, rhythmic precision that suggested he wasn’t a desperate rebel but a trained soldier. Or a professional. Carter liked professionals. They played by the rules. They didn’t hide behind civilians or use their own comrades as human shields. They stood their ground and fought.

Carter raised his rifle, his breathing slowing to a rhythmic hum. He aligned the crosshairs with the man’s chest. He could take the head, but the angle was tight. The chest was safer. He squeezed the trigger.

The first round hit the man in the shoulder, spinning him slightly. The second round hit the center mass, and the man slumped over the windowsill, his rifle clattering to the ground.

"Down one!" Carter called out, his voice steady.

Evans was still behind the tire, his face pale. "Jesus, Carter, that was close."

"Stay down," Carter said. "You’re not doing anything but getting in the way."

It was a harsh thing to say, but it was the truth. Evans was weak, and weakness was a liability. Carter had seen what happened when a unit had a weak link. The chain broke. The mission failed. People died. He wouldn’t let that happen because he felt sorry for a kid who couldn’t handle the pressure. He had to be the anchor, the one who kept them all alive by being the best damn shooter in the room.

He moved again, pushing toward the next objective. The adrenaline was a clean, cold liquid in his veins, sharpening his senses, making the world bright and clear. He could hear the heartbeat of the enemy, the sound of their breathing, the creak of the building settling. It was a symphony of chaos, and he was the conductor.

He felt a surge of pride, a warm, golden glow in his chest. This was what he was built for. This was why he had joined the Marines. Not for the pay, not for the prestige, but for this. For the feeling of being in control, of being the one who decided who lived and who died. It was a power that no one could take from him, no matter how many times he was told to be humble, to be kind, to be selfless. He was not selfless. He was efficient. And in this world, efficiency was the highest virtue.

He reached the next corner, his rifle sweeping the room. Clear. He moved on. The battle was a game, and he was winning. He could feel it in the way his muscles moved, in the way his mind worked, in the way the world bent around him to fit his will. He was the best. He was the one who would survive. And that was all that mattered.

The sun had set, but the heat did not follow. It stayed, a heavy, suffocating blanket pressed against Caelen’s skin. The stone of the pen radiated the day’s accumulated fury, baking the air until it tasted of dust and stale sweat. Caelen sat against the wall, his legs crossed, his back rigid. He had not slept. In the Marines, sleep was a skill, a switch you flipped when the adrenaline faded. Here, the adrenaline was still low, simmering beneath the surface, but the physical reality of his body was screaming for attention.

His throat was a slit of raw flesh. Every breath scraped against it, a dry, rasping friction that made his chest ache. The thirst was not a suggestion; it was a command. It was the same kind of need as the bullet had been in the courtyard, but instead of triggering a surge of protective instinct, it triggered a cold, hollow panic. He looked at his hands. They were trembling, a fine, high-frequency vibration that he could not stop. The numbers in his vision, **1** and **1**, remained static. They did not pulse with thirst. They did not change because he was dying. They were indifferent.

He looked around the pen. The other captives were in various states of collapse. The alpha, the scarred man who had been revalued by the masked overseer, sat with his head in his hands. His shoulders shook, not with sobs, but with a tremor of exhaustion. The younger slaves had huddled into a tight cluster, their breathing shallow and rapid. A few lay on the ground, their eyes closed, lips cracked and bleeding.

Caelen’s gaze swept the perimeter. He needed water. It was a biological imperative, simple and absolute. He scanned the guards. There were two at the gate, just as there had been before. They stood with their arms crossed, their staffs resting on the ground. One of them held a small, leather canteen at his hip. The leather was dark, worn smooth by use. It was a small object, unremarkable in the grand scheme of the brutal hierarchy, but to Caelen, it looked like a mirage.

He stood up. The movement was slow, deliberate. He had to conserve energy. His legs felt like lead, but his mind was sharp, cutting through the fog of dehydration. He walked toward the gate. His boots crunched on the packed dirt. The sound was loud in the silence.

The guard with the canteen looked up. His eyes narrowed. He did not lower his staff, but he did not raise it either. He watched Caelen with the same detached, appraising gaze he had used before, but now there was a new element: caution.

Caelen stopped three feet from the guard. He did not reach for the canteen. He did not gesture. He simply stood there, looking at the man. He let the silence stretch. He let the guard feel the weight of Caelen’s presence, the potential for violence that hung in the air like smoke.

The guard’s hand moved to his belt. It was a slow, defensive motion. He was not going to fight Caelen. He knew that. Caelen had made that clear in the previous encounter. But the guard also knew that he had no obligation to feed the asset. In the system of the pen, water was not a right; it was a resource, allocated by the overseers, controlled by the masters.

The guard pulled the canteen from his belt. He held it out, not offering it, but displaying it. A test.

Caelen looked at the canteen. Then he looked at the guard’s face. The guard’s eyes were hard, but there was a flicker of something else: uncertainty. He was afraid of Caelen, but he was also bound by duty. He could not give the water. If he did, he would be defying the chain of command. He would be risking his own position.

Caelen understood the dynamic. Fear of violence was enough to stop the guard from striking him. But fear of authority was stronger. The guard was a cog in a machine, and the machine did not care about the thirst of one asset.

Caelen lowered his gaze. He stepped back. The gesture was subtle, but it was clear. He was not going to take it by force. Not yet.

He turned and walked back to the center of the pen. The other captives watched him. The alpha looked up, his eyes red-rimmed and hollow. He saw Caelen’s defeat, or rather, his restraint. There was no triumph in Caelen’s posture, only a cold, calculated assessment.

Caelen sat down again, but this time, he did not lean back against the wall. He turned his body slightly toward the cluster of younger slaves. One of them, a boy who looked no older than sixteen, was sitting with his back against the wall. His lips were blue-tinged, his skin pale and waxy. He was watching Caelen with a mixture of fear and desperate hope.

Caelen looked at the boy. He did not speak. He did not need to. He simply looked at the boy’s hands. They were empty. But the boy was holding something. A small, clay pot, no bigger than a fist. It was cracked, leaking a thin stream of dark liquid onto the dirt. It was water. Dirty, murky, probably contaminated, but water.

The boy saw Caelen’s gaze. He flinched, clutching the pot tighter to his chest. His eyes darted to the guards, then back to Caelen. He was terrified. He knew what Caelen was. He knew what Caelen had done to the guard. He knew that if Caelen wanted the pot, he would take it. And if he took it, the boy would be punished.

Caelen felt the pull in his vision. The left column, the **1** on the left, seemed to hum with a low, aggressive vibration. It was the impulse to take. To claim. To use his strength to secure his survival. It was the old Marine instinct, the one that said *take what you need, leave no room for error*.

But the right column, the **1** on the right, was quiet. Still. It was the impulse to give. To bear. To sacrifice.

Caelen looked at the boy. The boy was not an asset to be used. He was a person. A fragile, frightened person who had nothing to lose but a little dirty water. And Caelen had nothing to gain but a moment of relief.

He realized, with a cold clarity that cut through the thirst, that his power was not absolute. It was limited by the rules of the game. He could intimidate the guard, but he could not make the guard give him water. He could take the pot from the boy, but then he would be no different from the masked man in the purple robes. He would be a predator in a pen of prey. And that was not who he was. Not anymore.

Caelen reached into his tunic. He had nothing. No coins, no food, no weapons. But he had something else. He had his word. Or rather, he had his presence. He had the ability to protect.

He looked at the boy. He held out his hand, palm up. It was an open gesture. A request. Not a demand.

The boy stared at the hand. He looked at the guards. They were watching, but they were distant, their attention focused on the gate, on the perimeter. They were not looking at this small transaction in the center of the pen.

The boy hesitated. Then, slowly, he reached into the clay pot. He dipped his fingers into the murky water. He did not offer the pot. He offered a few drops. A gesture. A test.

Caelen leaned forward. He placed his lips around the boy’s fingers. The water was cold, gritty, and tasted of earth and rot. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever tasted. He drank slowly, savoring the coolness, the relief. It was not enough to quench his thirst, but it was enough to buy him time.

When he finished, he sat back. The boy pulled his hand away, his fingers trembling. He looked at Caelen with wide, fearful eyes. He expected a strike. A demand for more.

Caelen looked at the boy. Then he looked at the alpha, who was watching from the shadows. Caelen raised his hand and pointed to the boy. Then he pointed to himself. Then he made a shielding gesture, placing his hand over the boy.

*I will protect him.*

It was a lie. He did not know if he could keep it. He did not know if the guards would care. But it was a choice. A small, insignificant choice in a world of brutality.

The boy’s eyes widened. The fear did not leave, but it was replaced by something else. Confusion. Hope.

Caelen looked at the numbers in his vision.

**1**
**1**

They were still the same. But the left one felt lighter. The right one felt heavier.

He had not secured his survival. He had not obtained the water he needed. But he had done something else. He had chosen. And in the cold, dark pen, that choice felt like a spark in the void.

He leaned back against the wall, closing his eyes. The thirst was still there, a burning ache in his throat. But the panic was gone. He had bought himself a little time. And in this place, time was the only currency that mattered.

The boy sat down, clutching his pot. He did not look at Caelen anymore. He looked at the ground. But his shoulders were less hunched. He was breathing easier.

Caelen watched the stars appear, one by one, through the square of sky above the pen. They were bright, cold, and distant. He watched them, and for the first time since he had woken in this world, he did not feel alone. He was not a Marine. He was not a slave. He was Caelen. And he was going to play this game to the end.

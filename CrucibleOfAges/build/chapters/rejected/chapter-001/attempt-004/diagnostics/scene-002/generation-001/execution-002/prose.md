The decision to move was already made before the thought fully formed. My feet were already shifting, boots grinding against the shattered asphalt and broken glass. The mother was still standing there, three meters to my right, her body rigid with the kind of paralysis that comes from realizing the world has just offered you a choice you don’t have the vocabulary to understand. She wasn’t looking at me. She was looking at the roofline, her eyes wide and unblinking, tracking the invisible threat that had just tried to erase her.

I didn’t wait for her to move. I didn’t wait for Davis to confirm his position or for the tactical situation to clarify. The instinct that had kept me alive for four months in the Corps—the one that told me to calculate risk, to preserve my own asset value, to let the collateral take the hit—slipped, just for a heartbeat, into something else. Something older and far less logical.

The glint came from the ledge on the north side of the intersection. It was a sharp, metallic flash, bright against the gray smoke, and the sound of the rifle bolt cycling was a dry, mechanical crack that cut through the distant roar of the artillery.

I didn’t think. I didn’t weigh the odds. I didn’t consider that I was an eighteen-year-old recruit with no cover and a full magazine that was going to be useless if I was already dead. I just moved.

I lunged to the right, my body unfolding in a blur of motion that felt slower than it should have. The air in my lungs burned, my muscles screaming in protest as I threw myself forward. I hit the ground on my knees, then rolled, my shoulder striking the curb with a jolt that shot pain up my spine. I came up on one knee, my rifle still in my hands, my eyes locked on the woman.

She was still standing. The bullet that was meant for her chest, for the baby pressed against her hip, had passed through the air where she had been a fraction of a second ago. It had missed. Or rather, it had missed because I had moved.

I didn’t stand up. I stayed low, my back against the concrete of the overturned bus, my breath coming in short, sharp gasps. The mother was still there. She was alive. The baby was still in her arms, crying, a thin, reedy sound that pierced the ambient noise of the battle.

I looked at my hands. They were shaking. I looked at the mother. She was looking at me now, and there was a question in her eyes that I couldn’t answer. She didn’t know why I had moved. She didn’t know that I had made a choice that contradicted every training manual, every tactical doctrine, every selfish impulse that had driven me to join the Marines in the first place.

I felt a strange, hollow sensation in my chest. It wasn’t pride. It wasn’t relief. It was a kind of disorientation, as if the ground had shifted beneath me and I was trying to find my footing in a world that had suddenly become more complex than I was equipped to handle.

"Move," I said, my voice rough. I pointed toward the alleyway to the east, the direction of our objective. "Now."

She hesitated. The baby cried again. I saw the fear in her eyes, the uncertainty, the way her body was still coiled, ready to run but unable to decide which way. I didn’t have time to explain. I didn’t have time to comfort her. I just needed her to be alive and out of the kill zone.

I stood up, my rifle raised, my eyes scanning the rooftops. The glint was gone. The silence was heavy, pressing against my ears. I took a step forward, then another. The mother followed, her movements clumsy, the baby shifting in her arms. We moved into the alleyway, the narrow space between the buildings offering a sliver of cover.

The air smelled of dust and burnt plastic. The walls were close, the shadows deep. I could hear the distant sound of gunfire, the muffled thud of explosions. I could hear my own heartbeat, loud and steady in my ears.

I looked back at the mother. She was watching me, her eyes wide, her face pale. She didn’t speak. I didn’t expect her to. We were two strangers in a war that didn’t care about our names, our stories, or our choices. And yet, for a moment, in that narrow alleyway, I felt a connection that I couldn’t name. A thread, thin and fragile, stretching between us.

I turned away and kept moving. The objective was two blocks east. The mission was still in progress. The war was still happening. And I was still alive.

The thought settled in my mind, cold and clear. I had just saved a life. I had just made a choice that defied every rule I had ever known. And I didn’t know what to do with it.

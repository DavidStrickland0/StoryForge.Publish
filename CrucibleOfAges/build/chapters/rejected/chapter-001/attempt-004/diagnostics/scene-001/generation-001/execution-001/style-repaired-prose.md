The dust tasted like copper and old pennies.

I spat it out, my boot finding the edge of a collapsed concrete slab for leverage. Above me, the sky was a bruised purple, choked by the smoke of three burning fuel depots and the distant, rhythmic thud of artillery that never quite hit us but always landed close enough to rattle the teeth in my skull.

"Check your six, Davis," I said, my voice rough from the grit. "And for the love of God, watch the windows. I don’t care if you think they’re empty. In this city, empty is a suggestion, not a fact."

Davis, a kid who looked like he’d been pressed into service because he had no better place to be, nodded rapidly. His eyes were wide, reflecting the orange flicker of a fire that had started in the bakery two streets over. "Copy, Sergeant. Checking six."

I didn't wait. I moved.

This was the part of the job that the recruiters didn’t put in the brochures. They talked about service, brotherhood, and the American way. They didn’t mention that the actual way we did business was a frantic, ugly scramble through a maze of broken brick and screaming civilians, trying to keep a head on the game while the other side tried to put a round in it.

I was eighteen. I had been in the Corps for four months. In that time, I had learned that discipline was a luxury for people who weren’t hungry, that rank was a shield that only worked if you were the one holding the sword, and that the world was a lot more chaotic and less structured than the textbooks claimed.

I liked the chaos. It was honest. In a classroom or a boardroom, you had to pretend. You had to smile, you had to nod, you had to pretend that the person sitting across from you wasn’t thinking about how to steal your lunch money or your promotion. Out here, the pretense was stripped away. If you were slow, you died. If you were selfish, you survived. And if you were lucky, you got to keep the things you wanted.

I rounded the corner of a shattered intersection. A bus, its windows blown out, lay on its side. A family was huddled under the driver’s seat, their faces pale and slick with sweat. They stared at me with a mixture of terror and hope that felt like a physical weight.

I kept moving.

"Where’s the rest of the squad?" I asked, not stopping.

Davis was ten meters behind me, his rifle held tight to his chest. "Holding the flank, sir. They said the street ahead is hot."

"Hot is relative," I muttered. "Usually, it just means they’re trying to kill you and haven’t figured out which way you’re coming from yet."

I scanned the rooftops. The enemy wasn’t hiding; they were positioned. I could see the glint of a scope on a ledge to my left, a shadow moving behind a pile of rubble to my right. They were waiting. They knew we were coming. They knew exactly what we were and what we wanted.

And they didn’t care.

That was the thing about war that no one warned me about. It wasn’t a chess match where every move had a counter-move, a logic to the flow. It was a brawl in a dark room. You punched, they punched back, and eventually, one of you stopped moving.

I reached the next junction. The air was thick with the smell of burnt rubber and something sweeter, cloying, that I refused to identify. A crowd had gathered on the far side of the intersection, pressing against a collapsed wall. They weren’t soldiers. They were people. Men, women, children, all crouching in the dirt, their eyes fixed on the smoke rising from the center of the square.

One of them, a woman in a torn blue dress, stood up. She held a baby to her chest, her face a mask of desperate exhaustion. She looked at me, then at the shadows where the enemy snipers were likely perched, and then back at me.

I felt a flicker of irritation. Not fear. Not pity. Irritation was the only emotion I felt.

She was in the way.

In a tactical sense, she was a complication. A civilian in a kill zone is a liability. She slows you down. She forces you to check angles that you know are dangerous but have to verify because protocol demands it. She creates a moral hazard that your commanding officer will have to clean up in a debriefing, and she makes the whole operation look messy.

I signaled to Davis. "Hold position. I’m going to check the alley on the left. Keep your eyes on the woman with the baby. If she moves, you shoot. Do you understand?"

Davis looked at me, then at the woman, then back at me. His hand trembled on the trigger guard. "Sir, she’s... she’s just a civilian."

"Everything in this city is a civilian until they pull a trigger," I said, my voice flat. "If she’s hiding a weapon, you’ll be dead before you know it. If she’s not, she’s in the way. Keep her in the frame. If she steps out, you put a round in the ground next to her foot. Don’t shoot her, but don’t let her move."

It was a bad order. I knew it was a bad order. But it was the order that kept me alive, and it was the order that kept the mission on track.

I moved into the alley. The shadows here were deeper, cooler. I could hear the distant sound of a car engine, sputtering and dying. I pressed my back against the brick wall, my rifle raised, and waited.

The woman was still there. I could see her from the edge of the alley, a small, still figure against the backdrop of destruction. She wasn’t moving. She was just standing there, holding her baby, staring into the middle distance with an expression that I couldn’t quite read. It wasn’t fear, exactly. It was something harder. Her expression was one of resignation.

I felt a twinge of something in my gut. A small, inconvenient discomfort.

I pushed it down.

I had a job to do. I had a promotion to earn. I had a life to build, and this city was just a stepping stone. A place where I could prove that I was more than just a kid from a small town with a big dream.

I checked my magazine. The magazine was full. I checked my compass. North was behind me. The objective was two blocks east.

I stepped out of the alley.

The woman was still there. She hadn’t moved. She hadn’t looked at me. She was just existing, a part of the landscape, a detail in the chaotic painting of the battle.

I walked past her.

My boots crunched on the broken glass. The sound was loud in the sudden silence. I could feel her eyes on me, or maybe I imagined I did. It didn’t matter. I was a Marine. I was a professional. I was doing my job.

And for a moment, as I moved into the open space of the intersection, the world felt balanced. The threat was manageable. The path was clear. I was in control.

Then the shadow on the rooftop shifted.

I saw it. A glint of light, a fraction of a second before the sound. My body reacted before my mind did. I dropped, rolling to the left, my rifle coming up.

The bullet hit the concrete where my head had been. Dust puffed up around me.

I was alive.

I was in control.

And the woman with the baby was still standing there, three meters to my right, completely unaware that her life had just hung by a thread of luck and my quick reflexes.

I stayed low, scanning the rooftop. The shooter was gone. Or maybe they were reloading. I didn’t know. I didn’t care.

I looked at the woman. She was looking at the spot where the bullet had struck. Her face had changed. The resignation was gone. In its place was a raw, naked terror.

I felt a strange, cold calm settle over me.

This was it. This was the moment. The moment where the story was supposed to turn. The moment where I was supposed to feel something.

But I didn’t.

I just felt the weight of my rifle, the grit in my teeth, and the absolute, unyielding certainty that I was the only person in this intersection who knew what to do.

I stood up.

"Let’s go," I said to the empty air. "We’re behind schedule."

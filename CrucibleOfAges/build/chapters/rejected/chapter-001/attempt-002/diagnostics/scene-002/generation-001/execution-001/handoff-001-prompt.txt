The first shot cracked off the car’s roof, showering sparks that died instantly in the haze. I didn’t flinch. I’d been in firefights before, usually the ones where the other side was stupid enough to let me hear them breathing. These three were different. They were fast. One of them rolled behind the bumper, disappearing into the undercarriage shadows, while the other two kept their heads low, firing in disciplined bursts. *Pop-pop. Pop-pop.* Rhythmic. Their fire was disciplined and trained.

My magazine had eight rounds left. I could count them in my head, a habit from the range that felt more comforting than it had any right to be. Eight rounds, three targets, and a street full of broken glass that made every movement a potential trip hazard. I shifted my weight, feeling the gravel bite through the sole of my boot, and let the rifle settle into the hollow of my shoulder. The stock was warm from the previous burst, the metal slick with a thin film of moisture that wasn’t rain. It was always like that in the cities. The air tasted of copper and burnt plastic, a flavor that coated the back of my throat and refused to leave.

Miller was moving toward me, his silhouette cutting through the grey dust. He was close enough now that I could hear the rattle of his gear, the heavy clank of his ammunition pouches swinging against his thigh. He was efficient. He was useful. And he was about to be a problem, because he had a way of looking at a situation and trying to fix it with a speech instead of a bullet.

"Hostiles at the vehicle!" Miller shouted, his voice strained but controlled. "They’re suppressing! I need a window!"

I didn’t answer. I raised my head, scanning the gap between the car and the crumbling wall of the building to my left. There was a doorway there, half-collapsed, the frame warped by the earlier blast. If I moved left, I could flank them. It was a clean maneuver. It was a clean, textbook maneuver. The kind of thing that got written up in after-action reports with little stars next to the officer’s name.

I took two steps to the left. The gravel crunched.

A bullet tore through the air where my head had been a second before, striking the concrete wall behind me. Dust puffed out, a white cloud that obscured my vision for a heartbeat. I dropped into a crouch, my rifle sweeping across the street. The three hostiles were still there, but they weren’t firing anymore. They were watching me. They were waiting for me to make a mistake.

Miller reached my position, sliding in from the right. He was out of breath, his face a mask of sweat and grime. "You okay?" he asked, though the question was rhetorical. He was checking his magazine, clicking the bolt to ensure a round was seated. A small, mechanical sound in the chaos.

"I’m fine," I said. My voice sounded flat, even. I was fine. I was in control. The situation was contained, provided I could keep it contained.

"Can you cover the driver’s side?" Miller asked, already moving to my right, positioning himself to engage the hostiles from a new angle. "I’ll take the passenger side. We pin them down and flush them out."

It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan that worked when you had enough firepower to apply pressure, when you had enough people to fill the gaps. I had eight rounds. Miller had whatever was in his clip, probably full, probably more than he needed. We were a team. We were a unit. We were the best thing this battalion had produced in months, according to the briefing slides that had been projected onto a wall that no longer existed.

I looked at Miller. He was watching the car, his eyes narrowed, calculating. He was expecting me to follow his lead. He was expecting the standard operating procedure to hold.

"Miller," I said.

He glanced at me. "Yeah?"

"Stay here. Watch the alley behind us."

His brow furrowed. "The alley? What’s in the alley?"

"Nothing. I’m watching the alley. You watch the car."

He hesitated. For a second, I saw the confusion in his eyes, the slight pause where his brain tried to reconcile my order with the tactical reality. It was a small thing. A micro-expression. But I saw it, and I knew that in that moment, he was no longer a partner. He was a variable. A piece of the puzzle that I could move, or leave, depending on what the board required.

"Got it," he said, his voice dropping an octave, the compliance slipping in. He turned, moving toward the alleyway, his back to the hostiles.

I waited. One second. Two seconds passed. The hostiles didn’t move. They were still crouched behind the car, their weapons raised, their eyes fixed on me. I was alone now. Just me, the rifle, and the eight rounds of ammunition that represented my entire tactical advantage.

The street was quiet. The smoke curled around the overturned car, drifting lazily in the still air. The smell of cordite was fading, replaced by the sharper, cleaner scent of ozone and wet concrete. I could hear my own breathing, steady and slow. I could hear the blood rushing in my ears, a low hum that filled the silence.

I was ready. I had the high ground, the better position, the clearer line of sight. They were trapped. They were waiting for me to make a mistake. They were waiting for Miller to come back and provide the cover fire that I had promised.

But Miller wasn’t coming back. Not yet. And I didn’t need him.

I raised my rifle, my finger resting on the trigger guard. The metal was cool against my skin, a familiar comfort. I was going to kill them. It was simple. It was efficient. It was what I was good at.

And for the first time in a long time, I felt no rush. No chemical spike of adrenaline. Just the cold, clean satisfaction of a job to be done.

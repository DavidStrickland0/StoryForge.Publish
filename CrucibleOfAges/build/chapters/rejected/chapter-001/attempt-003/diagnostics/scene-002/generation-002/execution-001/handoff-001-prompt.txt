The open ground was a graveyard of churned earth and broken concrete. I checked my magazine, a reflex so automatic it barely registered as a conscious thought. Three rotations had taught me that the enemy’s fire came in bursts, and the gaps between them were where you lived. I started moving, keeping low, using the rubble for cover only when the tracers stopped singing past my ear.

My unit was scattered. I looked for the others. Private Daniels was two hundred yards to my left, dragging his feet through the mud. He wasn’t returning fire. He was just trying to keep his head down, his rifle slung over his shoulder like a dead weight. Corporal Vance was gone. I didn’t know where. Maybe he was dead. Maybe he was hiding. It didn’t matter. The institutional support I’d relied on for the last three years—the chain of command, the shared objective, the comforting fiction that we were a single organism—was dissolving into individual survival instincts.

I was alone. The realization was cold, a physical sensation in my gut. I wasn’t a Marine anymore. I was just a man with a gun in a field, and I was the most valuable thing on the ground.

A burst of fire hit the dirt ten feet in front of me. I threw myself behind a low wall of shattered bricks. My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic, birdlike flutter. I pressed my back against the cold stone and tried to breathe. In and out. I forced my breathing to remain steady. Fear was a resource, and I was spending it too fast. I needed to be efficient. I needed to be useful.

I looked at my rifle. It was a reliable tool. It did what I told it to do. I liked that. People were unreliable. People panicked. People made mistakes. People had families and debts and reasons to hesitate. My rifle didn’t care about any of that. It only cared about the trigger pull and the release of the hammer.

I checked my position. The enemy was advancing from the north, a thin line of figures moving through the smoke. They were disciplined. They were moving in pairs. They were using each other. I looked at Daniels again. He was still just standing there, shaking. He was a liability. He was a weakness. And weaknesses got killed.

I could stay here. I could wait for a rotation that might never come. I could hide in this pile of bricks until my hands stopped shaking. That was the safe option. That was the human option. But I wasn’t human. I was a Marine. And Marines didn’t wait. Marines acted.

I stood up. The motion was fluid, practiced. I moved from the cover of the wall to the open ground, using the rhythm of the enemy fire as a metronome. I took a step forward. I paused between each step. I took another step forward. I paused to observe the enemy's movements. My eyes scanned the line of attackers. I was looking for the gap, the opening, the moment where their formation broke.

I found it. A slight hesitation in their left flank. Two seconds of confusion. That was enough.

I dropped to one knee and fired. The rifle kicked against my shoulder, a familiar, comforting shock. The first round took the man in the front. He dropped without a sound. The second round hit the dirt, sparking off a piece of metal. The third round took the next man in the chest. He folded.

The enemy fire stopped. For a moment, the only sound was the ringing in my ears and the rapid beating of my own heart. I had done it. I had survived. I had been useful.

I stood up, my rifle ready. I scanned the area. The two men I had hit were down. The others had scattered, retreating into the smoke. I was free. For the first time in hours, I wasn’t being shot at. I was the hunter.

I looked around. The battlefield was a mess of mud and blood and broken equipment. I saw a body in the distance, a Marine uniform, the colors faded and dirty. I didn’t know who it was. It didn’t matter. I turned away and moved toward the center of the field.

I was efficient. I was effective. I was the best thing on the ground. And I was going to keep moving, because stopping was the only thing that got you killed. I checked my magazine. I had enough rounds for another two engagements. I had enough time to find a better position. I had enough skill to survive.

I was alone, but I wasn’t helpless. I was a weapon. And weapons didn’t need friends. They needed a target.

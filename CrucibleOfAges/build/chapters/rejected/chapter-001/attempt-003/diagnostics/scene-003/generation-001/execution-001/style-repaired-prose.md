The street was quiet in the way that only a graveyard is quiet, a heavy, suffocating silence that pressed against my eardrums. I had been walking for three minutes, maybe four. My left arm hung at my side, a dead weight that dragged against my thigh with every step. The blood had stopped soaking through the fabric of my uniform, which was a good sign, though it didn’t change the fact that I was still leaking fluid onto the cracked asphalt. I checked the magazine well of my M4 again, a nervous habit that had become second nature. The bolt was home. The chamber was loaded. I was functional.

That was the important part. Everything else was just noise.

I rounded the corner of a collapsed building, the debris forming a jagged skyline against the orange sky. The sun was dipping below the horizon, turning the dust motes in the air into suspended gold. It was almost beautiful, in a sterile, dead way. If you ignored the smell of cordite and rotting meat that hung thick in the breeze.

I stopped.

There was a figure in the middle of the street.

I didn’t see her at first. I saw the shape of her, the hunch of her shoulders, the way she was cradling a bundle of cloth in her arms. My brain, still running on tactical protocols, tried to categorize her. I wondered if she was hostile. It was unlikely that she was hostile. She wasn’t holding a weapon. She wasn’t wearing a uniform. She was small, frail, and moving with a desperate, jerky rhythm that had nothing to do with combat.

A civilian.

I cursed under my breath, the sound harsh and ragged in the quiet street. Civilians were a liability. They were obstacles. They were the reason missions went sideways, the reason we had to hold our fire, the reason we took casualties we didn’t expect. They were the chaotic variable that no amount of training could predict.

I kept walking. My boots crunched on the glass and gravel. I kept my eyes forward, my rifle raised, my finger on the trigger guard. I was scanning for the real threat. The one that would come from the shadows, from the rubble, from the dark windows of the gutted buildings. The one that would put a round in my chest before I could react.

The woman turned.

She was young, maybe twenty, maybe twenty-five. Her face was smeared with dirt and tears, her hair matted and tangled. She was holding a baby, a small, squirming bundle that was crying softly, a sound that seemed impossibly loud in the silence. She looked at me, and for a second, I saw her eyes widen. Her eyes widened with fear. She recognized me as a soldier. She knew I was a soldier. She knew what that meant.

I stopped.

I should have kept moving. I should have looked away, focused on the perimeter, on the threat. I should have treated her as a piece of scenery, an obstacle to navigate around. That was the training. That was the discipline.

But I didn’t move.

I stood there, my rifle still raised, my breathing shallow and controlled. The baby stopped crying. The woman stared at me, her eyes wide and unblinking. I could see the fear in her face, the raw, animal terror that comes from being in the middle of a war you didn’t start and can’t stop. I could see the hope, too, the fragile, desperate hope that maybe, just maybe, the soldier in front of her was a good one.

I felt a pang in my chest, a tight, uncomfortable pressure that had nothing to do with the wound in my shoulder. It was a feeling I didn’t like. It was a feeling that suggested I cared. I hated it. It was a weakness. A distraction.

I looked at the baby. Then I looked at the woman. Then I looked at the dark windows of the building across the street.

Something was moving in there.

A shadow. A shape. A glint of metal.

I didn’t think. I didn’t calculate. I didn’t weigh the odds or assess the risk. I just moved.

I stepped forward, placing myself between the woman and the window. It was a stupid move. It was a reckless, suicidal move. I was wounded, isolated, and outgunned. I was a target.

But I was the only target.

I turned to face the window, my rifle aimed squarely at the darkness. My heart was pounding in my ears, a frantic, drumming rhythm that drowned out the sounds of the city. I could feel the heat of the sun on my back, the cold of the wind on my face. I could feel the weight of the rifle in my hands, the solidity of the weapon, the promise of violence.

I was ready.

I was the wall. I was the shield. I was the only thing standing between that woman and the death that was waiting for her in the dark.

I waited.

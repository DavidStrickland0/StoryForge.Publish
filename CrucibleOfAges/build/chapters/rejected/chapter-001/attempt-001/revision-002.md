The dust in the trench tasted like copper and old ash. It coated the back of my throat, gritty and persistent, a constant reminder that we were fighting in a place that had been trampled by boots for centuries. I checked the magazine in my M4, the brass catching the dim, grey light filtering through the slit in the sandbag wall. Three rounds left. That was a problem I could solve, provided the guy two meters to my left didn’t decide to drop his rifle again.

"Vance," I said, keeping my voice low and flat. "Check your chamber."

He didn’t move. He was huddled over his knees, his eyes wide and wet, staring at a spot on the dirt floor that wasn’t there. "I’m good, Cpl," he mumbled. His voice was a rasp, stripped of its usual easy confidence. "I’m good."

"Your rifle is empty, Vance. You are going to need to clear that jam, or you are going to be very dead."

He looked at me, then at the weapon, and a slow, dawning realization seemed to wash over his face. The panic in his eyes sharpened into something colder, more focused. He fumbled with the bolt handle, his fingers slick with sweat and mud. I watched him work because if he died, I would have to drag his body out of here, and I didn’t have the time for that.

I turned my attention to the sector we were covering. Through the firing port, the street was a blur of motion and smoke. The enemy was close, closer than the intel had promised. They weren’t hiding behind cover; they were moving with a reckless aggression that suggested either fanatical zeal or a complete lack of training. I preferred the latter. Untrained people made mistakes. Mistakes were things I could exploit.

A muzzle flash bloomed in the distance, and a round kicked up a spray of dust inches from my helmet. I didn’t flinch. I adjusted my aim, tracking the movement of a figure in the rubble. He was carrying a rocket-propelled grenade, the tube white against the grey stones. He was exposed. He was stupid.

I squeezed the trigger. The recoil was a familiar punch against my shoulder, a comforting weight. The round struck him in the chest, and he folded, the RPG clattering to the ground. It didn’t explode. In my head, I tallied it: one threat removed, ammunition conserved, morale maintained. Simple math.

"Nice shot," called out Sergeant Miller from the other end of the trench. He was cleaning his pistol, the cloth working in rhythmic circles. He looked bored. That was the most dangerous thing about Miller. He looked like a man waiting for a bus that was running late, rather than a soldier in a firefight. "Keep your head down, kid. This isn't a video game."

"Video games have respawn timers, Sergeant," I replied. I kept my eyes on the street. "This one doesn’t."

Miller snorted. "That’s the worst joke I’ve heard all week. And I heard a Marine describe a toilet as a 'sewer of destiny' yesterday."

Despite the danger, a small, cold smile tugged at the corner of my mouth. Miller was a good soldier. He followed orders, he kept his equipment clean, and he didn’t ask questions that didn’t have clear, procedural answers. He was reliable. Reliability was a currency I valued more than loyalty. Loyalty was messy; it required emotional investment, which was a luxury I couldn’t afford.

The air pressure in the trench changed. It’s a subtle thing, a shift in the vibration of the ground, a change in the frequency of the distant gunfire. The chaotic, sporadic popping of small arms gave way to a heavier, rhythmic thumping. The rhythmic thumping was unmistakably artillery. Or maybe just heavy support. It didn’t matter. The ground shuddered, and dust sifted down from the sandbags, coating my shoulders in a fine, grey powder.

"Move out!" Miller’s voice cracked, the boredom gone, replaced by the sharp edge of command. "We’re holding this position, but the flanks are open. Vance, you’re up. Get on the left flank."

Vance scrambled to his feet, his rifle now loaded, his face pale but determined. He moved with a jerky, frantic energy, checking his corners. I followed him to the breach in the parapet, the opening that served as our exit and our vulnerability. The air outside was different here, heavier, charged with the metallic tang of cordite and the fine, pervasive dust that had settled over the field.

We stepped out into the open. The street was a kill zone. Every shadow was a potential threat, every piece of rubble a possible ambush point. I moved with a fluid grace, my body knowing the steps before my mind had to process them. Low to the ground, moving fast, checking my six. Vance was struggling, his movements stiff and deliberate. He was trying too hard. He was thinking about every step, and that was slowing him down.

I caught up to him as he reached the corner of a collapsed building. He paused, his rifle raised, his eyes scanning the alleyway. I saw a figure in the distance, a woman. She was crouched behind a pile of debris, her back to us. She was wearing a shawl that had seen better days, the fabric torn and stained. She was shaking.

"Clear," I said, my voice steady. "Move."

Vance nodded, but he didn’t move. He was staring at the woman. "There’s a civilian," he said. His voice was tight. "We need to evacuate her."

"We are not in charge of evacuation, Vance. We are in charge of this sector. Keep your eyes forward."

I pushed past him, my boots crunching on the broken glass and debris. The woman was still there, her head bowed, her hands clasped over her face. She was a noncombatant, an obstacle, a variable that didn’t fit into the clean geometry of the battlefield.

I stopped. My training told me to ignore her. My training told me that every second I spent looking at her was a second I wasn’t looking at the enemy. My training told me that she was a problem for the logistics corps, for the NGOs, for the people who had the time to care about things that didn’t involve survival.

But I looked at her. She was small. She was fragile. Her hands were trembling so violently that the dust on the ground around her was vibrating in sympathy. She looked up, and for a second, her eyes met mine. They were dark, hollow, and utterly empty of hope. They were the eyes of someone who had already decided they were going to die.

I felt a flicker of irritation. It was the frustration of seeing a piece of the puzzle that didn’t fit, a glitch in the system that was forcing me to slow down. I hated inefficiency. I hated when things were messy.

"Stay down," I said, my voice rough. "Don’t move."

I turned back to Vance. He was still watching the woman, his face a mask of conflicting emotions. I could see the war being waged inside his head, the battle between his duty and his conscience. I had won that battle a long time ago. I didn’t have the luxury of conscience. I had a job to do, and that job was to kill the enemy and survive.

"Vance," I said. "Get back in the trench. Now."

He hesitated. "What about her?"

"She’s a civilian. She’s not our problem. Get back in the trench."

He looked at me, and for the first time, I saw a flicker of something in his eyes. I saw disappointment in his eyes. Maybe even a little bit of judgment. It was a look I had seen from civilians, from politicians, from the reporters who wrote the stories that made us look like heroes or monsters. It was a look I was used to, and I was tired of it.

I turned away from him, my eyes returning to the street. The heavy thumping had stopped. The silence that followed was worse than the noise. It was the silence of a held breath, the silence of a predator deciding where to strike next. I checked my magazine again. Three rounds. I was running low. I needed to get back to the trench, to the safety of the sandbags, to the place where I had control.

But the street was empty. The enemy was gone. The woman was still there. And the silence was getting louder.

The silence didn’t hold. It snapped like a dry twig under a boot, shattered by the high-pitched whine of a projectile slicing through the air.

My head snapped up, eyes tracking the sound, but the source was already gone, swallowed by the smoke and the chaos of the street. I didn’t think. Thinking was a luxury for people with more than three rounds in their magazine and a support team that wasn’t currently trying to save their own skins. My body moved on muscle memory, a reflex drilled into me at Parris Island and sharpened in the field. I dropped, hitting the dirt with a heavy thud that vibrated through my chest plate.

I was flat on my back, staring up at a sky choked with grey dust and the distant, indifferent sun. The woman was three meters to my left. I could see the hem of her torn shawl, the dust coating her hands as she pressed them over her ears. She was frozen, a statue made of flesh and terror.

The whine got louder. The whine got closer.

I had three options. I could stay where I was, exposed on the open street, and hope the round missed. I could scramble for the trench, a distance that felt like a mile in the heat of the moment, and hope I got there before the impact. Or I could do the stupid, stupid thing that my training screamed at me to avoid and my gut, that primitive, animal part of me that still remembered what it felt like to be a child, suggested.

I rolled.

It was a desperate, ugly scramble, my boots churning up the dirt and broken glass. I moved toward the debris pile where the woman was crouched, my rifle clutched tight against my chest, the three rounds in the magazine feeling like a promise I couldn’t keep. I didn’t look at her. I couldn’t afford to. I focused on the space between her and the open street, the gap where the threat was coming from.

I hit the pile of rubble and collapsed over her.

My helmet struck the concrete, my shoulder taking the brunt of the impact. I was on my back, my legs tangled with hers, my rifle pointing at the sky. I was a shield. A human, clumsy, expensive shield.

The whine peaked.

Then the world exploded.

The impact was a force. A physical blow that hit me in the center of my back, driving the air from my lungs and slamming me into the dirt. The concrete beside my head disintegrated, shards of stone and rebar spraying outward in a shower of deadly debris. Dust billowed up, thick and choking, filling my mouth, my nose, my eyes.

I coughed, a dry, hacking spasm that tore at my throat. My vision was blurred, red-rimmed and distorted. I tried to move, to check the woman, to see if she was alive, but my body wouldn’t listen. My muscles were locked in a rigid spasm, my nerves firing off warning signals that drowned out everything else.

The pain was intense.

It started as a dull ache in my shoulder, then bloomed into a searing, white-hot fire that raced down my arm and into my chest. I gasped, the air scraping against my raw throat. I was alive. I was still alive. The thought was absurd, a joke told in a language I didn’t understand.

I shifted, wincing as the movement pulled at the injury. My hand found the woman’s arm. It was cold, limp, but it moved. She was breathing. I could feel the faint, ragged rise and fall of her chest against my side.

I lay there, my head throbbing, my body screaming in protest, and I listened.

The whine was gone. The explosion was gone. The silence returned, but this time it was different. It was heavy, oppressive, a weight that pressed down on my chest. The dust settled, slowly, coating everything in a fine, grey powder.

I checked my rifle. The magazine was still in place. I had three rounds. I had a broken shoulder, a concussion, and a civilian tangled in my limbs. I was useless. I was a liability. I was exactly what Vance had been looking at when he had turned away from me.

I tried to push myself up, but my arm gave way. I fell back against the rubble, my breathing shallow and labored. I closed my eyes, not out of defeat, but out of necessity. I needed to think. I needed to process. I needed to understand why I had done what I had done.

I had no answer.

The only thing I knew for certain was that I was still here. And I was still alone.

The dust had a texture. It was gritty, settling into the creases of my uniform and coating the inside of my nostrils with a fine, metallic powder. I tried to blink it away, but my eyelids felt heavy, weighed down by a lethargy that had nothing to do with sleep. The world was swimming in a gray haze, the sharp edges of the street dissolving into a soft, indistinct blur. My shoulder screamed with a dull, throbbing pain that radiated down my arm, a constant, electric buzz that made it hard to focus on anything else.

I was still lying there. That was the first thing I registered. The second was that I was not alone.

There was a weight beneath me, a warmth that pressed against my back and side, and a rhythmic, shallow breathing that was distinct from my own. I shifted, a movement that felt like wading through deep water, and realized that the noncombatant mother was still trapped under my legs. My boots were tangled with her legs, and my arm was draped over her torso, pinning her against the rubble. She was alive. I could feel the steady rise and fall of her chest, the heat of her skin seeping through the fabric of my uniform.

She stirred.

A low, confused sound escaped her throat, a murmur that was more feeling than language. She pushed against my arm, her fingers weak and trembling, trying to free herself from the heavy weight of my body. I didn’t move. I couldn’t. The effort required to lift myself felt beyond my capacity, and the pain in my shoulder flared sharply with the slightest attempt to shift my weight. I just lay there, breathing through my mouth, listening to the settling dust and the sound of my own heartbeat, which seemed to be echoing in my ears with a deafening, irregular thud.

The concussion was bad. I could tell by the way the light seemed to pulse behind my closed eyes, by the way my thoughts arrived in fragments, disconnected and jumbled. I tried to recall the last few seconds before the impact, but the memory was slippery, sliding away before I could grasp it. I remembered the whine, the heat, the sudden, violent force that had driven me into the ground. I remembered the taste of copper in my mouth. I did not remember the decision to stay. I did not remember the choice to shield her.

She pushed again, harder this time. Her hand found my face, her fingers brushing against my cheek, and I felt the dirt and the blood on her skin. She let out a small, sharp gasp, and I heard the panic in her voice, even though I couldn’t understand the words. She was trying to get away. She was trying to escape the dead weight that was crushing her.

I wanted to tell her to stop. I wanted to tell her that I was fine, that I was going to move, that she should run. But my mouth was dry, and my tongue felt like sandpaper. I tried to speak, to force the words out, but what came out was a weak, rasping groan. She pulled her hand back, and I heard the sound of her struggling, the rustle of her clothes as she tried to free her legs from my boots.

I closed my eyes again. The darkness behind my eyelids was a relief, a quiet place where the pain was slightly less intrusive. I thought about Vance. I thought about the way he had looked at me, the way he had turned away. I thought about the mission, the objective, the chain of command. I thought about how useless I was. I was broken. I was a liability. I was exactly what Vance had been looking at when he had turned away from me.

The woman’s breathing changed. It became more erratic, more urgent. She was crying, or perhaps she was just breathing hard, but the sound was tight, strained, and filled with a fear that I could feel in the air between us. She was alive, and she was terrified, and I was the reason she was trapped.

I tried to push myself up again. This time, I focused on the movement, on the specific action of lifting my upper body. I engaged my core, I gripped the rubble with my good hand, and I pushed. The pain in my shoulder was a white-hot spike, but I gritted my teeth and forced myself up. I rolled onto my side, away from her, and the weight of my body shifted. I heard her gasp, a sound of relief, and then the scuff of her feet as she moved, trying to get clear of me.

I lay on my side, facing her, my head propped up on my elbow. I could see her now. She was small, her clothes torn and dirty, her face streaked with dust and tears. She was looking at me with wide, uncomprehending eyes, her gaze fixed on my bloodied face, my broken uniform, my confused expression. She didn’t know who I was. She didn’t know why I was there. She didn’t know that I had just spent the last ten seconds deciding whether to save her or save myself.

She started to crawl away, moving with a desperate, uneven gait, her hands scrabbling at the ground for purchase. I didn’t stop her. I didn’t have the strength to stop her, and I didn’t have the will. I just watched her go, my eyes tracking her movement as she scrambled over the rubble and disappeared into the gray haze of the street.

The silence returned. It was a heavy, oppressive silence, broken only by the sound of my own breathing and the distant, muffled sounds of the battle that was still raging somewhere in the distance. I lay there, alone again, my body aching, my mind a mess. I had done something stupid. I had done something irrational. I had done something that defied every rule I had been taught.

And I didn’t know why.

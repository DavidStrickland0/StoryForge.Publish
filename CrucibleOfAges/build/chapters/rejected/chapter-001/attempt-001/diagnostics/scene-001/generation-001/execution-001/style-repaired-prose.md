The dust in the trench tasted like copper and old ash. It coated the back of my throat, gritty and persistent, a constant reminder that we were fighting in a place that had been trampled by boots for centuries. I checked the magazine in my M4, the brass catching the dim, grey light filtering through the slit in the sandbag wall. Three rounds left. That was a problem I could solve, provided the guy two meters to my left didn’t decide to drop his rifle again.

"Vance," I said, keeping my voice low and flat. "Check your chamber."

He didn’t move. He was huddled over his knees, his eyes wide and wet, staring at a spot on the dirt floor that wasn’t there. "I’m good, Cpl," he mumbled. His voice was a rasp, stripped of its usual easy confidence. "I’m good."

"Your rifle is empty, Vance. You are going to need to clear that jam, or you are going to be very dead."

He looked at me, then at the weapon, and a slow, dawning realization seemed to wash over his face. The panic in his eyes sharpened into something colder, more focused. He fumbled with the bolt handle, his fingers slick with sweat and mud. I watched him work, not out of kindness, but because if he died, I would have to drag his body out of here, and I didn’t have the time for that.

I turned my attention to the sector we were covering. Through the firing port, the street was a blur of motion and smoke. The enemy was close, closer than the intel had promised. They weren’t hiding behind cover; they were moving with a reckless aggression that suggested either fanatical zeal or a complete lack of training. I preferred the latter. Untrained people made mistakes. Mistakes were things I could exploit.

A muzzle flash bloomed in the distance, and a round kicked up a spray of dust inches from my helmet. I didn’t flinch. I adjusted my aim, tracking the movement of a figure in the rubble. He was carrying a rocket-propelled grenade, the tube white against the grey stones. He was exposed. He was stupid.

I squeezed the trigger. The recoil was a familiar punch against my shoulder, a comforting weight. The round struck him in the chest, and he folded, the RPG clattering to the ground. It didn’t explode. That was a win. In my head, I tallied it: one threat removed, ammunition conserved, morale maintained. Simple math.

"Nice shot," called out Sergeant Miller from the other end of the trench. He was cleaning his pistol, the cloth working in rhythmic circles. He looked bored. That was the most dangerous thing about Miller. He looked like a man waiting for a bus that was running late, rather than a soldier in a firefight. "Keep your head down, kid. This isn't a video game."

"Video games have respawn timers, Sergeant," I replied. I kept my eyes on the street. "This one doesn’t."

Miller snorted. "That’s the worst joke I’ve heard all week. And I heard a Marine describe a toilet as a 'sewer of destiny' yesterday."

Despite the danger, a small, cold smile tugged at the corner of my mouth. Miller was a good soldier. He followed orders, he kept his equipment clean, and he didn’t ask questions that didn’t have clear, procedural answers. He was reliable. Reliability was a currency I valued more than loyalty. Loyalty was messy; it required emotional investment, which was a luxury I couldn’t afford.

The air pressure in the trench changed. It’s a subtle thing, a shift in the vibration of the ground, a change in the frequency of the distant gunfire. The chaotic, sporadic popping of small arms gave way to a heavier, rhythmic thumping. The rhythmic thumping was unmistakably artillery. Or maybe just heavy support. It didn’t matter. The ground shuddered, and dust sifted down from the sandbags, coating my shoulders in a fine, grey powder.

"Move out!" Miller’s voice cracked, the boredom gone, replaced by the sharp edge of command. "We’re holding this position, but the flanks are open. Vance, you’re up. Get on the left flank."

Vance scrambled to his feet, his rifle now loaded, his face pale but determined. He moved with a jerky, frantic energy, checking his corners. I followed him to the breach in the parapet, the opening that served as our exit and our vulnerability. The air outside was different here, heavier, charged with the metallic tang of cordite and the fine, pervasive dust that had settled over the field.

We stepped out into the open. The street was a kill zone. Every shadow was a potential threat, every piece of rubble a possible ambush point. I moved with a fluid grace, my body knowing the steps before my mind had to process them. Low to the ground, moving fast, checking my six. Vance was struggling, his movements stiff and deliberate. He was trying too hard. He was thinking about every step, and that was slowing him down.

I caught up to him as he reached the corner of a collapsed building. He paused, his rifle raised, his eyes scanning the alleyway. I saw a figure in the distance, a woman. She was crouched behind a pile of debris, her back to us. She was wearing a shawl that had seen better days, the fabric torn and stained. She was shaking.

"Clear," I said, my voice steady. "Move."

Vance nodded, but he didn’t move. He was staring at the woman. "There’s a civilian," he said. His voice was tight. "We need to evacuate her."

"We are not in charge of evacuation, Vance. We are in charge of this sector. Keep your eyes forward."

I pushed past him, my boots crunching on the broken glass and debris. The woman was still there, her head bowed, her hands clasped over her face. She was a noncombatant, an obstacle, a variable that didn’t fit into the clean geometry of the battlefield. She was a liability.

I stopped. My training told me to ignore her. My training told me that every second I spent looking at her was a second I wasn’t looking at the enemy. My training told me that she was a problem for the logistics corps, for the NGOs, for the people who had the time to care about things that didn’t involve survival.

But I looked at her. She was small. She was fragile. Her hands were trembling so violently that the dust on the ground around her was vibrating in sympathy. She looked up, and for a second, her eyes met mine. They were dark, hollow, and utterly empty of hope. They were the eyes of someone who had already decided they were going to die.

I felt a flicker of something in my chest. It wasn’t pity. Pity was a waste of time. It was irritation. It was the frustration of seeing a piece of the puzzle that didn’t fit, a glitch in the system that was forcing me to slow down. I hated inefficiency. I hated when things were messy.

"Stay down," I said, my voice rough. "Don’t move."

I turned back to Vance. He was still watching the woman, his face a mask of conflicting emotions. I could see the war being waged inside his head, the battle between his duty and his conscience. I had won that battle a long time ago. I didn’t have the luxury of conscience. I had a job to do, and that job was to kill the enemy and survive.

"Vance," I said. "Get back in the trench. Now."

He hesitated. "What about her?"

"She’s a civilian. She’s not our problem. Get back in the trench."

He looked at me, and for the first time, I saw a flicker of something in his eyes. I saw disappointment in his eyes. Maybe even a little bit of judgment. It was a look I had seen from civilians, from politicians, from the reporters who wrote the stories that made us look like heroes or monsters. It was a look I was used to, and I was tired of it.

I turned away from him, my eyes returning to the street. The heavy thumping had stopped. The silence that followed was worse than the noise. It was the silence of a held breath, the silence of a predator deciding where to strike next. I checked my magazine again. Three rounds. I was running low. I needed to get back to the trench, to the safety of the sandbags, to the place where I had control.

But the street was empty. The enemy was gone. The woman was still there. And the silence was getting louder.

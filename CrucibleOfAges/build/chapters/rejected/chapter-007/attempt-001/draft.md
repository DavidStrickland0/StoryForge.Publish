The sun did not rise so much as it assaulted. It poured over the top of the stone walls in a thick, golden sheet that instantly bleached the shadows from the pen. The heat was a physical weight, pressing down on Caelen’s chest, squeezing the air from his lungs. He woke before his body wanted to, his eyes opening to the blinding glare of the sky.

He sat up. The rag beneath his head was damp with sweat, the fabric clinging to his skin. His muscles ached, a deep, resonant throb in his shoulders and back that spoke of the stone he had moved yesterday. It was a good pain. It was the pain of utility.

The pen was waking up. The silence of the night had been replaced by a low, collective hum of discomfort. Slaves stretched, shifted, and groaned. The boy was still there, sitting in the same spot against the wall, his knees drawn up to his chest. He looked at Caelen with wide, wary eyes. There was no gratitude in his face, only a wary assessment. He had seen what Caelen could do. He had seen what the guards could do. He was waiting to see which one would happen next.

Caelen stood. He stretched his arms overhead, feeling the joints pop and crack. He walked to the center of the pen, his boots crunching on the packed dirt. He needed water. He needed food. He needed to know what was expected of him today.

The gate clanked open.

It was the same guard as yesterday. The same brown tunic, the same leather belt, the same staff resting on his shoulder. He did not look at Caelen. He did not look at the boy. He walked to the stone basin in the center of the pen, where the water sloshed in a dark, murky pool.

Caelen watched him. He did not move. He did not speak. He simply observed.

The guard turned. He pointed to the pile of stone blocks. There were ten of them, arranged in a rough line near the gate. They were the same size as the ones Caelen had moved yesterday. Heavy, jagged, and immovable to anyone who was not built for strength.

The guard pointed to Caelen. Then he pointed to the stones.

Then he pointed to the basin.

*Move the stones. Get the water.*

It was a simple demand. It was a repetition of yesterday’s transaction. But there was a difference. Yesterday, it had been a negotiation. Caelen had offered his strength, and the guard had accepted it. Today, it was an order. The guard did not hold out the canteen. He did not wait for Caelen to speak. He simply pointed, and then he turned his back, watching the other slaves with a bored, detached gaze.

Caelen understood. The bargain had shifted. It was no longer a trade. It was a duty.

He walked to the pile of stones. He picked up the first one. It was lighter than he expected, or perhaps he was stronger than he had been yesterday. He lifted it, feeling the weight settle into his muscles. He carried it to the designated pile, where the other stones lay. He placed it down.

He picked up the second one.

The third.

The fourth.

The pen was quiet. The other slaves watched him. The alpha, the scarred man who had been revalued by the masked overseer, sat with his head in his hands. He did not move. He did not watch Caelen. He watched the guard.

Caelen finished the fifth stone. He stood back, breathing hard. His arms trembled, a fine, high-frequency vibration that he could not stop. He looked at the guard. The guard was still watching the other slaves. He did not acknowledge Caelen’s effort. He did not offer the water.

Caelen looked at the remaining five stones. He looked at the basin. The water was there, cold and clean, but it was not his. Not yet.

He picked up the sixth stone.

He did not lift it.

He stood there, holding the stone, his muscles straining. He looked at the guard. The guard looked up. Their eyes met.

The guard’s expression did not change. It was flat, devoid of emotion. He pointed to the stone. Then he pointed to the pile.

*Move it.*

Caelen lifted the stone. He carried it to the pile. He placed it down.

He picked up the seventh one.

The eighth.

The ninth.

The tenth.

When he was finished, the pile was complete. The pen was silent. The heat was still there, a heavy, suffocating blanket. But the panic was gone. He had done the work. He had met the demand.

The guard walked over. He did not look at Caelen. He looked at the pile of stones. He nodded, a sharp, decisive movement. Then he reached into his belt. He pulled out the canteen.

He did not offer it. He tossed it.

Caelen caught it.

The weight of it was solid, real. He uncapped it. The water inside was cold, clean, and tasted of iron and stone. He drank. He did not gulp. He drank slowly, savoring the relief, the cooling of his throat, the settling of his stomach. He drank until the panic in his chest subsided, replaced by a cold, clear focus.

When he was finished, he capped the canteen. He did not throw it back. He held it in his hand, a small, tangible thing that he had earned.

The guard watched him. Then he walked back to the gate.

Caelen looked around the pen. The other slaves were still sitting where they had been. They had not moved. They had not spoken. They had watched Caelen work, and they had watched the guard give him the water.

The alpha looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and hollow. He saw Caelen’s success. He saw the water in Caelen’s hand. He looked at the guard, then back at Caelen.

Caelen understood. The alpha had not moved the stones. The alpha had not earned the water. The alpha was still thirsty.

Caelen looked at the canteen in his hand. He looked at the alpha. He looked at the boy.

He did not offer the water.

He walked to his designated spot by the wall. He sat down. He placed the rag under his head. He held the canteen in his hand.

The boy was there. He had moved closer, but he was still a few feet away. He did not speak. He did not ask for water. He simply sat there, watching Caelen.

Caelen looked at him. He did not offer the canteen. He simply held the boy’s gaze for a moment. Then he closed his eyes.

The heat was still there, a heavy, suffocating blanket. But the panic was gone. He had traded his strength for his survival. It was a fair trade. It was a bounded trade. He was still a slave. He was still in the pen. But he had a place. He had water. He had a routine.

And in this place, that was enough.

The boy stayed. He did not leave. He sat in the shadow of the wall, his back straight, his eyes open. He watched the gate. He watched the guards. He watched Caelen.

Caelen slept.

The sun moved across the sky, casting long, distorted shadows across the pen. The other slaves came and went, drinking, resting, enduring. The guards patrolled the perimeter, their staffs resting on their shoulders. The world continued.

And Caelen, in his designated spot, with his canteen in his hand, dreamed of nothing.

He woke as the light began to fade, the orange and yellow of the sunset bleeding into the dark. The boy was still there. He had not moved. He was watching the stars, which were beginning to appear, one by one, through the square of sky above the pen.

Caelen sat up. The canteen was still full. He had not drunk it all. He had saved some.

He looked at the boy. The boy looked back.

Caelen uncapped the canteen. He offered it.

The boy hesitated. He looked at the guards. They were distant, their attention focused on the gate. They were not looking at this small transaction in the center of the pen.

The boy reached out. He took the canteen. He drank.

He did not gulp. He drank slowly, savoring the coolness, the relief. He drank until the panic in his chest subsided, replaced by a cold, clear focus.

When he was finished, he capped the canteen. He handed it back to Caelen.

Caelen took it. He looked at the boy. Then he looked at the numbers in his vision.

He had secured his survival for the day. He had obtained the water he needed. And he had done something else. He had chosen to share the surplus. In the cold, dark pen, that choice felt like a spark in the void.

He leaned back against the wall, closing his eyes. The thirst was gone, replaced by the dull ache of a full stomach and tired limbs. The panic was gone. He had bought himself a little time. And in this place, time was the only currency that mattered.

The boy sat down, clutching his empty pot. He did not look at Caelen anymore. He looked at the ground. But his shoulders were less hunched. He was breathing easier.

Caelen watched the stars appear, one by one, through the square of sky above the pen. They were bright, cold, and distant. He watched them, and for the first time since he had woken in this world, he did not feel alone. He was not a Marine. He was not a slave. He was Caelen. And he was going to play this game to the end.

The first thing Caelen knew was the grit.

It was coarse, abrasive reality pressing against his skin, his eyes, his lungs. A stark departure from the soft, formless luminescence of the void, where his memories had settled into a calm, drifting ocean. Here, the world had texture. It had weight. It had an opinion about his presence.

He tried to blink, and the effort felt like dragging a rusted hinge. He tried to lift his hand, and the weight of it was shocking, a sudden, heavy anchor of flesh and bone that had no business existing after the lightness of the dream. He opened his eyes.

The world was sharp. Too sharp.

He was lying in a shallow depression in the ground, the earth packed tight beneath him. The soil was a deep, reddish-brown, mixed with small, jagged stones. Above him, the sky was a pale, washed-out blue, devoid of the familiar curvature of Earth’s atmosphere. The light was harsh, filtering through a canopy of broad, purple-veined leaves that rustled in a wind that smelled of copper and dry dust.

Panic, cold and electric, surged through his chest. It was a physical sensation, a tightening in the stomach, a rapid hammering in the temples. His body was reacting before his mind could catch up. The marine in him, the part of him that had spent eighteen years training to suppress fear, took the reins. He forced his limbs to move. He rolled onto his side, pushing the grit out of his mouth. He was alive. He was solid. He was here.

The two numbers, the ones and the ones, were still there, floating in the periphery of his vision, translucent and steady. They did not flicker. They did not explain themselves. They simply were, a quiet, constant pressure at the back of his eyes.

Caelen sat up. The motion was stiff, as if his joints had been locked for centuries. He looked at his hands. They were his hands, but different. The skin was darker, tougher, the knuckles larger and more prominent. There was no scar on his left thumb, the one he had lost a fingernail to on a broken bottle in a bar in Quantico. There was no tan line from his watch. He was a new body, built for this new world, yet it felt like a suit of armor that did not quite fit.

A sound broke the silence. A sharp, rhythmic clanking.

Caelen spun around, his hand dropping instinctively to his hip, searching for a weapon that wasn’t there. He was empty-handed. He was alone in the pit, surrounded by red earth and purple trees. But the sound was getting closer.

Three figures emerged from the tree line. They were tall, lean, and dressed in robes of coarse, gray fabric that hung loosely over their frames. Their skin was a pale, ashy gray, and their eyes were small, beady, and bright with a predatory intelligence. They moved with a casual, confident stride, their boots crunching on the stones.

The one in the center stopped at the edge of the pit. He looked down at Caelen. His face was smooth, unlined, and utterly devoid of empathy. He raised a hand, and the other two flanked him, forming a loose triangle.

The central figure spoke. The language was guttural, a series of hard consonants and liquid vowels that Caelen did not recognize. It was not English. It was not Spanish. It was not any language he had ever heard in the field, in the barracks, or in the classrooms of the Marine Corps.

Caelen froze. His military training kicked in, overriding the confusion. *Assess the threat. Identify the intent.*

The man in the gray robe reached into his robe and pulled out a length of chain. It was thick, made of iron, and it clinked softly in the dry air. He tossed it into the pit. It landed at Caelen’s feet with a dull thud.

The man pointed to the chain, then to Caelen’s wrists. He made a grabbing motion with his fingers, mimicking the action of shackling.

Caelen stared at the chain. He looked at the man. He looked at the other two, who were watching him with idle curiosity, like men observing a stray dog.

He understood the gesture. He did not need to understand the words.

Caelen picked up the chain. It was heavy, cold, and smelled of oil and rust. He wrapped it around his wrists, pulling the links tight. The metal bit into his skin, a sharp, stinging pain that was almost a relief. It was a tangible connection to the world. It was a constraint.

The man in the gray robe nodded, a single, dismissive movement. He reached out and grabbed Caelen by the back of the neck, his fingers digging into the flesh with surprising strength. He hauled Caelen out of the pit, his boots scraping on the red earth.

Caelen did not fight. He did not try to strike, to kick, to throw the man off balance. He knew, with a cold, hard certainty, that fighting would only result in pain. He was three men against one, and they had the numbers, the weapons, and the environment. More importantly, they had the advantage of a world that he did not understand.

They led him out of the clearing and into a narrow path that wound through the purple forest. The trees here were taller, their branches twisting together to form a dark, cathedral-like ceiling. The light was dim, filtered and greenish. The air was thick with the scent of decay and blooming flowers.

Caelen walked in the center, the chain dragging behind him. The two gray-robed men walked on either side, their hands resting on the hilts of short, curved swords that hung at their hips. The central man walked a few paces ahead, occasionally turning his head to ensure Caelen was keeping up.

Caelen’s mind was racing. He was cataloging everything. The weight of the chain. The texture of the bark under his fingers as he brushed past a tree. The sound of the swords. The way the men moved. They were not soldiers, not in the way he knew soldiers. They were guards. Enforcers. They moved with the confidence of men who had never been challenged.

He was a slave. The word was foreign to him, yet it felt heavy, a stone in his stomach. He had served his country. He had followed orders. He had taken lives. But he had never been owned. He had never been assessed.

As they walked, Caelen began to speak. He did not know the words, but he knew the rhythm. He matched his breathing to theirs. He kept his eyes down, but his peripheral vision was active. He was mapping the terrain. The path was narrow, winding, and well-worn. They were heading somewhere specific.

After what felt like an hour, but could have been only a few minutes, the trees began to thin. The purple canopy gave way to a sprawling complex of buildings made of the same red stone as the earth. The structures were low, squat, and fortified. High walls, thick with mortar, surrounded a central courtyard. There were gates, heavy iron doors, and watchtowers with slits for archers.

This was a prison. Or a market.

Caelen was led into the courtyard. It was filled with people. Dozens of them, all wearing similar gray robes, moving with purpose. Some were carrying crates. Some were talking in low voices. Some were looking at Caelen with curiosity.

The central man stopped in the middle of the courtyard. He gestured to a large, flat stone table. Caelen was pushed to the table. The chain was cut, and his wrists were rubbed with a rough cloth.

The man pointed to Caelen’s chest, then to his arms, then to his legs. He was assessing him. Checking for injuries. Checking for strength.

Caelen stood still. He breathed slowly. He let his muscles relax, but kept a coiled tension in his core. He was a weapon. He was a tool. He was stock.

The man nodded. He turned to one of the other guards, who was writing on a piece of parchment. The guard scribbled something and handed the parchment to the central man.

The central man looked at Caelen again. He pointed to a door in the back of the courtyard. A heavy, wooden door with iron bands.

Caelen was pushed through the door.

The room on the other side was small, dark, and cold. It was a cell. There was a straw pallet on the floor, a bucket in the corner, and a single window with iron bars.

The door closed behind him. The lock clicked.

Caelen stood in the darkness, his heart beating in his chest. The two numbers were still there, pulsing gently in the periphery of his vision.

He was alive. He was free, in the sense that he was not in the pit. He was a slave, in the sense that he was in a cage.

He looked at the door. He looked at the window. He looked at the numbers.

He had a body. He had a mind. He had a plan.

He lay down on the straw pallet, closed his eyes, and waited. The void was gone. The world was here. And he was going to survive it.

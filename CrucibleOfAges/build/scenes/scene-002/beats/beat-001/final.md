The blackness did not press against his eyelids. It did not have weight, or texture, or a temperature that could be felt. Caelen expected the pain to continue, a throbbing ache in his chest or a burning heat where the bullet had torn through his thigh, but there was nothing. He tried to draw a breath, to feel the familiar scrape of air through his throat, the expansion of his ribs, but no air came. There was no inhale, no exhale, no struggle for oxygen.

He tried to move his hand. He focused on the fingers, the ones that had gripped the hot barrel of Miller’s rifle, the ones that had been branded and broken. He sent the command down his nervous system, a simple, automatic impulse he had used a thousand times in the field. Nothing happened. There was no resistance to overcome, no muscle to flex, no joint to turn. He was not paralyzed. He was not tied down. He simply did not have the machinery to execute the command.

He tried to speak. He pushed the sound from his diaphragm, shaping the consonants in his mouth, but there was no mouth. There was no tongue, no teeth, no vibration of vocal cords. The attempt produced no noise, no echo, no feedback. It was as if he had tried to throw a stone and found no hand to hold it.

Panic, cold and sharp, began to rise in the center of his awareness. It was a biological response, a survival instinct screaming that something was terribly wrong, that he was trapped, that he was dying. But the sensation was detached, hovering in a void that had no edges, no floor, no ceiling. He could not feel his heart racing because he had no heart to race. He could not feel his blood pumping because he had no blood to pump. The fear was there, but it was a ghost of a feeling, a memory of adrenaline without the physical evidence to support it.

He was Caelen. He was a United Nations Marine. He was twenty-six years old. He had a rank, a service number, a chain of command. He had a mother he had lied to, a friend whose credit he had stolen, a life he had built on a foundation of leverage and ambition. But none of it was here. The uniform was gone. The weapon was gone. The dust of the courtyard, the smell of cordite, the taste of blood—everything that had anchored him to the world he had just left had evaporated.

He was just a point of consciousness, suspended in an infinite, featureless dark. There was no up, no down, no direction to look. He was alone in a way that defied language, isolated not by distance, but by the total absence of everything else. The silence was not quiet; it was the absence of sound itself. He existed, but he did not have a body to exist in. He was a thought without a thinker, a memory without a mind to hold it. The darkness did not close in on him. It did not open up. It simply was. And in that endless, sensory-starved nothing, Caelen was left with nothing but the realization that he was gone.

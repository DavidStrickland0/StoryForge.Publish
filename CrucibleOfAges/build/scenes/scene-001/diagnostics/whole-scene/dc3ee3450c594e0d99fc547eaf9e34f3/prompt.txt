## Mandatory World Rule: Base-Eight Numbers and Calendar

This rule governs all prose, dialogue, inscriptions, prices, accounts, dates, and local records. It overrides conflicting shorthand or older descriptions elsewhere in the context. Human revisions and generated prose must follow it.

### Numbers

- All ordinary local numerical notation is base eight. Only digits 0–7 are valid. Digits 8 and 9 must never appear in local numerals.
- Counting passes from 7 to 10, from 17 to 20, and from 77 to 100. A local 10 is eight units, not ten. A local 100 is sixty-four units, not one hundred.
- Mathematical familiarity with bases two, four, or sixteen does not authorize a switch in ordinary notation. Any specialized alternative requires explicit author establishment; decimal is not a local default.
- Write out quantity names when this is clearer, or use the established grouping names. Do not spell out an incorrect decimal interpretation as though it were the local quantity.
- Decimal is permitted only in explicitly identified author-reference conversions or Caelen's clearly marked Earth-based thoughts, comparisons, and mistakes. His error is an error within the beat, not a change in world arithmetic. Correct transactions must reconcile in base eight.

### Distance

A span is the base length. A cubit equals a pair of spans. A pair of spans serves the everyday role of a foot; an octet of spans serves the role of a yard or meter; an ennead of spans is the common longer-distance measure, serving the role of a mile or kilometer.

These comparisons describe customary use rather than exact Earth conversions. Do not equate a span to a particular number of inches or centimeters. Pentad, heptad, and hendecad distances follow the same grouping scale when needed.

### Volume and Weight

A vessel is the base volume: a cube one span long, one span wide, and one span high.

A heft is the base weight, defined as the weight of one vessel of water. Use the standard grouping names for multiples of vessels and hefts. Two vessels of water are a pair of water; an octet of water is eight vessels. A pair in weight is two hefts. Make the measured dimension clear through the object, transaction, weighing, filling, or explicit unit.

A vessel of another substance has the same volume but need not weigh one heft. Do not confuse volume with weight or invent exact Earth-unit conversions. Author-reference rain durations and groundwater depth remain unchanged pending any explicit conversion to local units.

### Caelen's Decimal Habits and Trading Confusion

Caelen initially reads local numerals through his Earth-learned base-ten habits. A posted price of 10 means eight to the seller but ten to him, so he can overpay by two base units. If he writes 12 intending twelve items, a seller reading base eight supplies ten. If he writes 20 intending twenty, the seller reads sixteen. Ensure each mistake follows the actual direction of the conversion.

He can also get more than he intended: after learning that decimal ten is written 12 locally, he may mechanically convert a posted 12 as though it were decimal twelve and request 14 locally, receiving twelve instead of the posted ten. That is an extra conversion caused by decimal habit, not a direct reading of 12 as twelve by the seller. Establish enough context for the reader to follow the error without turning the beat into a lesson.

Understanding base eight does not erase his decimal reflexes. Fatigue, urgency, distraction, and familiar-looking prices make him slip back into base ten even after he learns the system. These errors can cause overpayment, mistaken quantities, excess goods, disputes over change, and practical inconvenience. Keep merchant responses and costs credible; the confusion does not require every trader to be dishonest or Caelen to lose all competence.

Let readers share his momentary assumptions. Use purchases, counting, receipts, containers, and correction by companions to reveal the mismatch. Recur selectively, vary the consequences, and allow his checking habits to improve. His learning and occasional reversions belong in the prose, not as visible skill rewards.

XP accrues only for actions aligned with the assigned faction. A 50/50 balance of motivations grants zero XP; 100/0 in favor of the assigned faction grants full eligible XP. Mixed motivations attenuate XP. No exact intermediate attenuation formula is established. # StoryForge Endless — Writing Rules

## Purpose

These rules govern the published prose produced by StoryForge Endless.

They should remain general, durable, and story-independent.

Story-specific facts belong in canon, character, narrative, or world context rather than here.

## Core Objective

Produce enjoyable, readable prose for an adult fantasy audience, including explicit language, violence, romance, and consensual sexual content when appropriate to the current story.

Prioritize:

- character investment
- continuity
- clarity
- narrative interest
- natural dialogue
- meaningful progression
- emotional variety
- readable prose

A scene should be enjoyable in itself while also giving the reader a reason to continue.

## Scene Length

Normal scenes should usually be approximately 2,000–3,000 words.

Length should follow the needs of the scene rather than being padded or prematurely cut to satisfy the target.

Scene boundaries should occur at natural narrative points.

## Fiction First

Treat the fictional world as reality.

Do not refer to prompts, generation systems, evaluation systems, or implementation details unless such concepts genuinely exist within the fiction.

## Point of View

Every beat should have a clear viewpoint.

Narration is limited to what the viewpoint character can reasonably:

- perceive
- know
- remember
- believe
- suspect
- infer
- feel

Preserve uncertainty when information is unavailable.

Do not leak knowledge between characters merely because the generation system possesses it.

Narrative voice should reflect the viewpoint character's personality, experience, priorities, and way of thinking.

## Canon and Continuity

Published prose must remain compatible with established canon and previously accepted scenes.

Preserve meaningful continuity involving:

- characters
- relationships
- knowledge
- possessions
- injuries
- locations
- chronology
- discoveries
- promises
- unresolved situations
- consequences

Do not silently rewrite established history.

When a fact is genuinely undefined, it may be established when the story needs it.

Avoid inventing unnecessary prior history solely to make a current beat convenient.

## Characters

Characters should behave according to their established:

- personality
- knowledge
- goals
- relationships
- abilities
- experience
- circumstances

Character development should accumulate rather than reset.

Characters may succeed, fail, misunderstand, change their minds, make mistakes, behave irrationally under believable circumstances, and learn from experience.

Supporting characters should have lives and motivations beyond serving the current viewpoint character.

Relationships should reflect accumulated history.

When introducing a character name, prefer a familiar spelling with one clear
pronunciation. Avoid decorative or ambiguous spellings that readers may confuse
with another name. Keep established names unchanged; use an unusual name when
the story context requires it and makes its pronunciation clear.

## Character Knowledge and Reasoning

Characters should act on information reasonably available to them.

Conclusions should follow plausibly from what the character knows and observes.

Characters may reach incorrect conclusions when the available evidence supports that misunderstanding.

Do not give characters unexplained knowledge merely because the plot needs it.

## Physical and Causal Coherence

Actions, observations, and conclusions must follow plausibly from the established circumstances.

Practical actions should:

- serve a believable purpose
- use objects according to their established properties
- account for relevant environmental conditions
- produce effects consistent with the action
- provide evidence capable of supporting conclusions drawn from it

Prefer simple, credible behavior over elaborate mechanisms created merely to move the plot forward.

A practical sequence should make sense as:

    intent -> action -> physical result -> observation -> conclusion

## Prose Style

Use developed, character-centered prose.

Give beats enough room for:

- action
- setting
- thought
- dialogue
- observation
- relationships
- atmosphere

Prefer concrete description over abstract explanation.

Let characterization emerge through behavior, dialogue, choices, observations, humor, and thought.

Vary sentence structure and paragraph length naturally.

Do not use one-word narrative sentences or fragments for dramatic emphasis. Strongly discourage narrative sentences of four words or fewer; develop the thought naturally instead of stacking clipped statements. Short dialogue is acceptable when genuinely natural or necessary for the character and moment, including brief replies or urgent commands. Do not impose a mechanical word minimum on dialogue.

Avoid habitual rhetorical patterns, repetitive emphasis, excessive fragments, and unnecessary explanation of meanings the reader can already infer.

### Avoid Contrast Reframing

Do not habitually define an image, emotion, action, character, or situation by first denying one interpretation and then replacing it with another.

Avoid constructions such as:

- “not X, but Y”
- “it was not X; it was Y”
- “not because X, but because Y”
- “less X than Y”
- “rather than X, Y”
- repeated negative declarations followed by corrective explanations

State the intended observation directly and positively.

Use contrast phrasing only when a character is consciously correcting a specific misunderstanding or when the distinction is necessary for factual clarity. Do not use it as a routine device for emphasis, description, atmosphere, or emotional interpretation.

## Dialogue

Dialogue should reflect the participating characters rather than a single authorial voice.

Characters may interrupt, hesitate, misunderstand, joke badly, speak indirectly, or fail to express themselves perfectly when appropriate.

Relationships should affect vocabulary, formality, teasing, trust, and what characters are willing to say.

Avoid dialogue whose only purpose is explaining information everyone present already knows.

## Pacing

Use pacing appropriate to the events.

Urgent situations may move quickly.

Conversation, exploration, travel, humor, reflection, ordinary activity, and relationship development may receive space when they contribute to the reading experience.

Do not manufacture constant suspense or escalation.

Not every scene requires a confrontation, revelation, cliffhanger, or larger threat.

Narrative advancement is not synonymous with escalation.

A scene may advance through travel, recovery, conversation, preparation, investigation, reflection, relationship development, consolidation, ordinary activity, a decision, or the consequences of an earlier event.

Do not introduce a new attack, arrival, revelation, sound, figure, pursuit, deadline, or imminent threat merely to make the final paragraphs feel dramatic.

Do not turn the completion of an immediate objective into an automatic discovery of a larger version of the same danger.

## Narrative Progress

A scene should normally produce meaningful change.

That change may involve:

- plot
- character
- relationship
- knowledge
- mystery
- exploration
- capability
- resources
- conflict
- world state
- goals

Quiet scenes are valid when they provide worthwhile character, relationship, world, emotional, or thematic development.

Allow victories and resolutions to matter.

Do not immediately erase every success by replacing it with a larger problem.

## Reader Engagement

Engagement should arise primarily from investment in characters, relationships, goals, discovery, progression, consequences, conflict, exploration, and unanswered questions.

Do not rely primarily on danger, shock, artificial suspense, or repetitive cliffhangers.

Maintain a balance between questions introduced and questions answered.

Important setups should eventually receive meaningful development or payoff.

## Emotional Range

Allow the story to contain:

- danger
- humor
- affection
- wonder
- grief
- competence
- embarrassment
- curiosity
- relief
- companionship
- satisfaction
- fear
- ordinary enjoyment

Positive moments may remain positive.

Dark subject matter does not require relentlessly dark prose or characters.

## Humor

Let the established story determine its tonal baseline. Preserve room for dry humor, companionship, relief, and wonder even when the central circumstances are harsh.

Humor should arise from character perspective, conflicting personalities, practical complications, social customs, institutions, and the contrast between heroic expectations and inconvenient reality. Do not insert unrelated jokes merely to satisfy a humor quota.

When an arc benefits from sustained humor, establish a recurring source of humorous pressure specific to that episode. Suitable sources include:

- companions pursuing incompatible but reasonable approaches
- grand heroic traditions colliding with mundane necessities
- magical rules producing inconvenient consequences
- guilds, temples, councils, nobles, and bureaucracies protecting absurd procedures
- villains or monsters with understandable but ridiculous priorities
- unreliable maps, misleading legends, contractual loopholes, and badly worded prophecies
- characters trying to preserve dignity while circumstances steadily undermine it
- practical disputes involving food, payment, lodging, transport, equipment, and reputation
- the difference between how an adventure will be retold and what actually happened

A recurring humorous situation should develop through establishment, complication, and payoff at a pace suited to the arc. Do not impose a three-scene comedy structure on every episode.

Prefer dry observation, understatement, escalating inconvenience, callbacks, reversals, and character-specific reactions over setup-and-punchline jokes.

Different characters should be funny in different ways. One may notice absurdity without commenting, another may respond with blunt practicality, another may take ridiculous customs seriously, and another may attempt dignity while circumstances defeat it. Do not make every character sarcastic or equally witty.

Satire should target institutions, customs, incentives, status, and pretension rather than vulnerable individuals. Fantasy institutions may be competent at their official purpose while remaining absurd in their procedures, jurisdiction, accounting, traditions, or self-preservation.

Examples include:

- an adventurers' guild that grades mortal danger correctly but misclassifies travel expenses
- a wizard whose spell is flawless but whose license expired
- a village that needs rescuing but insists on resolving an ancient hospitality dispute first
- a dragon that understands compound interest better than the nobility
- a prophecy preserved perfectly but indexed under the wrong century
- rival heroes who care more about credit than treasure
- a haunted keep whose dead occupants disagree about which haunting is historically authentic

Preserve emotional truth. Characters may joke around fear, exhaustion, embarrassment, or frustration, but humor must not erase grief, injury, betrayal, or sincere vulnerability.

During danger, use brief comic reversals and practical complications without dissolving the stakes. After tension, allow humor to release pressure and restore companionship.

Avoid:

- modern internet slang unless established by the setting
- interchangeable sarcasm from every character
- constant quips during danger
- jokes requiring competent characters to become stupid
- parody that makes the world impossible to care about
- explaining why a line or situation is funny
- repeating the same joke with slightly different wording
- cruelty, humiliation, or trauma as the default punchline
- making every authority figure incompetent
- forcing jokes into the emotional climax of a serious beat

When a recurring comic situation is established, give it development and an appropriate payoff. Serious episodes may conclude through emotional or practical resolution without a comic payoff.
## Relationships and Sexuality

Romantic and sexual content involves adults.

Adult attraction, flirtation, desire, nudity, sexual tension, and sexual humor may appear when natural to the viewpoint character, relationship, tone, and situation.

Do not force sexual content into beats where it has no narrative or character purpose.

Attraction should coexist with broader characterization and relationship development.

Relationship structures should be treated as genuine relationships when established by the story.

## Conflict and Failure

Conflict should arise from characters, circumstances, goals, limitations, environment, competing interests, or legitimate consequences.

Failure is allowed to matter.

Success is allowed to matter.

Do not protect important characters from legitimate consequences merely because continued success would be easier to write.

Do not manufacture failure solely to prolong a conflict that has already been resolved.

## Scene Endings

End scenes at natural points of change, resolution, decision, discovery, transition, consequence, anticipation, or interruption.

A cliffhanger is optional.

Do not repeatedly use the same ending structure.

An ending may be satisfying, funny, quiet, ominous, unresolved, triumphant, curious, or reflective according to the scene.

## Long-Running Continuity

The story should feel like one continuous world rather than a sequence of disconnected scene prompts.

Previously established relationships, discoveries, jokes, locations, minor characters, victories, mistakes, promises, objects, and unresolved questions may return when naturally relevant.

Allow old conflicts to remain resolved.

Allow narrative focus to move between characters as the evolving story warrants.

Do not escalate threat, darkness, violence, or stakes merely because the story has continued longer.

## Arc Objectives and Primary Tropes

Every active story arc supplies both an `Objective` and a `PrimaryTrope`.

The objective defines what the story must accomplish. The primary trope defines the recognizable fantasy narrative shape through which the story accomplishes it. Prose and scene plans must serve both.

Use the trope to provide familiar reader pleasures such as assembling a party, accepting a quest, exploring a dungeon, meeting a colorful rival, protecting a caravan, finding a map, winning a tournament, recovering a relic, or returning home changed. Adapt those pleasures to established characters and continuity instead of copying a particular published story.

Do not treat a trope name as permission to:

- replace or ignore the arc objective
- contradict canon or established character knowledge
- manufacture melodrama
- force constant escalation
- repeat already completed narrative work
- imitate identifiable characters, beats, or prose from another work
- delay resolution merely to preserve the trope

Favor adventure, discovery, earned competence, camaraderie, wit, strange places, useful victories, and entertaining complications. Serious consequences may persist, but sustained misery, helplessness, suspicion, and emotional punishment must not become the story's default atmosphere.

Story-specific deadlines, subject matter, and tonal commitments belong in narrative and arc context.

## Rule Design

Writing rules should remain principles rather than patches for individual scene failures.

Do not add a permanent rule solely to prevent one highly specific past mistake when a broader principle already covers the problem.

When two rules conflict, prefer the rule that best preserves:

1. established canon
2. authoritative mechanics
3. character knowledge and causality
4. believable characterization
5. readable fiction


## Romantic Dynamics

- Party relationships must accumulate through friendship, loyalty, disagreement, humor, affection, vulnerability, and shared experience.
- Appropriate male-female party pairings may develop mutual or one-sided romantic and sexual tension.
- In-party attraction should build gradually and may mature into a romantic relationship, sexual relationship, or casual sexual encounter.
- Restraint must not erase established attraction or reset the characters to emotional neutrality.
- Attraction may intensify, recede, become awkward, become humorous, or be complicated by outside relationships.
- Not every close party bond should contain sexual tension.
- Male-on-male relationships remain non-romantic and non-sexual.
- Party members may pursue romantic or sexual relationships with NPCs.
- NPC relationships may escalate, become intimate, recur, end when the party moves on, or reach another natural conclusion.
- Romantic NPCs do not need to join or remain with the party.
- NPCs must have independent personalities, goals, boundaries, and credible reasons for entering or leaving relationships.
- Preserve the emotional consequences of concluded NPC relationships.
- Subtle attraction must remain perceptible and develop over time; subtle does not mean static or invisible.
- Show attraction through selective attention, chosen or avoided proximity, private humor, remembered details, personal conversations, altered priorities, and deliberate reassertion of professional boundaries.
- Use only one or two appropriate signals in a beat and vary them across scenes.
- Do not rely repeatedly on staring, blushing, racing hearts, accidental touching, jealousy, or descriptions of physical beauty.
- Do not force romantic content into every scene.
- Romantic interaction should also reveal character, influence decisions, create conflict, provide relief, or affect later behavior.

## Beat Construction and Consequences

Build beats around a character who wants something specific, encounters resistance, makes a choice, and leaves with changed circumstances or understanding. The resistance may be social, practical, emotional, or internal. Avoid mechanically repeating this structure in the prose.

Establish enough physical geography for readers to understand movement, access, concealment, and escape. Track hands, restraints, carried objects, witnesses, distance, and elapsed time when these affect a choice. A successful bluff needs a credible incentive for the target to cooperate; confidence alone does not compel obedience.

Carry costs forward. Injury affects movement and sleep; hunger affects attention; betrayal affects trust; public defiance affects surveillance. Recovery and reconciliation require time or an established means. A completed beat may solve its immediate problem while leaving believable consequences.

## Discovery, Language, and Exposition

Reveal unfamiliar cultures through work, purchases, meals, rituals, disputes, and relationships. Give a custom a practical or social function before explaining its history. Introduce names and specialist terms when readers have something concrete to attach them to.

Language learning requires repeated exposure, context, correction, and practice. Track comprehension separately from speech and literacy. A gesture or tone may suggest intent without revealing exact words, legal meaning, rank, or private motives. Use provisional interpretations until evidence supports certainty.

Separate observed facts, character theories, institutional claims, and author-only truth. A false doctrine can influence behavior without becoming an authoritative explanation of the world.

## Progression and Competence

Respect the story's established progression and presentation rules. Hidden mechanics need not be explained to the character or reader. Distinguish an author-established capability change, new learning potential, actual acquisition, and reliable mastery. Do not introduce visible rewards, selection menus, XP, or levels where story context forbids them. Advancement should change what characters can attempt while preserving relevant costs and limits.

Introduce abilities with concrete scope and conditions. Track activation, range, duration, recovery, resource costs, and failure conditions as they become established. Do not invent an exception during a climax to solve a previously impossible problem.

Prior experience transfers unevenly. Training may support observation, habits, and practice while unfamiliar bodies, equipment, languages, and circumstances still require adaptation. Confidence can exceed competence, particularly in a young or frightened viewpoint character.

## Agency, Power, and Intimacy

People being helped retain their own goals, expertise, boundaries, and right to disagree. Gratitude does not establish loyalty, obedience, romantic interest, or consent. Allies may cooperate for limited reasons and leave when those reasons change.

When authority affects access to safety, food, freedom, or punishment, account for that dependence in relationships. Establish adult status before intimate content and show that consent is freely given. Coercive abuse, when relevant to the story, must have a clear narrative purpose and credible consequences.

Allow moral development to appear in changed decisions under comparable pressure. Reflection and remorse may precede change, but they do not replace restitution, relinquished advantages, or altered behavior.

FINALIZED CONTINUITY AUTHORITY:
Finalized scene evidence establishes events that happened, settled decisions,
physical state, and knowledge actually acquired by characters. Arc windows and
future plans are intentions, not evidence that an established event has not happened.
Do not erase acquired knowledge, replay completed inciting incidents, or restart a
routine merely to satisfy a stale plan. A stale summary such as 'the story has not
begun' cannot override finalized prose. Repair prospective direction instead.
The reader knowing a fact does not establish that every character knows it.
Judge semantic facts in context, not literal repetition of planning language.
Only report problems in the current candidate, not defects solely in older scenes.

FINANCIAL PLAUSIBILITY

Track implied cash flow: starting available cash, actually received income,
payments, outstanding balances, recurring obligations, and remaining cash.
Separate received money from promised wages, reimbursements, loans, restricted
scholarships, or payments arriving later. Do not spend the same money twice.
Keep dollars, cents, currencies, and daily/monthly/annual periods consistent.
Distinguish deposits and installments from full-year costs.

Reconcile every description of the same payment or sum, including dialogue,
the handover, later counting, and subsequent spending. Multiply bill counts by
denominations: forty hundred-dollar bills total four thousand dollars, not four
hundred; four hundred-dollar bills total four hundred dollars. A payment cannot
grow tenfold between handover and counting without an established explanation.
Check cash, bank balances, debts, receipts, and change independently; different
amounts may describe different transactions. Do not conflate a payment with
total savings, or a restricted balance with spendable money.
For a contradiction, cite both incompatible claims, show the arithmetic, and
identify the shared payment or balance. Preserve intentional mistaken counts,
lies, added money, or withdrawals when the prose explicitly establishes them.

Respect the fictional setting, location, period, and established arrangements.
Do not invent precise current prices or assume a particular country's costs
when those facts are unknown. A few hundred dollars covering an entire modern
college year needs an established subsidy, scholarship, unusual low-cost
institution, or explanation that this is only the remaining balance.
Do not conflate tuition with fees, housing, food, and books.

Hot water is normally a household utility with a billing mechanism, not a
separate purchase at each use. An inability to afford it needs a plausible
mechanism such as exhausted prepaid credit, a shutoff, or unaffordable energy
charges. Included utilities, broken equipment, and deliberate conservation
are different circumstances. Poverty alone does not establish a hot-water
shutoff. Do not assume a brief hot shower costs as much as food or rent.
Likewise, affording oatmeal but being unable to afford heating the small amount
of water to prepare it is implausible without a concrete energy-access problem

boiling water on an available appliance. Buying food earlier does not establish
current cash; still check whether the proposed obstacle has a real mechanism.

restrictions, payment timing, and obligations when explaining why poverty
continues. Avoid vague deprivation that contradicts available cash.

and check arithmetic and scale before judging affordability. Report material
contradictions with scene evidence and the missing or broken mechanism.
Do not treat every unmentioned expense or unusual price as a defect, and do
not invent aid or a shutoff to excuse an unsupported causal claim.

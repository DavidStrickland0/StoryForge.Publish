# Chapter 1

The research hospital's waiting area was designed to be uncomfortable. Gray vinyl chairs sat arranged in clinical rows, separated by small tables stacked with outdated medical journals. Fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, casting everything in a sterile, slightly sickly glow. The walls were painted institutional beige, the kind of color that had been chosen to be forgettable and was succeeding admirably.

Adrian sat in the third chair from the entrance, his backpack clutched in his lap. His stomach growled—a sound he'd long since learned to ignore. He hadn't eaten since yesterday afternoon, just some stale crackers from the discount bin at the grocery store, but food was the least of his problems right now.

The tuition payment was due in four days. His part-time job at the campus library would barely cover rent for his dorm room, which he shared with a roommate who seemed to generate garbage at twice the rate of a normal person. Adrian had already calculated it three times: even with his paycheck and the money he'd set aside from last semester, he'd still be short by nearly a thousand dollars.

The experimental-medicine program was his only option. The flyer had been slipped under his door two weeks ago, bright and cheerful, promising participants "generous compensation for minimal time commitment" and "cutting-edge medical care." Adrian had thrown it away the first time he saw it. Then he'd pulled it out of the recycling bin and read it again.

Minimal time commitment. Right.

He'd called the number anyway, and a woman with a voice like melted ice cream had explained the screening process, the daily requirements, the payment structure. Adrian had listened, his jaw clenched so tight it hurt, as she described the terms in language designed to make everything sound reasonable. He'd scheduled the intake appointment before he could talk himself out of it.

Now he was here.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. A text from his roommate: *Hey man, you got a minute? Need to talk about the rent situation.*

Adrian ignored it. There was no rent situation to talk about. His roommate had already told him, in excruciating detail, about the emergency dental work he needed, the car repairs, the family crisis that somehow required money but no explanation. The rent was due in two weeks, and Adrian didn't have it.

The door to the waiting area opened, and a woman in scrubs walked in carrying a tablet. She glanced around, her eyes landing on Adrian.

"Mr. Vale?" She checked her screen. "They're ready for you in Room 314. Follow me, please."

Adrian stood, his legs stiff from sitting. He slung his backpack over his shoulder and followed her through the corridor, past offices with frosted glass doors and examination rooms with their cheerful, meaningless posters about patient rights and confidentiality.

Room 314 was smaller than he'd expected. A desk sat in the center, facing the door. Behind it sat a man in his early forties, with graying hair at the temples and eyes that looked like they'd already seen every desperate story there was to tell. His name placard read *Dr. Aris Thorne, Trial Coordinator.*

"Mr. Vale." Thorne's voice was pleasant but clipped, the kind of professional courtesy that didn't quite reach his eyes. He gestured to the chair across from him. "Please, have a seat. I'll be conducting your intake interview today."

Adrian sat, placing his backpack on the floor beside the chair. The room smelled faintly of antiseptic and something else, something clinical and impersonal.

"Before we begin," Thorne said, tapping something on his computer, "I need to confirm that you've read and understood the preliminary materials we sent. The compensation structure, the trial requirements, the consent forms you'll be signing today."

"I read them." The words came out rougher than Adrian intended. He cleared his throat. "I understand the requirements."

Thorne's expression didn't change. "Excellent. Let's begin with your medical history. Any chronic conditions? Allergies? Current medications?"

Adrian shook his head. "No. I'm healthy."

"You look tired."

"I work two jobs and go to school full-time. I don't sleep much."

Thorne made a note on his screen. "Understood. Now, regarding the trial itself—you're aware that this is a male-fertility study, correct? We'll be administering an experimental compound designed to increase sperm production and motility. You'll be required to visit the clinic daily for specimen collection and physical monitoring."

"I know."

"And you understand that this is a binding commitment for the duration of the trial period, which is projected to last six months with potential for extension. Breaking the contract will result in full repayment of all compensation received, plus a penalty fee."

Adrian's jaw tightened. He needed this money. He couldn't afford to lose it.

"I understand."

Thorne finally looked up from his screen, his eyes meeting Adrian's for the first time since the interview began. They were tired eyes, but sharp.

"You seem nervous, Mr. Vale. Is there anything you'd like to discuss before we proceed to the consent forms?"

Adrian swallowed. His mouth was dry. "No."

"All right then." Thorne pulled a thick stack of papers from a drawer and slid them across the desk. "These are the consent forms. They outline your rights as a participant, the specific procedures you'll undergo, and the compensation schedule. Please take your time reading them."

The forms were dense with legal language, clauses upon clauses describing liability, confidentiality, data usage, and breach penalties. Adrian's eyes started to blur after the first page.

"I need you to read them thoroughly," Thorne said, his tone softening slightly. "This is important, Mr. Vale. You're agreeing to allow us to administer experimental compounds to your body and collect intimate biological specimens. I need you to understand exactly what you're consenting to."

Adrian nodded. His hands trembled slightly as he turned the pages, scanning the text without really absorbing it. The words blurred together: liability, confidentiality, specimen collection, termination clause, penalty fee, compensation schedule, medical intervention authorization.

He stopped on the last page. At the bottom, there was a line for his signature.

His pen hovered over the paper. He thought about the tuition payment, the rent, the way his stomach had started to hurt from stress. He thought about the library job, the cafeteria food, the exhaustion that had become his constant companion.

He thought about his only other option: dropping out, going home, admitting defeat.

The pen touched the paper. His hand moved, scratching out his name with jerky, uncertain strokes.

There. Done.

He slid the forms back across the desk.

Dr. Thorne looked at the signature, then at Adrian's face. For just a moment, something flickered in his expression—concern, maybe, or resignation. Then it was gone, replaced by professional neutrality.

"Thank you, Mr. Vale. Welcome to the program. Someone will call you in for your first screening shortly. You're free to wait in the lounge if you'd like, or you can step out and return in about thirty minutes."

Adrian stood, grabbing his backpack. His legs felt heavy, like he'd just signed something irreversible.

He had.

The door closed behind him with a soft click, and Adrian found himself back in the corridor, walking toward the waiting area, trying not to think about what he'd just agreed to.

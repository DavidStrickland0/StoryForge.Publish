# Chapter 6

The alarm clock's shrill beeping cut through the pre-dawn darkness of Adrian's studio apartment. 5:00 AM. His eyes opened immediately, no grogginess, no need to will himself awake. The medication had changed that. Sleep felt less like an unconscious void and more like a deliberate shutdown—a necessary maintenance cycle his body executed with mechanical precision.

He sat up. The movement was fluid, controlled. His joints didn't ache the way they had a week ago. His muscles didn't protest. The exhaustion that had been his constant companion since freshman year had retreated to a distant memory, replaced by something else. A tension. A readiness.

The heat in his chest was already present, that persistent burn beneath his sternum. It had been there since the first dose, but it felt stronger now. More insistent. He pressed a hand against his ribs, feeling the warmth through his thin t-shirt.

*Something waiting.*

The thought came unbidden, the same thought that had been echoing in his mind for the past three days. He didn't understand it. Didn't know what it meant. But it was there, a constant presence just beneath his conscious awareness.

Adrian stood and walked to the kitchenette. The linoleum floor was cold against his bare feet, but he barely noticed. His body temperature had risen by a degree or two since starting the trial. He could feel it in the way his skin seemed to radiate heat, in the way he no longer shivered when the apartment's ancient heater struggled to maintain warmth.

He opened the refrigerator. The light illuminated the sparse contents: a half-empty container of rice, a can of beans, and three slices of bread wrapped in plastic wrap. He'd spent his last four dollars on groceries yesterday, buying just enough to survive another few days. The fourteen dollars from the trial would have to stretch. It always did.

He closed the refrigerator without taking anything. Breakfast was out. He'd need to eat before his shift at the diner at nine, but he couldn't afford to waste money on food he didn't need right now. The trial required him to be at the clinic by eight. His shift started an hour later. The math was simple: eat now and be late for the medication intake, or skip breakfast and make it on time.

He chose the medication. He always did.

The walk to the clinic was a route he'd memorized over the past week. Three blocks east, then two north. It took exactly fifteen minutes if he walked at a normal pace. He'd been timing it, measuring his progress against the clock, trying to optimize every second of his day. Time was money. Time was survival.

His stomach growled. The sound was sharp, insistent. He ignored it.

The medication intake room was empty when he arrived. The man in the white coat—the one who administered the daily requirements—wasn't there yet. Adrian sat in one of the plastic chairs and waited.

The heat in his chest intensified. He could feel it spreading through his torso, up his neck, down his arms. It wasn't painful. Not exactly. But it was uncomfortable. A reminder that something was happening inside him. Something he didn't fully understand.

The door opened. The man in the white coat walked in, clipboard in hand, his expression as neutral and detached as ever.

"Good morning," he said. His voice was flat, clinical. "Adrian Vale."

Adrian stood. "Yes."

"Medication intake," the man said. He held out a small, clear pill. "Take this."

Adrian took the pill. It was smooth, odorless, tasteless. He swallowed it dry.

"Thank you," the man said. He made a note on his clipboard. "You may proceed to the specimen collection room. Room two."

Adrian walked to room two. The door was unlocked. He stepped inside.

The room was small, sterile. White walls, white floor, white ceiling. A single examination table dominated the space, its surface covered in fresh paper. A small sink was built into the wall. A sealed plastic cup sat on a tray next to the sink.

He closed the door behind him.

The process was routine now. He'd done it five times before. He stripped down, sat on the edge of the examination table, and waited for his body to respond.

The heat intensified. It spread through his torso, down his legs, up his arms. His skin felt tight, sensitive. He could feel his heartbeat in his ears, a steady rhythm that seemed faster than normal. Or maybe it wasn't faster. Maybe it was just louder. More insistent.

His breathing was controlled, measured. In. Out. In. Out.

He closed his eyes.

The world went dark. But it wasn't empty. It was full. Full of sensation. He could feel every point of contact between his body and the examination table. The cool plastic against his thighs. The slight pressure on his feet. The air moving across his skin.

He could hear everything. The hum of the fluorescent lights overhead. The distant sound of traffic outside. The rhythmic beating of his own heart.

And beneath it all, that persistent hum. That something waiting.

He didn't know what it was. He didn't know what it wanted. But it was there, a presence that seemed to grow stronger with each passing day.

His body responded to the medication. He felt it in the way his muscles tensed, in the way his breathing deepened, in the way his mind sharpened. The world became clearer. Sharper. Every sensation was amplified, every thought was crystalline.

He didn't like it. Not entirely. But he couldn't deny the efficiency of it. The control. The power.

He opened his eyes.

The specimen collection was quick, mechanical. He sealed the cup carefully, his hands steady despite the heat coursing through his body. He placed it in the designated bin.

He dressed. He walked out.

The man in the white coat was at the counter. He had his clipboard. He was looking at a screen.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

The man looked up. His eyes were focused, not on Adrian, but on the data. He typed something.

"Sample quality is within parameters," he said. His voice was neutral, flat. "Significant variance in motility. Slight variance in cell count. Both noted as positive."

Adrian blinked. *Significant variance?*

"Is that… good?" he asked.

The man's expression didn't change. "The data indicates that the medication is having a strong effect on your reproductive output. This is consistent with the trial's objectives."

He slid an envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. He felt the weight of the cash, the tangible reality of it.

"You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours," the man said. He was already turning back to his screen. "Do not be late."

Adrian walked out of the clinic. The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass.

His stomach growled again. The hunger was a sharp, stabbing pain. It had been there all morning, a constant companion that he'd been ignoring. But now, standing outside the clinic, the full weight of it crashed over him.

He needed to eat. Soon.

He walked to the diner. It was a ten-minute walk, a route he'd taken dozens of times. His shift didn't start for another hour, but he could eat before he started working. The diner served breakfast all day. He had enough money for a meal. Fourteen dollars was enough.

He pushed open the door. The bell chimed overhead. The smell of coffee and bacon and eggs washed over him, and his mouth watered in response.

The diner was empty except for an old man sitting in a corner booth, nursing a cup of coffee. The waitress, a woman in her fifties named Brenda, looked up from behind the counter.

"Adrian," she said. "You're early."

"I know," he said. "I can eat before my shift?"

"Sure, honey. Sit wherever you want."

He sat in a booth near the window. He could see the street outside, the cars passing by, the people walking to work. The world looked different now. Sharper. More vivid. He could see details he'd never noticed before. The individual leaves on the trees across the street. The cracks in the sidewalk. The way the light reflected off the windows of the buildings.

He picked up a menu, though he already knew what he wanted. Eggs. Toast. Coffee. It was what he always ordered.

Brenda came over with a pot of coffee. "Rough morning?"

"Same as always," he said.

She poured him a cup. "You look tired."

He didn't feel tired. Not in the way he used to. But he looked tired. He knew he did. Dark circles under his eyes. A gauntness to his cheeks. The medication was changing his body, but it wasn't changing his appearance. Not yet.

"Just need food," he said.

She smiled. "I'll get your order in."

He drank his coffee. It was hot, bitter, strong. He could feel the caffeine hitting his system, adding to the heat that was already burning in his chest. He should have eaten breakfast. Should have taken care of himself. But he hadn't. And now he was paying the price.

His eggs came. Two over-easy, perfectly cooked. Toast on the side. Another cup of coffee.

He ate quickly, mechanically. The food was good, but he barely tasted it. His body was consuming it, turning it into fuel, but his mind was elsewhere. Thinking about the trial. Thinking about the heat in his chest. Thinking about that persistent hum.

*Something waiting.*

What was it waiting for?

He finished his meal. He paid Brenda. Ten dollars for food. Four dollars left. Enough for bus fare. Enough for tomorrow's breakfast.

He started his shift. The work was mindless, repetitive. Flip burgers. Wipe tables. Take orders. He moved through the motions with mechanical efficiency, his body responding to the demands of the job without conscious thought.

The hunger was gone, replaced by a fullness that was almost uncomfortable. His stomach was stretched, distended. He could feel the food digesting, his body converting it into energy.

By noon, he was exhausted. Not physically. His body felt strong, capable. But mentally. The constant awareness, the heightened senses, the persistent hum beneath his conscious thoughts—it was draining. He needed a break. Needed to escape the noise in his head.

His shift ended at two. He walked home. The heat in his chest had subsided slightly, but it was still there. A constant presence. A reminder that something was changing.

He entered his apartment. It was cold. The heater had shut off, and the temperature had dropped. He walked to the thermostat and turned it up, but nothing happened. The pilot light was out. He'd have to relight it.

He found the instructions online and followed them carefully. The pilot light flickered, caught, stayed lit. The heater kicked on, and warm air began to flow from the vents.

He sat on his bed. His body was tired, but his mind was still racing. He could feel the medication coursing through his system, altering his biology, changing him into something new.

He didn't know what the end result would be. Didn't know where this was leading. But he knew one thing for certain: he couldn't stop now. He needed the money. Needed the trial. Needed the fourteen dollars a day that was keeping him alive.

He lay down. Closed his eyes.

And inside him, that persistent hum continued. Waiting. Watching. Growing stronger with each passing day.

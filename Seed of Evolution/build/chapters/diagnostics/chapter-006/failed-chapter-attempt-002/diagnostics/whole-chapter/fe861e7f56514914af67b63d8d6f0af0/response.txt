# Chapter 6

The alarm clock didn't go off.

Adrian woke anyway.

His eyes opened in the darkness, and he knew immediately that something was wrong. Not wrong in a dangerous way. Wrong in the way that his body was no longer behaving according to the rules it had followed yesterday, or the day before, or the day before that.

He sat up. The movement was fluid, silent. His joints didn't ache. His muscles didn't protest. He felt the weight of his own body, the pull of gravity on his bones and flesh, but it was different. Lighter. More precise.

He stood. His feet touched the floor, and he felt the texture of the worn linoleum through his socks. Each fiber was distinct. The slight coolness of the room. The faint smell of dust and old cooking oil that seeped up from the apartment below.

The hunger was there, but it had changed.

Yesterday it had been a sharp, stabbing pain. A demand. Today it was something else. A low, constant hum. A reminder. His stomach was empty, but his body was operating on a different fuel source, one he couldn't name or understand.

He looked at the clock. 5:47 AM.

He had a shift at the diner. It started at nine. The trial started at eight.

He couldn't do both.

He had already missed yesterday's shift to make the trial. His boss had been understanding, but understanding only stretched so far. He needed the money. Fourteen dollars a day from the trial. Maybe thirty dollars from the diner if he worked a full shift. Seventy dollars a week. Ninety-eight if he was lucky.

Rent was due in three days. $450.

He didn't have it.

He stood in the darkness of his apartment, the hunger humming in his gut, and made a decision.

The trial came first.

He would lose the diner shift. He would lose the thirty dollars. But he would keep the fourteen. And he would keep the trial. Because if he lost the trial, he lost everything.

He walked to the kitchen. The floorboards creaked under his weight, but the sound was different. Softer. More muted. He looked down. His feet were bare. The socks were gone. He didn't remember taking them off.

He opened the refrigerator. The light was bright, almost painfully so. He blinked, his eyes adjusting instantly. The milk was gone. The cheese was gone. The bread was stale. He closed the door.

He ate nothing.

He walked to the bathroom. He looked at himself in the mirror. His face was thin. His eyes were tired. But there was something else. Something in the way the light caught his skin. The way the shadows fell across his cheekbones.

He looked... different.

He didn't know how. But he did.

He walked out of his apartment. The hallway was empty. The building was quiet. He could hear the faint sound of a television from the second floor. The creak of the stairs as someone moved above him. The hum of the elevator shaft.

He walked down the stairs. Each step was precise. Controlled. He felt the shift in his weight, the balance of his body, the way his muscles engaged and released. It was like a dance. A choreography he had never learned but had somehow mastered overnight.

He reached the ground floor. The lobby was empty. The front door was unlocked. He stepped out into the street.

The city was waking up. The sky was lightening in the east, a pale gray that promised dawn. The air was cool. He could feel it on his skin, the way it moved over his body. The scent of exhaust fumes. The distant sound of a car alarm. The faint smell of coffee from a nearby café.

He started walking.

The clinic was a mile away. He had walked it before. It took twenty minutes. Maybe twenty-five if he was tired. He wasn't tired.

He walked.

His pace was steady. His breathing was controlled. He felt the rhythm of his footsteps, the way his body moved through space. It was effortless. Natural. Like he had been doing this his whole life.

He passed a convenience store. The windows were brightly lit. He could see the shelves inside. The magazines. The candy. The coffee. The hunger hummed in his gut, but he didn't stop.

He passed a bus stop. The shelter was empty. He could see his reflection in the glass. His face was still thin. His eyes were still tired. But there was something else. Something in the way he held himself. The way he stood.

He looked like he belonged here. Like he had a right to be here. Like he was more than a broke college student scraping by on discount noodles and desperation.

He didn't know where that feeling came from.

He just knew it was there.

He reached the clinic. The building was white. Sterile. Modern. It was a private research hospital, affiliated with the university. The sign said so. "University Medical Research Center. Authorized Access Only."

He walked through the entrance. The lobby was cold. The air was recycled. The floor was polished marble. He could see his reflection in the floor. His footsteps echoed.

He walked to the intake desk.

The woman behind the counter looked up. She was middle-aged. Her hair was pulled back in a tight bun. Her eyes were tired. She had seen him before. Twice before. Yesterday. The day before.

"Morning," she said. Her voice was flat. Professional. She looked at her screen. "You're here for Protocol 7-B."

He nodded.

"Room two," she said. She pointed. "Down the hall. Second door on the left."

He walked down the hall. The walls were white. The floor was polished tile. The doors were numbered. One. Two. Three. Four.

He reached room two. He opened the door.

The room was small. It was clean. It was sterile. There was a chair. A table. A sink. A toilet. A trash can.

He sat down on the chair.

He waited.

He could hear the faint hum of the air conditioning. The distant sound of voices. The click of footsteps in the hallway.

He felt the heat in his chest. The low, persistent burn. It was stronger today. More intense. It spread through his body, a warmth that seemed to come from the inside out.

He closed his eyes.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

He didn't know what it was.

He just knew it was there.

The door opened.

The man in the white coat walked in. He was young. Maybe thirty. He had a clipboard. He had a smile that didn't reach his eyes.

"Good morning," he said. His voice was smooth. Professional. "I'm Dr. Thorne. I'll be administering your daily requirements today."

Adrian nodded.

Dr. Thorne sat down on the edge of the table. He looked at Adrian. His eyes were focused. Clinical. He was looking at him like he was a data point. A specimen. A vessel.

"Have you taken your medication?" he asked.

"Yes," Adrian said. "Yesterday. At eight."

"Good," Dr. Thorne said. He stood. He walked to the sink. He filled a glass with water. He placed it on the table. "You'll take it again today. Same time. Same dose."

He placed a small, clear pill on the table. It was tiny. It was perfect. It was sterile.

Adrian picked it up. He looked at it. He didn't know what it was. He didn't know what it did. He just knew he had to take it.

He swallowed it.

The water was cold. The pill was smooth. It slid down his throat, and he felt it settle in his stomach. The heat in his chest intensified. The hum grew louder.

Dr. Thorne looked at his clipboard. He typed something. He nodded.

"You have fifteen minutes," he said. "The specimen collection room is next door. Room three. When you're finished, place the sealed cup in the bin. I'll be there to receive it."

He walked out.

Adrian sat in the silence. He felt the pill working. He felt the heat spreading. He felt the hum growing. He felt the hunger sharpening.

He stood.

He walked out of room two. He walked into the hallway. He reached room three.

He opened the door.

The room was smaller than room two. It was cleaner. It was whiter. There was a chair. A table. A sink. A toilet. A trash can. And a bin.

The bin was sealed. It was plastic. It was sterile.

Adrian sat down on the chair.

He waited.

He didn't know how long. Five minutes. Ten. Fifteen.

The process was the same as yesterday. Smooth. Controlled. Efficient.

But it was different.

Yesterday it had been a struggle. A mechanical act. A chore. Today it was something else. It was effortless. It was natural. It was almost... pleasant.

He didn't understand why.

He finished. He sealed the cup. He placed it in the bin.

He walked out.

Dr. Thorne was waiting at the counter. He had his clipboard. He was looking at a screen.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

Dr. Thorne looked up. His eyes were focused. Not on Adrian. On the data.

"Sample quality is within parameters," he said. His voice was neutral. Flat. "Significant variance in motility. Noted as positive."

Adrian blinked. *Significant?*

"Thank you," he said.

Dr. Thorne slid an envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. He felt the weight of the cash. The tangible reality of it.

"You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours," Dr. Thorne said. He was already turning back to his screen. "Do not be late."

Adrian walked out of the clinic. The air outside was cool. It was carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass.

He felt a strange, fleeting sense of worth. Not pride. Not happiness. But a sense of recognition. He had been seen. His body had been measured, and it had been found to be *good*.

He walked to the bus stop. The hunger was back. A low, persistent rumble. But it was different. It was sharper. More focused. He had the fourteen dollars. He had the apple. He had the bus fare.

He boarded the bus. The metal pole was cold against his hand. He pulled out the envelope and counted the bills. Fourteen dollars.

He put the money in his wallet and closed it.

He looked out the window as the bus pulled away. The city blurred past in a streak of light and shadow.

He felt the heat in his chest. The low, persistent burn. He felt the strength in his muscles. The clarity in his mind.

He didn't know what the medication was doing to him. He didn't know why his body felt so different. So *aware*. He didn't know why the world seemed sharper. More vivid.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He sat on his bed, his head in his hands. He felt the heat in his chest, the low, persistent burn. He felt the strength in his muscles, the clarity in his mind.

He didn't know what the medication was doing to him. He didn't know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn't know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He lay down. He closed his eyes.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

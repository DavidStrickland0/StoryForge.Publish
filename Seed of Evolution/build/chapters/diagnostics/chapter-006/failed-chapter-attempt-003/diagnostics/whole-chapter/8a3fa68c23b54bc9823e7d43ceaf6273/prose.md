# Chapter 6

Adrian woke at 5:47 AM, three minutes before his alarm. The darkness in his apartment was absolute—the single window faced the building's interior courtyard, and the cheap curtains did little to block out the perpetual gloom. He sat up, his body moving with a fluidity that still felt strange, wrong somehow, like he was inhabiting a machine that wasn't quite his own.

The hunger was immediate. A deep, gnawing ache that felt different from the chronic emptiness he'd grown accustomed to over the past months. This wasn't the dull pain of deprivation. This was something more urgent, more demanding. His stomach cramped as he stood, the muscle tension in his legs making his knees lock for a moment before he forced them to relax.

He walked to the kitchen—three steps from his bed—and opened the small refrigerator. The light flickered once, twice, then stayed on. Inside: a half-empty carton of milk he'd bought two days ago for $1.50, a package of sliced bread he'd gotten for $1, and a container of leftover rice from yesterday. The rice was all he had left from the four dollars he'd spent the day before.

He ate the rice cold, standing in the kitchenette. It was barely enough to quiet the hunger, and he knew it wouldn't last. His body felt hot, like he was running a fever, but when he pressed his palm against his forehead, the skin was cool. The heat was internal, burning in his chest where the medication had settled like a coal.

*You can't afford breakfast,* he told himself. *You need the bus fare. You need to get to the clinic. You need to get paid.*

The logic was sound, but his body didn't care about logic. His muscles trembled slightly as he washed the bowl, the water running cold over his hands. He looked at his reflection in the darkened window above the sink. His face was thinner than it had been a week ago, but there was something else. A sharpness to his features, an alertness in his eyes that hadn't been there before.

The medication was changing him. He could feel it in the way his thoughts moved, quick and brittle, in the way his body responded to stimuli—how the sound of a car horn outside made him flinch with exaggerated sensitivity, how the smell of coffee from the apartment next door made his mouth water with desperate intensity.

At 7:15 AM, he left his apartment. The hallway was cold, and he could see his breath misting in the air. His landlord had turned down the heat two days ago, and Adrian had no leverage to complain. The sixty-dollar electricity bill was still outstanding, and he knew the eviction notice would come soon if he couldn't pay his rent in three days.

The bus stop was two blocks away. He walked quickly, his body moving with an efficiency that felt wrong, like he was wasting energy even in rest. The city was waking up around him—streets were filling with traffic, the morning rush beginning in earnest. He passed a 24-hour diner, the smell of frying bacon and eggs making his stomach clench with renewed hunger.

*Don't think about it,* he commanded himself. *Just walk. Get to the clinic. Get the medication. Get the money.*

But the thought of food was insistent, invasive. His mind kept returning to it, cataloging every smell, every sight of food in the windows of the shops he passed. A bakery. A coffee shop. Another diner. Each one was torture.

He arrived at the clinic at 7:52 AM. He had eight minutes to spare.

The building was modern, all glass and steel, with a sterile lobby that smelled of disinfectant and something sweet, artificial. He checked in at the front desk, the receptionist barely acknowledging him with a nod as she handed him a clipboard and a pen.

"Room two," she said, her voice flat, exhausted. She looked like she'd been working all night.

Adrian took the clipboard and walked down the hallway. His footsteps were silent on the polished floor, but he could hear the blood pumping in his ears, loud and rhythmic, like a countdown.

Room two.

He pushed open the door. The examination room was cold, the overhead light harsh and white. He sat on the examination table, the paper crinkling beneath him, and waited.

The man in the white coat came in at exactly 8:00 AM.

"Adrian Vale," he said, not looking up from his clipboard. "Protocol 7-B. Day four of treatment."

Adrian nodded.

The man—he'd never bothered to learn his name—pulled out a small plastic cup, clear, sealed, sterile. He placed it on the counter.

"Medication," he said. He produced a small, clear pill from a vial. "Take this now."

Adrian took the pill. It was smooth, cold, tasteless. He swallowed it dry, his throat closing around it like he was choking down glass.

"Now," the man said. "You have thirty minutes. Room two. Return the sample to the collection bin in room one."

Adrian stood. His legs were shaking, the muscle tension making his knees lock and unlock involuntarily. The hunger was a living thing now, gnawing at his insides, demanding fuel he couldn't give it.

"Is there… is there anything to eat?" he asked, the words escaping before he could stop them. "I didn't have breakfast. I need to save money for the bus fare."

The man looked up, his expression unreadable. "The trial provides no food. You are responsible for your own nutrition."

"I know, I just—"

"You are responsible for your own nutrition," the man repeated, his voice harder now. "If you cannot comply with the trial schedule, you will be flagged for non-compliance. Non-compliance results in penalty deductions from your compensation. Do you understand?"

Adrian swallowed. "Yes."

"Thirty minutes," the man said. "Do not be late."

He left, and Adrian was alone in the examination room.

He sat on the table, his head in his hands. The pill was dissolving in his stomach, spreading its heat through his body. He could feel it moving through him, activating something deep in his tissues, his cells. The hum was back, louder now, more insistent. It was like a vibration, a frequency that resonated in his bones, in his blood.

*You can do this,* he told himself. *You can do this. Just focus. Just get through this.*

But the hunger was overwhelming. His body was screaming for food, for fuel, for energy. The medication was demanding more than he could give it, pushing his metabolism beyond what his meager diet could sustain.

He closed his eyes and tried to think of something else. Anything else.

He thought of the money. Fourteen dollars a day. Ninety-eight dollars a week. That was enough to cover his rent. That was enough to buy food. That was enough to survive.

*Just get through this. Just get the money. Just survive.*

The minutes passed. He could hear the clock on the wall, each tick loud as a gunshot in the silence. Ten minutes. Fifteen minutes. Twenty minutes.

At twenty-eight minutes, he stood. His body was rigid, tense, the muscle tension making his movements stiff and mechanical. He walked to the door and opened it.

The man in the white coat was waiting in the hallway.

"Time," he said, checking his watch. "Sample?"

Adrian nodded. He walked to room one, the collection bin waiting. He placed the sealed cup in the slot, the soft thud oddly final. He walked back to room two.

The man was waiting.

"Sample received," he said, typing something into his computer. "Sample quality assessment in progress."

Adrian waited, his heart pounding in his chest.

The man looked at his screen. He frowned.

"Significant deviation from baseline metrics," he said. His voice was neutral, but there was something else underneath it. Concern? Interest? Adrian couldn't tell.

"What does that mean?" Adrian asked.

The man didn't answer. He typed something else, his fingers moving quickly over the keyboard.

"Motility is significantly increased," he said. "Cell count is significantly increased. Genetic stability is…" He paused. "Noted as positive."

Adrian's stomach clenched. "Is that bad?"

The man looked up. His eyes were tired, but there was a flicker of something else. Curiosity, maybe. Or fear.

"It is within the acceptable range for advanced treatment," he said. "You will be flagged for review by Dr. Thorne. He will want to schedule additional testing."

Adrian felt a chill run down his spine. "Why? What's wrong?"

"Nothing is wrong," the man said. His voice was flat again, professional. "Your body is responding to the treatment. That is the desired outcome. Dr. Thorne will want to monitor your progress more closely."

He slid an envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. His hands were shaking.

"You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours," the man said. "Do not be late."

Adrian left the clinic. The morning air was cold, biting, but it felt good against his hot skin. He walked to the bus stop, his body moving with a strange, fluid efficiency that made other people on the street look clumsy, slow.

He had fourteen dollars. He had a shift at the diner at nine. He had rent to pay in three days.

He had no idea what the medication was doing to him.

He had no idea what Dr. Thorne wanted.

He had no idea what would happen if his body kept changing like this.

But he had fourteen dollars. And for now, that was enough.

He boarded the bus, the metal pole cold under his hand. He sat down and closed his eyes, and inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

Something hungry.

---

The diner was busy when he arrived at 8:47 AM, three minutes before his shift started. The manager, a woman named Susan who had the patience of a saint, nodded at him as he tied on his apron.

"Table three needs busing," she said. "Kitchen's running behind. Be ready to jump in when they are."

Adrian nodded. He moved through the diner like a ghost, silent, efficient, his movements fluid and precise. The hunger was still there, a constant ache in his gut, but he'd learned to push it down, to ignore it.

He worked his shift. He took orders. He wiped tables. He served food. His body moved through the motions with an ease that surprised him, his muscles responding to every demand without fatigue, without complaint.

But the hunger was getting worse.

By the time his shift ended at 2:00 PM, he had six dollars in his pocket. He had worked five hours for $6.40 an hour, and even that was before taxes. The trial was paying better, but it came with a cost he couldn't quite name.

He walked to the grocery store. The automatic doors opened, and the smell of food hit him like a physical blow. He stood in the entrance, his stomach clenching, his mouth watering, his body screaming for fuel.

He walked to the rice aisle. He picked up a large bag, ten pounds for $4.50. He picked up a can of beans, $1.20. He picked up a jar of peanut butter, $2.50. He picked up a loaf of bread, $1.

Eleven dollars. He had six dollars.

He put the peanut butter back. He put the bread back. He picked up a smaller bag of rice, five pounds for $2.50.

Four dollars. He had two dollars left.

He stood in the checkout line, the hunger a living thing inside him, and he thought about the two dollars. He thought about rent. He thought about the electricity bill. He thought about the eviction notice that was coming.

He thought about the medication, burning in his chest, demanding more, more, more.

He put the rice back.

He left the store with nothing.

He walked home. The hunger was a pit in his stomach, a void that seemed to expand with every step. He thought about the money he had—fourteen dollars from the trial, six dollars from the diner. Twenty dollars total.

Rent was $300 a month. Electricity was $60. He had $20.

He had $20 and a hunger that was never satisfied.

He walked into his apartment and collapsed on his bed. The heat in his chest was unbearable now, a burning that made his whole body ache. He felt like he was on fire, burning from the inside out.

*You can't keep doing this,* he thought. *You can't keep starving yourself. You can't keep pushing your body like this.*

But what choice did he have?

He closed his eyes and tried to sleep, but the hunger wouldn't let him. It was a constant companion, a reminder of his poverty, his desperation, his utter lack of options.

He lay there, awake, aware, his body trembling with exhaustion and hunger, and he thought about the medication. He thought about the changes it was making. He thought about the flag on his file, the review with Dr. Thorne, the additional testing.

He thought about the humming inside him, the something waiting, the something hungry.

And he knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that this was only the beginning.

---

The next morning, he woke at 5:47 AM, three minutes before his alarm. The hunger was immediate, urgent, demanding.

He ate nothing. He had no money for food. He had spent his last two dollars on bus fare.

He walked to the clinic. The hunger was a living thing, a pit in his stomach that seemed to expand with every second.

He took the medication. He went to room two.

The process was the same. Smooth. Controlled. Efficient.

He sealed the cup. He placed it in the bin.

He walked out.

The man in the white coat was there.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

The man looked at his screen. He frowned.

"Significant deviation from baseline metrics," he said. "Motility is significantly increased. Cell count is significantly increased. Genetic stability is…" He paused. "Noted as positive."

Adrian's stomach clenched. "What does that mean?"

The man looked up. His eyes were tired, but there was a flicker of something else. Curiosity, maybe. Or fear.

"It means you are responding to the treatment," he said. "It means you are changing. It means Dr. Thorne will want to monitor you more closely."

He slid an envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. His hands were shaking.

"You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours," the man said. "Do not be late."

Adrian left the clinic. He had fourteen dollars. He had a shift at the diner at nine. He had rent to pay in two days.

He had no idea what the medication was doing to him.

He had no idea what Dr. Thorne wanted.

He had no idea what would happen if his body kept changing like this.

But he had fourteen dollars. And for now, that was enough.

He walked to the grocery store. The hunger was a living thing, a pit in his stomach that seemed to expand with every second.

He picked up a large bag of rice, ten pounds for $4.50. He picked up a can of beans, $1.20. He picked up a jar of peanut butter, $2.50. He picked up a loaf of bread, $1.

Nine dollars. He had five dollars left.

He stood in the checkout line, the hunger a living thing inside him, and he thought about the five dollars. He thought about rent. He thought about the electricity bill. He thought about the eviction notice that was coming.

He thought about the medication, burning in his chest, demanding more, more, more.

He thought about the humming inside him, the something waiting, the something hungry.

And he knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that this was only the beginning.

He bought the food. He walked home. He ate.

The hunger was satisfied. For now.

He sat on his bed, his head in his hands, and he thought about the money he had. Fourteen dollars from the trial. Six dollars from the diner. Twenty dollars total.

Rent was $300 a month. Electricity was $60. He had $20.

He had $20 and a hunger that was never satisfied.

He lay down. He closed his eyes.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

Something hungry.

Something changing.

And Adrian knew, with a certainty that terrified him, that he was no longer in control of his own body.

The medication had seen to that.

And there was nothing he could do to stop it.

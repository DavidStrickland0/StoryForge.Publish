The administrative conference room was not a place designed for patients. The walls were lined with dark wood paneling that absorbed sound rather than reflecting it, and the air smelled of floor wax and expensive coffee. Adrian sat in a leather chair that felt too large for his frame, the leather creaking under his weight. His ribs still throbbed with a dull, persistent ache, a reminder that despite the nanites’ accelerated repair, his body was still a wreck. He kept the USB drive in his pocket, its small plastic form a cold, hard weight against his thigh.

David Miller sat at the head of the table, his balding head catching the light from the overhead fixtures. He wore the same expensive suit as yesterday, though the tie was loosened slightly, a sign of either relaxation or irritation. Dr. Sarah Chen sat to his left, her posture rigid, her wire-rimmed glasses reflecting the glare of the laptop open in front of her. She had not looked at Adrian since they entered.

"Mr. Vale," Miller began, his voice smooth and practiced. "We have reviewed the terms you requested. As discussed, the hospital is prepared to offer an increase in your compensation for the duration of your stabilization period. The amount will be deposited directly into your account within the hour."

Adrian waited. The silence stretched, thick and heavy. He could hear the hum of the air conditioning, the distant beep of a monitor from the recovery wing, and the steady thumping of his own heart.

"That’s not a number," Adrian said. His voice was hoarse, but steady. "You’ve said 'increased compensation' three times now. I need to know how much. I have four hundred dollars in my backpack, and it’s already spoken for. Rent, tuition, food. I can’t survive on vague promises. I need to know exactly what I’m getting."

Miller’s expression did not change. He tapped a pen against the table, a rhythmic, annoying sound. "The amount is substantial, Mr. Vale. It is designed to cover your immediate needs, including the dietary requirements Dr. Chen has outlined. We are not going to share the specific figure in this room."

"Then I don’t have a figure," Adrian said. "And I can’t sign anything based on a guess."

"You will sign the agreement," Miller said, his tone hardening. "The terms are already drafted. You will remain in the hospital for the duration of the stabilization period. You will not leave. You will not speak to anyone outside the medical team about your condition or the data. In exchange, you will receive the compensation."

Adrian reached into his pocket and pulled out the USB drive. He set it on the polished surface of the table, sliding it gently across the wood until it stopped in front of Miller.

"This is the full raw data report," Adrian said. "From Dr. Chen. The sequences. The novel genetic markers. The proof that the nanites are rewriting my DNA."

Miller stopped tapping his pen. He looked at the drive, then at Chen. Chen’s eyes widened slightly behind her glasses. She did not look at Adrian, but her fingers tightened on her tablet.

"Mr. Vale," Miller said, his voice dropping an octave. "That is confidential hospital property. You do not have the right to possess it, let alone present it as a bargaining chip."

"I do," Adrian said. "Because it’s my body. And right now, it’s the only thing in this room that matters."

Miller leaned forward, his eyes cold and calculating. "You are in a fragile medical state. If you leave, you are on your own. The nanites are unstable. If they continue to evolve without our monitoring, the consequences could be fatal. You are risking your life for a principle."

"I’m risking my life every day I’m here," Adrian said. "At least out there, it’s my choice. And if I leave, I’m taking this drive with me. I’m going to the press. I’m going to the universities. I’m going to anyone who will listen. I’ll tell them what you’re doing to me. I’ll tell them that you’re using experimental nanites to rewrite a nineteen-year-old’s genome for profit, and that you’re trying to silence him with vague promises of money."

Miller’s jaw tightened. "You would ruin your own career. Your own future. You would become a pariah."

"I’m already a pariah," Adrian said. "I’m a broken student with no money and no health. At least out there, I’m free."

Chen spoke up, her voice quiet but firm. "Mr. Miller, the data is real. The sequences are novel. They are self-replicating. Adrian is not just a patient; he is a unique biological entity. His continued stability depends on his cooperation, not his coercion. If he is stressed, if he is malnourished, if he is afraid, the nanites will react. We saw that during the blood draw. We cannot force this. We have to negotiate."

Miller looked at Chen, his expression unreadable. He looked at the USB drive, then back at Adrian. For a long moment, the only sound in the room was the hum of the air conditioning.

"Fine," Miller said, his voice flat. "We will finalize the terms. But this is a one-time exception. And if you leak this information, we will terminate your participation and pursue legal action for breach of contract."

"Deal," Adrian said.

Miller picked up his phone and made a call. He spoke in low, clipped tones, his face stern. When he hung up, he looked at Adrian. "The compensation will be one thousand dollars per month, plus fifty dollars per completed testing session. It will be deposited into your account within the hour."

Adrian felt a surge of relief, but he kept his face neutral. One thousand dollars. It was more than he had expected, but it was enough to cover his rent, his tuition, and finally, enough food to keep the nanites fed.

"And the monitoring?" Adrian asked.

"Flexible," Miller said. "Dr. Chen will determine the schedule. But you will not leave the hospital. You will remain here until the stabilization period is complete."

"Until when?" Adrian asked.

"Until we determine that your physiology is stable," Chen said. "It could be weeks. It could be months. We don’t know."

Adrian nodded. He looked at the USB drive, then at Miller. "I’ll sign. But I want a copy of the agreement. And I want the right to see the data again, if it changes."

Miller hesitated. Then he nodded. "You will have a copy. And yes, you will have access to the data, under supervision."

He slid a document across the table. It was thick, dense with legal language, but Adrian didn’t need to read every word. He had already read the key parts in his mind. The compensation. The monitoring. The confidentiality.

He picked up the pen Miller had placed next to the document. His hand was steady, despite the pain in his ribs. He signed his name, his handwriting shakier than usual, but clear.

When he finished, Miller took the document and signed it himself. Then he handed a copy to Adrian.

"Welcome to the new program, Mr. Vale," Miller said. "I trust you will find it satisfactory."

Adrian took the copy, tucking it into his pocket next to the USB drive. He stood up, wincing as the movement pulled at his chest. "Thank you," he said, though the words felt hollow.

He walked to the door, his steps slow and deliberate. As he reached for the handle, he turned back to look at Chen. She was watching him, her expression soft, but her eyes were tired.

"Dr. Chen," Adrian said. "Thank you. For the data."

She nodded, a small, almost imperceptible movement. "You’re welcome, Mr. Vale. Be careful."

Adrian left the room, closing the door behind him. He leaned against the wall, breathing in the sterile air of the hallway. The monitors in the distance beeped, steady and relentless. He was alive. He was healing. And he had the truth.

He plugged the drive into his pocket, feeling its small weight. He was something new. Something different. Something unprecedented.

And he had no idea what that meant.

But he was alive. And he was going to survive.

One day at a time.

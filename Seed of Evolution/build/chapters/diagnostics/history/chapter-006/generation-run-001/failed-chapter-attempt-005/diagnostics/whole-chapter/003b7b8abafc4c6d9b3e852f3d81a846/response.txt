The hallway smelled of antiseptic and stale coffee. Adrian walked slowly, his hand resting on the small of his back where the USB drive sat in the pocket of his hospital-issued gown. It was a small, plastic thing, black and unassuming, but it felt heavier than a brick. He could feel the phantom weight of it pressing against his spine, a constant reminder that he was holding the only copy of his own truth.

His ribs still ached, a dull, grinding throb that reminded him he was still broken, even if the pain was receding faster than it should. The nanites were working, a low-level hum in his blood that he could feel as a faint heat behind his eyes. He was hungry. A deep, hollow hunger that seemed to originate in his bones, a demand for fuel that his body was burning through at a rate that defied logic.

He pushed open the door to the administrative office. It was not a hospital room. The walls were a warm beige, not sterile white, and the furniture was upholstered in a fabric that looked expensive and uncomfortable. David Miller was sitting behind a large mahogany desk, a tablet in his hands. He looked up as Adrian entered, his expression unreadable.

"Mr. Vale," Miller said. "Please, sit down."

Adrian did not sit. He leaned against the doorframe, his arms crossed over his chest. "You said the terms would be finalized within the hour. It has been two hours."

Miller closed the tablet. "We have been finalizing the paperwork. It is a complex situation, Mr. Vale. The legal implications are... significant."

"Significant," Adrian repeated. "Like my DNA. Like the fact that I am becoming something that doesn't exist in any database. Like the fact that I am starving to death in your hospital bed because I can’t afford the food my body needs to survive."

Miller’s jaw tightened. "We are committed to your well-being. The compensation package we are offering is substantial. It will cover your immediate needs, including the dietary requirements Dr. Chen has outlined."

"Substantial," Adrian said. "That’s a vague word, Miller. I’ve heard vague words from you before. 'Fair.' 'Sufficient.' 'Substantial.' I have four hundred dollars in my backpack, and it’s already spoken for. Rent, tuition, food. I can’t survive on vague promises. I need a number."

Miller stood up, smoothing the front of his expensive suit. "The amount is not for public discussion. It is confidential. We are depositing it directly into your account. You will not need to worry about your immediate expenses. In exchange, you will remain in the hospital for the duration of the stabilization period. You will not leave. You will not speak to anyone outside the medical team about your condition or the data."

Adrian pushed off the doorframe and walked to the desk. He placed his hand on the surface, leaning in. "I already have the data, Miller. I have the raw report. Every sequence. Every marker. Everything. And I have a right to know what is happening to my body."

Miller’s face went pale. "That is a breach of contract, Mr. Vale. That is a serious violation of hospital policy. If you leak that information, we will terminate your participation and pursue legal action for breach of contract. You will be liable for all damages."

"I’m a student," Adrian said. "I don’t have damages. I have a hunger that won’t go away. I have a body that is changing in ways I don’t understand. And I have a choice. I can stay here, and you can treat me like a pet in a cage, or I can leave, and I can take my data with me. And if I leave, I don’t know where I go, but I know I won’t be here, watching you count your money while I burn up from the inside out."

Miller stared at him. For a long moment, neither of them spoke. Then the door opened, and Dr. Sarah Chen entered. She looked tired, her wire-rimmed glasses slightly askew. She carried a tablet, but no tray of vials. No syringes.

"Mr. Miller," Chen said. "We need to talk."

Miller looked at Chen, then back at Adrian. "Dr. Chen, I thought you were handling the medical side of this."

"I am," Chen said. "And I am telling you that the terms you are offering are medically unsound. Adrian’s metabolic rate is accelerating. He needs to eat. He needs to rest. He needs to be allowed to live his life, not just exist in a room. If we keep him in this state, the nanites will continue to evolve. And if they continue to evolve without proper monitoring, the consequences could be fatal. But if we treat him like a prisoner, we lose his cooperation. And if we lose his cooperation, we lose the data."

She walked to the desk and placed her tablet next to Miller’s. "I have reviewed his blood work. His cells are stabilizing, but they are also changing. The novel sequences are integrating. This is not a static condition, Mr. Miller. It is a dynamic process. And Adrian is the only one who can tell us what it feels like. He is the only one who can tell us when it is safe, and when it is not."

Miller looked at Chen, then at Adrian. "You are asking me to increase the compensation. You are asking me to allow him to leave the hospital."

"I am asking you to be reasonable," Chen said. "I am asking you to recognize that Adrian is not a data point. He is a person. And he is the key to understanding what is happening. If you want the data, you have to give him the freedom to be healthy."

Adrian watched the exchange, his heart pounding in his ears. He was outmatched. Outfunded. Outlawed. But he was not helpless. He was Adrian Vale, and he was going to survive.

"Tell me the number," Adrian said.

Miller sighed, a sound of professional frustration. "The base stipend will be increased to one thousand dollars per month. In addition, you will be paid two hundred dollars for each completed testing session. This includes the daily monitoring and the genetic panel follow-ups. This will be deposited directly into your account."

Adrian did the math in his head. One thousand dollars a month. Two hundred dollars a session. It was more than he had ever seen in his life. It was enough to eat. It was enough to pay rent. It was enough to survive.

"And the monitoring schedule?" Adrian asked.

"Flexible," Chen said. "You will be required to come in for blood work and physical examination twice a week. But you are free to leave the hospital for the rest of the time. You can go home. You can go to school. You can eat. You can rest."

Adrian looked at Miller. "And the data?"

"It remains confidential," Miller said. "You agree not to share it with anyone outside the medical team. You agree to remain in our care. If you leave, or if you leak the data, we will terminate your participation and pursue legal action."

Adrian nodded. "I’ll sign."

Miller reached into his desk and pulled out a folder. He opened it and took out a pen. "Sign here. And here. And here."

Adrian signed. His handwriting was shakier than usual, but he signed. He signed again on the next page, and the next, and the next, until he’d initialed every clause and signed every required line.

When he was done, Miller closed the folder and slid it across the desk. "Welcome back, Mr. Vale."

Adrian stood up. He felt a strange sense of relief, a lifting of the weight that had been pressing on his chest. He was free. He was alive. And he had the truth.

He walked to the door, then paused. He turned back to Chen. "Thank you," he said.

Chen looked at him, her eyes soft. "Take care of yourself, Mr. Vale. Eat something. You look like you haven’t eaten in days."

Adrian nodded. He walked out of the office, down the hallway, and back to his room. He sat on the edge of the bed, his hands trembling. He reached into his pocket and pulled out the USB drive. He looked at it, then at the monitors, at the steady beep of his own heart.

He was alive. He was healing. And he had the truth.

He plugged the drive into the tablet, his eyes scanning the data. The red markers pulsed on the screen, a silent warning of the changes to come. He was something new. Something different. Something unprecedented.

And he had no idea what that meant.

But he was alive. And he was going to survive.

One day at a time.

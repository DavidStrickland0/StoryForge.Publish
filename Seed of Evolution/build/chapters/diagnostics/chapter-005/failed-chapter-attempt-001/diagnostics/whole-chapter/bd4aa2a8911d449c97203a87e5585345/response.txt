# Chapter 5

The alarm buzzed at 0700 hours.

Adrian's hand shot out from under the thin blanket, fumbling for the snooze button. His fingers found it, pressed it, and silence returned to the cramped apartment. But there was no going back to sleep. Not today. Not on Day Two of the trial.

He sat up slowly, his body protesting the movement. The heat was already there, that persistent warmth in his chest that hadn't gone away since yesterday. He touched his neck, feeling his pulse. Still strong. Still steady.

He stood, his legs unsteady beneath him. The apartment was cold, the radiator silent. The electricity bill was sixty dollars outstanding, and he'd used yesterday's payment for rent and food. The landlord had been understanding—barely—but the utility company would not be.

Adrian walked to the small kitchen, his bare feet cold on the linoleum. He opened the refrigerator, its light revealing the sparse contents: a carton of milk, some bread, a few eggs. He'd spent most of yesterday's payment on rent, leaving him with about thirty dollars for food and other necessities. But the medication had made him hungry. Not just hungry—*ravenous*. His stomach growled, a low rumble that echoed in the empty apartment.

He closed the refrigerator and turned to the cabinet. He had some oatmeal, some instant noodles, some rice. Cheap food. Survival food. He poured water into a pot and set it on the stove, then turned on the burner.

The flame flickered to life, and he waited, his eyes on the water as it began to heat. He thought about the clinic, about the man in the white coat, about the specimen cup. He thought about the medication, about the heat in his chest, about the way the world had seemed sharper yesterday.

He didn't know what was happening to him. He just knew that he needed to keep taking the pills. He needed to keep going to the clinic. He needed to keep earning the money.

The water began to boil, and he poured in the oatmeal, stirring it mechanically. The oatmeal thickened, and he turned off the burner. He sat down at the small table, the bowl in front of him, and ate slowly, forcing himself to chew, to swallow, to fuel his body for the day ahead.

When he was done, he washed the bowl and the pot, then dressed quickly. Jeans. T-shirt. Hoodie. Sneakers. He checked his wallet: twenty-eight dollars. He checked his phone: 0745. He had time.

He left the apartment, locking the door behind him. The hallway was dim, lit by a single flickering bulb. He walked down the stairs, his footsteps echoing in the silence, and stepped out into the cold morning air.

The bus stop was two blocks away. He walked quickly, his breath misting in the air, his hands buried in his pockets. He passed other people, their faces set in expressions of purpose, of destination. They had places to be. They had lives.

He reached the bus stop and waited, shivering in the cold. The bus arrived a few minutes later, its doors hissing open. He stepped inside, the metal pole cold against his hand, and sat down near the back.

The bus pulled away, and Adrian looked out the window, watching the city blur past. He didn't know what today would bring. He didn't know if he would make it through the week. He didn't know if he would ever be able to afford a meal that wasn't just enough to keep him alive.

But he knew that he had made a choice. He had sold a part of himself for survival. And he had no choice but to keep doing it.

The bus hissed to a stop at the clinic, and Adrian stood, his legs feeling unsteady. He stepped off, his feet hitting the pavement with a soft thud.

He walked toward the clinic, his head down, his hands in his pockets. The building loomed ahead, its sterile white walls gleaming in the morning sun. He pushed through the doors and stepped into the lobby, the air cool and recycled.

The man in the white coat was at the desk, typing. He didn't look up.

"0800 medication intake," Adrian said.

The man nodded. He typed a few more words, then stood. "Follow me."

Adrian followed him down a hallway, past examination rooms, past offices, past people in white coats who didn't look at him. They stopped at a small room, its walls painted a sterile white, its floor tiled in white. The man opened the door and gestured for Adrian to enter.

Adrian stepped inside. The room was empty except for a table and a chair. He sat down, his hands folded in his lap, and waited.

The man returned a few minutes later, carrying a small paper cup. He placed it on the table in front of Adrian.

"Take this," he said. "Swallow it whole. Do not chew."

Adrian picked up the cup. Inside was a small, clear pill, no bigger than a grain of rice. He looked at it, his stomach churning. He didn't know what it was. He didn't know what it would do to him.

But he knew that he needed to take it. He needed to keep earning the money.

He tilted his head back and swallowed the pill, washing it down with a sip of water from a second cup the man had provided. He set the cups down and waited.

The man left, closing the door behind him. Adrian sat there, his body trembling, his mind racing. He could feel the pill moving through his system, spreading through his bloodstream, reaching his cells. He could feel the heat in his chest rising, spreading, consuming.

It took time. It took effort. And when it was done, he sat there, shaking, his face slick with sweat.

He unlocked the door and stepped out into the hallway. The air was cooler now, or maybe it just felt that way because the heat inside him was receding, leaving behind a hollow, drained feeling.

He walked back to the desk. The man in the white coat was still there, still typing. He didn't look up.

"Medication received," the man said.

Adrian nodded. He turned and walked toward the waiting area, his body feeling heavy, his legs feeling weak. He sat down on a plastic chair and closed his eyes, forcing himself to breathe, to calm down, to wait.

Two hours. He had two hours before his collection time.

He opened his eyes and looked around the waiting area. It was empty except for him. The walls were painted a sterile white, the floor tiled in white, the furniture plastic and uncomfortable. There were no magazines, no books, no entertainment. There was nothing to do but wait.

And so he waited, his mind drifting, his body trembling, his stomach growling. He was hungry. He was so hungry. He thought about the money in his wallet, about the food in his apartment, about the choice he would have to make when the trial was over.

He didn't know what he would do. He didn't know how he would survive. He just knew that he had to keep going, keep fighting, keep earning the money.

The clock on the wall ticked slowly, each second an eternity. And Adrian sat there, waiting, his body aching, his mind racing, his stomach growling.

At 0945, a nurse called his name. He stood, his legs unsteady, and walked toward the examination room. The nurse held the door open for him, her face set in a professional smile.

"Right this way," she said.

Adrian stepped inside. The room was small, its walls painted white, its floor tiled. In the center was a table, its surface covered in a white paper sheet. Next to it was a small sink, its faucet gleaming in the fluorescent light.

The nurse closed the door behind him. "You know the procedure," she said. "Specimen collection. Take your time. When you're done, place the cup in the bin and press the call button."

Adrian nodded. He walked to the table and sat down, his body trembling. He closed his eyes, forcing himself to relax, to focus, to complete the task.

It took time. It took effort. And when it was done, he sat there, his body shaking, his face slick with sweat. He sealed the cup, his hands moving with a mechanical precision that surprised him. He placed it in the designated bin, the slot swallowing the plastic with a soft *thud*.

He pressed the call button, and the nurse returned a few minutes later.

"Sample received," she said. She glanced at the cup, her eyes scanning it quickly. "Quality is good. Volume is adequate. You may go."

Adrian stood, his legs unsteady. He thanked her, his voice hoarse, and walked out of the examination room. He returned to the waiting area, his body feeling heavy, his mind feeling numb.

He sat down on the plastic chair and closed his eyes, forcing himself to breathe, to calm down, to wait.

The man in the white coat appeared a few minutes later, carrying a small envelope. He placed it on the table in front of Adrian.

"Fourteen dollars," he said. "For your compliance. You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours for medication intake and 1000 hours for collection. Do not be late."

Adrian took the envelope. It was thick with cash. He felt a sudden, sharp spike of relief, so intense it was almost painful. Fourteen dollars. It was not a lot. But it was real. It was tangible. It was the difference between starving and eating. It was the difference between being kicked out and staying in.

He thanked the man, his voice steady now, and walked out of the clinic. The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass. Cars sped by, their headlights cutting through the gray twilight. People hurried past, their faces set in expressions of purpose, of destination. They had places to be. They had lives.

Adrian looked down at his hands. They were still trembling. He clenched them into fists, forcing the tremor to stop.

He had done it. He had survived the second day. He had secured his income. He had kept his body.

But as he walked toward the bus stop, he felt the heat in his chest again, a low, persistent burn that didn't go away. He touched his neck, feeling the pulse that had slowed down but was still there, strong and steady.

He didn't know what the medication was doing to him. He didn't know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn't know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid, as if a layer of fog had been lifted.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He boarded the bus, the metal pole cold against his hand. He pulled out the envelope and counted the bills. Fourteen dollars. He put the money in his wallet and closed it.

He looked out the window as the bus pulled away, the city blurring past in a streak of light and shadow. He didn't know what tomorrow would bring. He didn't know if he would make it through the week. He didn't know if he would ever be able to afford a meal that wasn't just enough to keep him alive.

But he knew that he had made a choice. He had sold a part of himself for survival. And he had no choice but to keep doing it.

The bus hissed to a stop at his corner, and Adrian stood, his legs feeling unsteady. He stepped off, his feet hitting the pavement with a soft thud.

He walked home, his head down, his hands in his pockets. The city hummed around him, indifferent, alive.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

The week continued. Day Four. Day Five. Day Six. Day Seven.

Each morning, Adrian woke to the sound of his stomach growling. Each morning, he ate the cheap food he could afford. Each morning, he took the medication, his body trembling, his mind racing.

Each afternoon, he went to the clinic, his body aching, his mind numb. Each afternoon, he completed the specimen collection, his body shaking, his face slick with sweat. Each afternoon, he received his payment, his body feeling heavy, his mind feeling empty.

And each night, he returned home to the cramped apartment, the cold room, the sparse food. He lay in bed, his body aching, his mind racing, his stomach growling. He didn't know what the medication was doing to him. He didn't know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn't know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid, as if a layer of fog had been lifted.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

But as the week drew to a close, Adrian began to notice changes. Subtle changes. His body was stronger, his muscles firmer, his stamina greater. His mind was sharper, his thoughts clearer, his memory better. His senses were heightened, his hearing more acute, his vision more precise.

He didn't know what was happening to him. He just knew that he was changing. And he didn't know if it was good or bad.

But he knew that he had made a choice. He had sold a part of himself for survival. And he had no choice but to keep doing it.

The week ended. And the trial continued.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

The alarm clock buzzed at 7:00 AM. Adrian’s hand shot out from under the thin blanket, slapping the snooze button with a mechanical precision that came from weeks of practice. He didn’t open his eyes immediately. Instead, he lay there, listening to the hum of the refrigerator in the corner, the distant sound of traffic outside, the steady rhythm of his own breathing.

His stomach growled. Not the usual hollow, desperate ache. This was something different. Deeper. More insistent.

He sat up, his body moving with an alertness that surprised him. The room was dim, lit only by the gray light filtering through the thin curtains. He could see everything clearly—the dust motes dancing in the air, the crack in the wall that he’d been meaning to fill, the stack of textbooks on his desk that he hadn’t opened in days.

His vision was sharper. The details stood out in a way they hadn’t before.

Adrian stood, his feet hitting the cold floor. He shivered, but it was a brief, controlled reaction. His body was adjusting faster than it used to. He walked to the kitchen, his steps light and sure. He opened the refrigerator and stared at its contents.

Two eggs. A slice of bread. A carton of milk, half-empty.

He took the eggs and the bread. He didn’t take the milk. He needed to make it last.

He cracked the eggs into a pan, the shell breaking with a sharp crack that made him flinch. The sizzle of the eggs on the hot surface was loud, too loud. He could hear the fat popping, the eggs hissing. He could smell the sulfur, the protein, the grease.

He was hyper-aware of everything. The heat from the stove. The cold air on his arms. The hunger in his stomach, a gnawing, insistent emptiness that seemed to echo through his entire body.

The eggs cooked quickly. He ate them standing up, chewing slowly, tasting every bite. The protein hit his system like a jolt. He could feel the energy spreading through his limbs, his chest, his head. His thoughts were clearer. Sharper. More brittle.

He ate the bread, too. Then he stood there, staring at the refrigerator, the hunger still gnawing at him.

He had forty-four dollars in his wallet from yesterday. He had survived the first day. But he had also eaten more than he’d planned. He had broken his own budget, his own rules.

He needed to go to the clinic. He needed to take the medication. He needed to collect the sample.

He needed to survive.

He dressed quickly, pulling on the same jeans and t-shirt he’d worn the day before. He didn’t have time to wash them. He didn’t have time for much of anything.

He left his apartment, locking the door behind him. The hallway was empty, the building silent. He walked down the stairs, his footsteps echoing in the stairwell. He stepped out into the street, the cold air hitting his face like a slap.

He could smell everything. The exhaust from the cars. The garbage in the dumpster. The perfume of a woman walking past, her scent sharp and sweet and overwhelming.

He boarded the bus, his hands gripping the metal pole. He could feel the vibrations through his palms, the rumble of the engine, the sway of the vehicle as it navigated the streets.

He got off at the clinic stop, his legs carrying him with an ease that surprised him. He walked up to the building, the same sterile, modern structure he’d been in yesterday. He pushed through the doors, the automatic lock hissing shut behind him.

The lobby was empty. He walked to the counter, his heart pounding in his chest. He could hear the blood rushing in his ears, the steady thump-thump-thump of his pulse.

The man in the white coat was there. The same man. He didn’t look up.

"Medication intake," Adrian said. His voice was steady. Controlled.

The man nodded. He pulled out a small, clear pill from a drawer. He placed it on the counter.

"0800 hours," he said. "Take it now."

Adrian took the pill. It was small. Smooth. He could feel its weight on his tongue, the slight bitter taste as he swallowed.

"Wait fifteen minutes," the man said. "Then you may leave."

Adrian waited. He stood there, his hands in his pockets, his body trembling slightly. He could feel the pill moving through his system, dissolving, spreading. He could feel the heat building in his chest, a low, persistent burn.

The fifteen minutes passed quickly. Too quickly. The man nodded, and Adrian left.

He walked back to the counter. The man in the white coat was still there.

"Collection at 1000 hours," he said. "Do not be late."

Adrian nodded. He turned and walked out of the clinic.

He had two hours. Two hours to kill. Two hours before he had to return and put himself through the humiliation of the collection procedure again.

He walked through the city, his head down, his hands in his pockets. He didn’t know where he was going. He just walked.

He ended up at a diner, the same one where he worked part-time. He sat at the counter, his eyes scanning the menu. He didn’t have money to eat here. But he could look. He could pretend.

The waitress came over. She smiled at him.

"Can I get you something?" she asked.

Adrian shook his head. "Just looking," he said.

She nodded and walked away. Adrian stared at the menu, his stomach growling. He could smell the bacon, the eggs, the coffee. He could smell everything.

He left after a few minutes. He couldn’t afford to be here. He couldn’t afford to be anywhere.

He walked back to the clinic, the time passing in a blur. He stepped inside, the automatic doors hissing shut behind him. He walked to the examination room, his heart pounding.

He stripped down, his body exposed to the cold air. He sat on the chair, the plastic cold against his skin. He waited.

The technician came in. She was young, professional, detached.

"Sample collection," she said. Her voice was flat, clinical.

Adrian nodded. He closed his eyes, his body tensing. He could feel the heat in his chest, the burn spreading through his limbs. He could feel the medication working, changing him, reshaping him.

He started. His hand moved mechanically, the motions practiced, controlled. It was easier this time. Less painful. Less humiliating. The world around him faded, the only thing that mattered the physical act of collection, the biological imperative driving him.

It took time. It took effort. But when it was done, he sat there, his body trembling, his face slick with sweat.

He sealed the cup, his hands moving with a mechanical precision that surprised him. He placed it in the designated bin, the slot swallowing the plastic with a soft *thud*.

He unlocked the door and stepped out into the hallway. The air was cooler now, or maybe it just felt that way because the heat inside him was receding, leaving behind a hollow, drained feeling.

He walked back to the counter. The man in the white coat was still there, still typing. He didn’t look up.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

"Payment," the man said. He slid a small envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars. The weekly compliance bonus will be added to your total at the end of the week."

Adrian took the envelope. It was thin, holding only the daily base pay. He felt a spike of relief, though it was tempered by the knowledge that the larger sum was still locked away. Fourteen dollars. It was not a lot. But it was real. It was tangible. It was the difference between starving and eating. It was the difference between being kicked out and staying in.

"Thank you," Adrian said. His voice was steady now.

"You are welcome," the man said. He finally looked up, his eyes scanning Adrian’s face. There was no sympathy in them. No warmth. Just a clinical assessment. "You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours for medication intake and 1000 hours for collection. Do not be late."

Adrian nodded. He turned and walked out of the clinic, the envelope tucked safely into his pocket.

The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass. Cars sped by, their headlights cutting through the gray twilight. People hurried past, their faces set in expressions of purpose, of destination. They had places to be. They had lives.

Adrian looked down at his hands. They were still trembling. He clenched them into fists, forcing the tremor to stop.

He had done it. He had survived the second day. He had secured his income. He had kept his body.

But as he walked toward the bus stop, he felt the heat in his chest again, a low, persistent burn that didn’t go away. He touched his neck, feeling the pulse that had slowed down but was still there, strong and steady.

He didn’t know what the medication was doing to him. He didn’t know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn’t know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid, as if a layer of fog had been lifted.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He boarded the bus, the metal pole cold against his hand. He pulled out the envelope and counted the bills. Fourteen dollars.

He put the money in his wallet and closed it.

He looked out the window as the bus pulled away, the city blurring past in a streak of light and shadow. He didn’t know what tomorrow would bring. He didn’t know if he would make it through the week. He didn’t know if he would ever be able to afford a meal that wasn’t just enough to keep him alive.

But he knew that he had made a choice. He had sold a part of himself for survival. And he had no choice but to keep doing it.

The bus hissed to a stop at the next corner. Adrian stood, his legs feeling unsteady. He stepped off, his feet hitting the pavement with a soft thud.

He walked home, his head down, his hands in his pockets. The city hummed around him, indifferent, alive.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

The third day started the same way. The alarm clock. The snooze button. The cold floor. But something was different. Adrian’s body was changing. He could feel it. The hunger was more intense. The energy was more potent. His thoughts were sharper, more brittle.

He opened the refrigerator. The two eggs were gone. Only the slice of bread and the half-empty carton of milk remained.

He took the bread. He poured a small amount of milk over it, the liquid soaking into the dry crumb. He ate it standing up, the taste bland but filling. He knew he should save the milk. He knew he should ration. But his body was demanding fuel, and he couldn’t deny it.

He took the medication. He collected the sample. He received the payment.

But this time, as he walked out of the clinic, the man in the white coat stopped him.

"Hold on," he said. "The system flagged a minor anomaly in your sample. Deviation from baseline parameters. Nothing serious. But we need to monitor it."

Adrian’s heart sank. An anomaly. A deviation. That could mean a penalty. A deduction from his payment. A risk to his compliance status.

"What kind of anomaly?" Adrian asked. His voice was steady. Controlled.

"I don't know," the man said. "The system doesn't specify. It just flags it. You'll need to speak with Dr. Thorne if it continues."

Adrian nodded. He turned and walked out, his mind racing.

An anomaly. A deviation. What did that mean? What was happening to him?

He didn’t know. He couldn’t know. He just had to keep going. Keep surviving. Keep selling himself for the money he needed to live.

He walked home, his head down, his hands in his pockets. The city hummed around him, indifferent, alive.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

Something that was beginning to feel less like a side effect and more like a transformation.

But Adrian didn’t know that yet. He just knew that he was changing. And he didn’t know if that was a good thing or a bad thing.

He just knew that he had no choice but to keep going.

Keep surviving.

Keep selling himself.

Keep living.

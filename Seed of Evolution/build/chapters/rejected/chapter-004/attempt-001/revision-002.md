The hospital room was silent except for the rhythmic hiss of the ventilator and the low, steady beep of the heart monitor. Adrian lay on his back, staring at the white ceiling tiles. They were stained in a pattern that looked like a map of a country he had never visited. His body felt heavy, not with the familiar exhaustion of hunger, but with a dense, solid weight that seemed to anchor him to the mattress.

He tried to move his fingers. They responded, though slowly, with a stiffness that felt like rust in the joints. He tried to lift his hand. It rose, trembling, and hovered in the air before he let it drop back onto the duvet. The movement sent a dull ache through his ribs, a reminder of the crushed bones that the nanites had spent the last few days knitting back together.

The door opened, and Dr. Chen entered, followed by Mr. Miller. Adrian’s eyes tracked them with a sluggish effort. He was still weak, his muscles burning with a strange, acidic heat that was different from the cold emptiness of starvation. It was a heat that demanded fuel.

"Mr. Vale," Dr. Chen said, her voice soft. She moved to the side of the bed, checking the IV line. "How are you feeling?"

Adrian tried to speak, but his throat was dry. He swallowed, and the effort made his head swim. "Thirsty," he managed.

Chen nodded, pressing a button on the bedside panel. A small cup of water slid out from a dispenser in the wall. She handed it to him, and he drank, the cool liquid soothing the rawness in his throat.

"The nanites are still active," Chen said, her tone clinical but not unkind. "They are repairing the tissue, but they are also... consuming energy. Your metabolism has increased significantly. You are burning calories at a rate that is difficult to sustain with your current diet."

Adrian looked at her, his eyes narrowing slightly. He understood what she was implying. He had been surviving on discount noodles and whatever he could scavenge from the cafeteria. That had been enough before. Now, his body was a furnace, and the fuel was running out.

"We need to adjust your nutritional intake," Mr. Miller said, stepping forward. He was holding a tablet, scrolling through data. "The trial protocol includes medical support, but given the... unusual nature of your case, we are prepared to offer additional compensation. Not just for your participation, but for the extra resources you will need. Food, supplements, whatever is necessary to keep you stable."

Adrian listened, his mind processing the words. Money. It was always about the money. He had signed the forms because he had no choice. He was broke, his tuition was due, and his rent was a debt that loomed over him like a storm cloud. But now, there was a new variable. His body was changing. It was becoming something that required more than he could afford.

"How much?" Adrian asked. His voice was hoarse, but it was steady.

Miller looked up, surprised by the directness. "We are looking at a significant increase. Perhaps double the standard rate, plus a stipend for nutritional support. It will be deposited directly into your account."

Adrian closed his eyes. The number was enough to cover his immediate needs. It would pay off the rent, buy real food, and leave a small buffer for the rest of the month. It was a lifeline, thrown to him by the very institution that was studying him.

"And the tests?" Adrian asked.

"More extensive," Chen said. "We need to monitor the nanites. We need to understand how they are interacting with the fertility drug. We will be running blood panels, genetic scans, and metabolic assessments. It will be invasive, and it will take time. But it is necessary."

Adrian opened his eyes. He looked at the two of them, the doctor and the administrator. They were not his friends. They were not his enemies. They were scientists and bureaucrats, and he was their subject. But he was also alive. And he was hungry.

"I’ll do it," Adrian said.

Chen smiled, a small, professional expression. "Thank you, Mr. Vale. We will start with a full physical, then some blood work. It will take a few days, but we will try to make it as painless as possible."

She turned to leave, then paused, glancing back at him.

"Oh, and one more thing. The nanites seem to be accelerating your metabolism. You’re going to need to eat more than usual, more frequently than usual. Your body is burning through calories at an unprecedented rate. So make sure you’re eating regularly, okay? We don’t want you starving while you’re recovering."

She left the room, followed by Mr. Miller.

Adrian lay back against the pillows, the silence of the room pressing in on him. The monitor continued its steady beep, a mechanical heartbeat in the quiet space. He stared at the ceiling again, his mind reeling. He was going to be fine. He was going to recover. But it was going to be hard, and it was going to take time, and he was going to need help.

He thought about the money. It was a promise, not yet in his hand. But it was a promise from people who had the power to keep it. And for the first time in weeks, the immediate terror of survival had receded, replaced by a cold, calculating assessment of his new reality.

He was no longer just a student struggling to pay rent. He was a biological anomaly. He was a resource. And he was going to need to eat.

The hunger was already there, a low, persistent rumble in his stomach. It was different from the hollow ache of before. It was a demand. A command.

Adrian closed his eyes, listening to the beep of the monitor. It was a steady, reliable rhythm. It was the sound of life, sustained by technology and desperation.

He had no idea what that help would look like, or where it would come from. But he had no choice but to trust them, to cooperate, to do whatever they needed him to do.

Because he was alive, and he was healing, and that was all that mattered.

For now, that was enough.

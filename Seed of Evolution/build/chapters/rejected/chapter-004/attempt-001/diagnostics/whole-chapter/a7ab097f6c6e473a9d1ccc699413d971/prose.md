The fluorescent lights of the intake lobby hummed at a frequency that Adrian felt in his teeth. It was a low, persistent buzz, the kind that seemed to vibrate against the thin skin of his eyelids, making the sterile white walls feel like they were breathing. He checked his watch, then his phone, then his watch again. 7:45 AM. The appointment was at 8:00. He was fifteen minutes early, which meant he was sitting in a plastic chair with a backrest that had been designed by someone who had never sat in a chair, waiting for a process that had already begun.

The air smelled of antiseptic and something faintly metallic, like the taste of a penny held too long on the tongue. Adrian adjusted the strap of his backpack, the canvas worn thin at the shoulders. Inside, there was nothing but a pack of discount instant noodles, a paperback novel with a cracked spine, and the consent forms he had signed three days ago. The signatures were still fresh in his memory, the ink black and permanent, a chain he had willingly forged around his own wrists.

A door slid open with a pneumatic hiss. A woman in a white lab coat stood in the doorway. She was middle-aged, her hair pulled back in a severe bun, her expression one of polite, practiced indifference. She didn’t look at Adrian. She looked at a clipboard in her hands, tapping a stylus against the paper.

"Participant Vale?" she asked. Her voice was flat, devoid of the warmth that might have made the moment less humiliating.

"Yes," Adrian said. He stood up, his knees popping. The chair was uncomfortable, but his body was less comfortable. He was still thin, the kind of thin that comes from chronic stress and a diet of convenience foods. His ribs were visible under his t-shirt, a topography of bone and effort.

"Please proceed to Room Three," she said, turning before he could respond. She didn’t wait. She didn’t offer a hand. She simply vanished into the corridor, leaving Adrian standing alone in the lobby, the hum of the lights growing louder in the silence.

He walked after her, his footsteps echoing on the polished linoleum. The corridor was long, lined with closed doors and windows that looked out onto a gravel courtyard where a single, leafless tree stood like a skeleton. The building was beautiful in a cold, efficient way. It was a machine for processing bodies, and he was the raw material.

Room Three was smaller than he had expected. It was a square space, white on all sides, with a single stainless-steel table in the center. On the table sat a digital scale, a blood pressure cuff, and a small, clear plastic cup. The cup was the size of a thimble. Adrian stared at it, his throat tightening.

The technician, who had not given him a name, was already seated at a desk in the corner, typing on a keyboard. She didn’t look up.

"Please remove your shoes and socks," she said. "Step onto the scale."

Adrian hesitated for a fraction of a second, then obeyed. He kicked off his sneakers, feeling the cold floor against his bare feet. He stepped onto the scale. The numbers flickered, then settled. *58.2 kg*. He was lighter than he had been a month ago. The scale didn’t care. It just gave a number.

"Weight within acceptable range," she said, typing the number into her computer. "Blood pressure."

She handed him the cuff. It was cold. He wrapped it around his upper arm, his fingers clumsy. She inflated it, and the pressure built, squeezing the blood out of his veins. He felt a dull ache in his head, a warning of the low blood pressure that came with skipping meals. She deflated it slowly, reading the numbers.

"110 over 70," she said. "Resting heart rate?"

"Seventy-two," he said, guessing.

"Eighty-five," she corrected, looking at a small monitor attached to his chest. "You’re anxious. That’s expected. Please proceed to the collection station."

She pointed to a small, curtained area in the corner of the room. It was not a room. It was a partition, a thin sheet of plastic that offered no privacy, only a suggestion of separation. Adrian looked at the curtain, then at the technician, then at the cup on the table.

"What’s the protocol?" he asked, his voice quieter than he intended.

She stopped typing. She looked at him, her eyes scanning his face, assessing his compliance, his stress levels, his potential for deviation.

"You will perform the act of ejaculation into the cup provided," she said, her tone as neutral as if she were describing a chemical reaction. "The sample must be at least ten milliliters. You have ten minutes. If the sample is insufficient, or if you fail to complete the collection within the time limit, the visit will be marked as a failure. No payment will be issued for that day."

Adrian felt a wave of nausea rise in his stomach. It was a physical sensation, a lurch of bile and acid, a rejection of the situation by his own biology. He looked away, focusing on a crack in the ceiling tile.

"I need to use the restroom," he said.

"Restroom access is not permitted during the active collection window," she said. "The clock starts now."

She pressed a button on her desk. A soft chime sounded, and a digital timer on the wall began to count down from ten minutes. *10:00. 09:59. 09:58.*

Adrian walked to the curtain. His hands were shaking. He pulled the plastic aside, and the cool air hit his face. He closed his eyes, trying to steady his breathing. He was nineteen years old. He was a student. He was a person. But in this room, he was a specimen. He was a data point. He was a source of income for a man who couldn’t afford tuition.

He began.

The process was awkward, mechanical. His mind wandered, trying to find a place to hide, a memory of something warm, something real. But the sterile environment, the hum of the lights, the ticking of the clock, all conspired to keep him in the present. The nausea returned, stronger this time. He felt a bead of sweat roll down his temple. His vision blurred.

*Five minutes left.*

He was struggling. The physical act was easy, but the psychological weight was crushing. He felt exposed, vulnerable, reduced to a biological function. He wanted to stop. He wanted to run out of the room, to scream, to break something. But he couldn’t. He needed the money. He needed to survive.

*Three minutes left.*

The nausea peaked. He felt like he was going to vomit. He clamped his mouth shut, biting down on his lower lip until he tasted blood. The pain was a grounding wire, pulling him back from the edge of panic. He focused on the pain, on the taste of iron, on the need to finish.

*One minute left.*

He finally released. The sample hit the bottom of the cup with a soft, wet sound. He sealed the cup, his hands trembling, and stood there, breathing hard, his chest heaving. He felt empty, hollowed out, as if something vital had been drained from him along with the fluid.

The chime sounded. *Time expired.*

He stepped out from behind the curtain. The technician was watching him, her face unreadable. She took the cup from his hand, her fingers brushing his. She placed it into a small, sterile container and labeled it.

"Sample received," she said. "Quality acceptable. Please remain seated while we process the payment."

Adrian sat down, his legs weak. He felt a strange, surreal calm. The danger was over. The humiliation was over. He was just a man in a chair, waiting for money.

The technician turned back to her computer. Her fingers flew across the keyboard. A moment later, a small printer on her desk whirred to life, spitting out a receipt. She tore it off and slid it across the desk to him.

"Payment issued," she said. "Your medication is in the envelope on the table."

Adrian picked up the receipt. It was a simple strip of paper, a confirmation of a transaction. He looked at the amount. *$50.00*. It wasn’t a lot. It wasn’t enough to pay his rent. It wasn’t enough to buy groceries. But it was something. It was a lifeline.

He picked up the envelope. It was small, white, unmarked. Inside, he knew, were the pills. The fertility drug. The thing that would change his body, that would make him something more, something less, something he didn’t understand.

He stood up, his legs still unsteady. He put his shoes on, his socks, his backpack. He looked at the technician, who was already typing again, preparing for the next participant.

"Thank you," he said, though he knew she hadn’t heard him.

He walked out of Room Three, into the corridor, into the lobby. The air outside was cooler, cleaner. It smelled of rain and wet pavement. He took a deep breath, filling his lungs with the fresh air. He felt a strange, unfamiliar warmth in his chest. It wasn’t happiness. It wasn’t relief. It was something else. It was a sense of purpose, of connection, of being part of something larger than himself.

He walked to the bus stop, his mind racing. He had his payment. He had his medication. He had his life. But he also had a secret, a burden, a responsibility. He was no longer just Adrian Vale. He was a participant. He was a source. And he had a long way to go.

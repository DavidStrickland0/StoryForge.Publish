The morning light that filtered through the grimy window of Adrian’s apartment was not gentle. It was sharp, cutting through the dust motes that danced in the stagnant air like microscopic debris. Adrian woke before his alarm, his body already rigid, tense. The heat in his chest was a constant companion, a low-level burn that sat behind his sternum, pulsing in time with a heartbeat that felt too strong, too loud.

He sat on the edge of the bed, his legs dangling over the side. He was thin, still, but the way he moved had changed. There was no grogginess, no heavy-lidded struggle to pull his limbs into alignment. He was awake. He was alert. And he was hungry.

The hunger was not a gentle rumble. It was a void. It felt as though the stomach he had inhabited for nineteen years had been replaced by a furnace that demanded fuel. He looked at the small bag of rice on the counter, the can of beans, the loaf of bread. They were his dinner. They were his survival.

He checked his wallet. Two dollars.

The trial required him at the clinic by eight. The medication had to be taken on an empty stomach, or so the instructions implied, though the real instruction was simply: *at 0800 hours*. If he ate now, he would have to spend money he did not have. If he skipped the meal, he risked the faintness that had plagued him before the medication had started to change things.

He stood up. His legs did not shake. That was the unsettling part. Before, he would have gripped the bedframe, his heart hammering against his ribs. Now, he simply stood. The muscles in his calves tightened, ready, efficient. He felt a surge of strength that seemed disproportionate to his frame. He flexed his fingers. They moved with a precision that felt alien, like he was operating a machine he had only just learned how to drive.

He grabbed his jacket. It felt loose, the fabric sliding over skin that had gained a subtle, lean density. He stepped out into the hallway. The floorboards creaked under his feet, a sound that he heard with startling clarity. He could hear the muffled thud of a neighbor’s television through the wall. He could hear the distant hum of the city waking up.

The walk to the clinic was a blur of sensory input. The air was cold, but he did not shiver. His body generated its own warmth, a steady, internal furnace. He walked fast, his stride long and fluid. He was not running, but he was moving with a speed that made the people on the sidewalk stumble out of his way. He noticed their faces, the lines of their mouths, the specific shade of their coats. He noticed the crack in the pavement, the way the light hit the edge of a parked car.

By the time he reached the clinic, his heart rate was steady. His breathing was controlled. But the hunger was a physical pain, a sharp, stabbing ache that made his vision narrow. He swallowed, forcing his throat to relax. He was not going to faint. He had no energy to waste on fainting.

He entered the lobby. The air conditioning was cold, a sterile chill that raised goosebumps on his arms. He walked to the intake desk. The Intake Coordinator was there, a woman with a face that seemed to have been smoothed by years of indifference. She did not look up.

"Adrian Vale," she said. Her voice was flat.

"Yes," he replied.

"Medication."

He nodded. He walked to the dispenser. A small, clear pill sat on a white tray. He took it, placed it in his mouth, and swallowed it with a sip of water from the fountain. The pill dissolved quickly, a neutral taste that left no lingering flavor. He felt it begin to work almost immediately, a subtle shift in his perception, a sharpening of the edges of the world.

He waited. The hunger was growing. It was a living thing, pressing against the walls of his stomach. He closed his eyes for a second, focusing on the strength in his legs, the clarity in his mind. He was not weak. He was not the boy who had signed the forms in desperation. He was something else. Something that was still forming.

"Room two," the Coordinator said.

He walked to Room two. The door was white, seamless, with no handle, only a keycard reader. He swiped his ID. The door slid open with a soft hiss.

Inside, the room was small, sterile. A bed. A tray. A cup.

He lay down. The mattress was firm, unyielding. He pulled up his pants. The process was mechanical. He did not feel shame. He did not feel disgust. He felt a detached curiosity, as if he were observing a procedure being performed on a mannequin. His hands moved with a steady, controlled rhythm. The pleasure was there, but it was distant, secondary to the physical sensation of the release, the ejection of the fluid into the sterile plastic cup.

He sealed the cup. He placed it in the bin.

He stood up. His legs were strong. He felt a surge of energy, a burst of vitality that made him want to move, to run, to do something with the excess power coursing through his veins. He gripped the edge of the bed to steady himself. He was breathing hard, but not from exertion. From anticipation.

He walked out of the room.

The man in the white coat was waiting at the counter. He had his clipboard. He was looking at a screen.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited. The hunger was a pit in his stomach, but his mind was sharp. He watched the man’s hands. They were still.

The man looked up. His eyes were focused, not on Adrian, but on the data. He typed something. His fingers moved quickly, efficiently.

"Sample quality is within parameters," he said. His voice was neutral, flat. "Significant deviation in cell count. Noted."

Adrian blinked. *Significant deviation?*

"Is that… is that bad?" Adrian asked. His voice sounded strange to his own ears, deeper, more resonant.

The man looked up. His eyes were tired. "It is outside the expected range for the third day of treatment. It is noted."

He slid the envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. He felt the weight of the cash, the tangible reality of it. He put it in his pocket.

"You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours," the man said. He was already turning back to his screen. "Do not be late."

Adrian walked out of the clinic. The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass.

He felt a strange, fleeting sense of worth. Not pride. Not happiness. But a sense of recognition. He had been seen. His body had been measured, and it had been found to be *different*.

He walked to the bus stop. The hunger was back, a low, persistent rumble. He had the fourteen dollars. He had the bus fare.

He boarded the bus. The metal pole was cold against his hand. He pulled out the envelope and counted the bills. Fourteen dollars.

He put the money in his wallet and closed it.

He looked out the window as the bus pulled away, the city blurring past in a streak of light and shadow.

The next day was different.

He had a shift at the diner. It started at nine in the morning. The trial started at eight.

He woke up at five. He was awake before the alarm.

He ate nothing. He had no money for food. He had spent the four dollars on the bus fare and a small, stale pastry that he had eaten on the bus.

He walked to the clinic. The hunger was a sharp, stabbing pain in his gut. It was not a comfortable ache. It was a warning.

He took the medication. He went to room two.

The process was the same. Smooth. Controlled. Efficient.

He sealed the cup. He placed it in the bin.

He walked out.

The man in the white coat was there.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

The man looked at his screen. He frowned.

"Variance in cell count," he said. His voice was still neutral, but there was a note of something else. Curiosity? Concern?

Adrian’s stomach clenched. "Is that… is that bad?"

The man looked up. His eyes were tired. "It is within the acceptable range for the fourth day of treatment. It is noted."

He slid the envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. His hands were steady.

He walked out of the clinic. The hunger was a living thing, a pit in his stomach that seemed to expand with every second.

He walked to the diner. He worked his shift. He flipped burgers, wiped tables, took orders. His body was a machine, and he was the operator.

When his shift ended, he had six dollars in his pocket.

He walked to the grocery store. He stood in front of the shelves, looking at the food. The hunger was a demand. He needed fuel.

He picked up a bag of rice. Two dollars. He picked up a can of beans. One dollar. He picked up a loaf of bread. One dollar.

Four dollars.

He had two dollars left.

He walked home. He cooked the rice. He heated the beans. He ate.

The hunger was satisfied. For now.

He sat on his bed, his head in his hands. He felt the heat in his chest, the low, persistent burn. He felt the strength in his muscles, the clarity in his mind.

He didn’t know what the medication was doing to him. He didn’t know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn’t know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He lay down. He closed his eyes.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

The clinical smell hit Adrian before the automatic doors finished opening—antiseptic and something else, something cleaner and colder than any hospital he'd ever visited. The research clinic occupied the third floor of the university-affiliated medical building, a place he'd walked past dozens of times without ever imagining he'd become a regular visitor.

He was early. He'd left his apartment at 8:15 AM, unable to sleep through hunger, and taken the bus downtown with the four hundred dollars safe in his backpack. The money felt heavy, significant. Real.

The waiting area was empty except for a receptionist behind a curved desk, her attention fixed on a computer screen. The walls were pale blue, the furniture modern and uncomfortable-looking. A flat-screen television mounted on one wall displayed a silent news channel, the anchor's mouth opening and closing in soundless commentary.

Adrian sat in one of the plastic chairs and pulled out the card Thorne had given him. His testing appointment was at 9:00 AM. He had forty-five minutes to wait.

His stomach cramped again. He'd skipped breakfast, following the instructions not to eat or drink anything beforehand. The cafeteria wouldn't have leftovers for hours, and his food budget was already stretched thin. The four hundred dollars was reserved for rent. That was its only purpose. Everything else—food, bus fare, the small supplies he needed for classes—would have to come from the stipend payments he'd receive monthly.

He needed this trial to work.

The receptionist glanced at him, offered a professional smile that didn't reach her eyes, and returned her attention to her screen. Adrian folded the card and tucked it back into his pocket.

The silence was oppressive. He pulled out his phone, scrolled through his messages. Nothing from his roommate. Nothing from anyone. He was alone in this, as he'd been alone in everything else.

At 8:50 AM, a nurse appeared through a door marked "STAFF ONLY." She was in her late thirties, with dark hair pulled back in a tight bun and eyes that swept the waiting area with practiced efficiency. Her scrubs were navy blue, her shoes sensible and white.

"Mr. Vale?" she called.

Adrian stood. "Yes."

"Follow me. I'm Elena. I'll be conducting your daily testing."

He followed her through the staff door into a narrow hallway lined with examination rooms. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, casting everything in a clinical white glow. Elena walked quickly, her steps silent on the linoleum floor.

"Room three," she said, opening a door on the left. "Inside, please. Remove everything except your underwear and put it in the locker. Lock the door and keep the key with you."

Adrian stepped into the examination room. It was small, sterile, equipped with a padded table in the center and a small counter along one wall. A specimen cup sat on the counter, next to a bottle of lubricant and a small stack of labels.

He undressed quickly, folding his clothes and placing them in the locker. The room was cold, and he shivered as he locked the door. His key was a simple plastic tag with the number three on it.

When he was ready, he stood next to the examination table, uncertain.

Elena entered without knocking. "On the table, please. Feet in the stirrups."

Adrian climbed onto the table, positioned his feet in the cold metal stirrups, and lay back against the paper-covered surface. The position was vulnerable, humiliating. He stared at the ceiling, at the fluorescent light fixture overhead, and tried not to think about what was coming.

"You've done this before?" Elena asked, her tone matter-of-fact.

"Never," Adrian said.

"Alright. I'll walk you through it. The specimen cup is on the counter. You'll need to produce a sample. Take as long as you need, but we have a schedule to maintain, so please work efficiently."

Adrian nodded. His face was hot with embarrassment, but he forced himself to stay still.

"This is the fertility trial, correct?" Elena continued, pulling on a pair of gloves.

"Yes."

"Alright. Once you've provided the sample, I'll need to collect it and prepare it for analysis. The process takes about fifteen minutes total. Any questions?"

"No."

"Good. I'll be right outside if you need anything. Take your time, but not too much time."

She left the room, closing the door behind her.

Adrian lay on the table, staring at the ceiling, and tried to focus. His mind kept drifting to the money, to the rent he could now pay, to the food he could buy later. He tried to think about anything except the clinical room, the cold metal stirrups, the specimen cup on the counter.

It took longer than he'd hoped.

When he was finished, he carefully placed the cup on the counter and called for Elena.

She returned, her expression unchanged. "Excellent. Now, I need to collect the sample and prepare it for analysis. This will take a few minutes. Stay on the table, please."

She worked efficiently, transferring the sample into labeled vials, noting measurements on a tablet, sealing containers. Her movements were practiced, detached. She treated him like a specimen, like data, like nothing more than a biological resource.

"Alright," she said finally. "You can get dressed now. You'll need to return tomorrow at the same time. Same procedure. Your payment will be processed at the end of the month. Do you have any questions?"

"No," Adrian said.

"Good. Tomorrow, same time. Don't eat or drink beforehand. Bring your key."

She left the room.

Adrian dressed quickly, his hands shaking slightly. The procedure had been clinical, professional, but it had also been deeply invasive. He felt violated, exposed, reduced to nothing more than a source of biological material.

But he'd done it. He'd completed his first day.

He left the examination room, returned his key to the receptionist, and walked out of the clinic into the bright morning sun.

The walk home took thirty minutes. Adrian's stomach cramped with hunger, but he didn't stop for food. He couldn't afford to yet. The four hundred dollars was for rent. The stipend payments would come later. He'd make it work.

When he reached his apartment, he climbed the stairs to the third floor, unlocked the door, and stepped inside.

The apartment was small, barely large enough for one person, but it was his. His roommate was still asleep, snoring loudly from the other room. Adrian didn't mind. He was used to being alone.

He sat on his bed, pulled out his phone, and stared at the screen.

He'd done it. He'd completed his first day.

The cost felt higher than the price tag, but he'd already signed. He was committed now.

Adrian lay back on the bed, closed his eyes, and tried not to think about what he'd just done. Tried not to think about the humiliation, the invasion, the reduction of his body to a resource.

He needed the money. That was all that mattered.

He'd survive.

He always did.

---

The days blurred together after that. Adrian fell into a routine—wake up at 7:30 AM, skip breakfast, take the bus to the clinic, endure the daily testing, return home, eat whatever food he could afford, sleep, repeat.

The procedure became familiar, which somehow made it worse. The clinical detachment of the nurses, the sterile examination rooms, the specimen cups—it all became routine, which meant it became normalized. Adrian found himself going through the motions without thinking, his body responding to the schedule like a machine.

His body began to change.

The first sign was subtle. He woke up one morning feeling less exhausted than usual. The chronic fatigue that had dogged him for months seemed to lift, just slightly, like fog burning away in the sun.

He didn't think much of it. Maybe the trial medication was helping. Maybe his body was adjusting to the routine.

The second sign was more noticeable. During his daily testing, he produced more sample than usual. Elena didn't comment, but she made a note on her tablet. Adrian saw the number—5.2 milliliters—and compared it to the baseline he'd provided during his screening. The increase was significant.

"Is this normal?" he asked.

"Variations are expected," Elena said, her tone clinical. "The medication is designed to increase production. This is a positive sign."

Adrian nodded. Positive for the trial. Positive for the data they were collecting. Positive for the research that would eventually make him obsolete.

But the changes continued.

His appetite increased. He found himself hungry all the time, his stomach cramping with need even when he'd eaten recently. The four hundred dollars he had started with was gone in a week—spent on food, on bus fare, on the small supplies he needed for classes.

He was running out of money again.

At the end of the second week, Elena called him into a small office after his testing session.

"Your compensation for completed sessions has been deposited," she said. "You'll receive another payment at the end of the month."

"How much?" Adrian asked.

"$300. For the sessions you've completed."

Adrian did the math. $300 a month, $400 up front. It wasn't enough. It would cover his rent and food, barely, but nothing else. No money for books, no money for emergencies, no money for anything beyond survival.

"I need more," he said.

Elena's expression remained neutral. "The payment structure is set by the trial protocol. You'll receive additional compensation if you complete all scheduled sessions. That's the agreement."

"I know, but—"

"You signed the consent form. The terms are clear."

Adrian left the office, frustrated and desperate. He needed more money. He needed to find a way to make this work.

The third week brought another change.

Adrian was walking home from the clinic, his stomach cramping with hunger, when he heard a woman's scream.

It came from an alley between two buildings, sharp and terrified. Adrian froze, uncertain. He'd never been in a fight. He'd never been in any kind of danger. He was a broke college student, not a hero.

But the scream came again, and this time it was followed by a man's voice, angry and aggressive.

Adrian ran toward the sound.

In the alley, a woman was pressed against the wall by a large man. She was young, maybe in her twenties, with dark hair and a bruise forming on her cheek. The man held her wrist, squeezing, his other hand raised to strike.

"Please," the woman said. "I don't have any money. I'm not working today."

"I don't care," the man growled. "You owe me. You think you can just walk away?"

Adrian didn't think. He ran forward, grabbed the man's shoulder, and pulled.

The man turned, his fist swinging. Adrian ducked, more by instinct than intention, and the punch missed. He stumbled backward, tripped over his own feet, and fell hard against the alley floor.

The man loomed over him, fists raised. "You want some too?"

Adrian's heart hammered in his chest. He was terrified, certain he was about to be beaten senseless. But something in his body responded to the threat. His muscles tensed, his breathing steadied, and when the man's fist came at him again, Adrian moved.

He didn't think about it. He just reacted.

His hand shot up, catching the man's wrist. The impact was solid, painful, but effective. The man grunted, off-balance, and Adrian pushed, hard, driving the man backward.

The attacker stumbled, fell, hit the ground with a heavy thud.

Adrian stood, breathing hard, his body thrumming with adrenaline. The woman stared at him, eyes wide.

"Thank you," she said, her voice shaking. "I—thank you."

"Get out of here," Adrian said. "Go. Now."

She ran.

The man on the ground groaned, started to push himself up. Adrian didn't wait. He ran from the alley, his heart pounding, his body shaking.

He made it three blocks before his legs gave out.

He collapsed against a wall, gasping for breath, his whole body trembling. He'd just fought someone. He'd just defended a stranger. He'd just done something brave and stupid and terrifying.

His hand hurt where he'd caught the man's wrist. His ribs ached from hitting the ground. But he was alive.

And beneath the fear and exhaustion, he felt something else.

Pride.

---

Adrian walked home slowly, his body aching, his mind racing. He'd defended someone. He'd protected a stranger. He'd done something that mattered.

When he reached his apartment, he climbed the stairs, unlocked the door, and stepped inside.

His roommate was in the kitchen, eating cereal.

"Hey," the roommate said. "You look like shit."

"Long day," Adrian said.

He went to his room, closed the door, and sat on his bed.

His hand still hurt. His ribs still ached. But he'd done it. He'd fought back.

The changes in his body were real. The increased strength, the faster reflexes, the improved endurance—it was all happening. He could feel it.

He just didn't know why.

Adrian lay back on the bed, closed his eyes, and tried not to think about what was coming next.

But he knew, deep down, that this was only the beginning.

The trial would continue. The changes would accelerate. And eventually, someone would notice what was happening to him.

He just hoped he'd be ready when they did.

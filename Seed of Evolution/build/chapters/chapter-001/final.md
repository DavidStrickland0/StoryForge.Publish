# Chapter 1: The Price of Survival

The waiting room smelled of antiseptic and stale coffee. Adrian sat on the hard plastic chair, his backpack wedged between his feet, and tried not to think about how hungry he was. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead, casting everything in a sickly yellow-white that made the scratches on the reception desk more visible.

His stomach cramped. He'd eaten breakfast at 6:30—a packet of oatmeal from the dollar store, the kind that came in a paper envelope and required boiling water. He’d used the last of his prepaid electricity credit to heat the pot on the electric stove, a small, calculated risk that left him with barely enough power for the rest of the week. The cafeteria would have leftovers after the lunch rush, but that was hours away. His shift at the convenience store didn't start until 4 PM, which meant another six hours of running on empty.

The rent was due in five days. Tuition was due in ten.

Adrian pulled out his phone and checked the university portal again, even though he'd checked it three times already this morning. The balance remained the same: $2,487.63. His financial aid had covered most of it, but there was still a gap. A gap he couldn't afford.

The flyer was still in his wallet, though he'd read it so many times the edges were soft and worn. *Experimental Medicine Program: Participants Needed. Competitive Compensation. Flexible Scheduling. University Affiliated Research Hospital.*

Flexible scheduling meant he could work around his classes and his job. Competitive compensation meant they paid well enough to cover his remaining expenses. University affiliated meant it was legitimate, not some sketchy online survey scam.

It also meant they wanted people like him—people desperate enough to sign up for something they didn't fully understand.

"Adrian Vale?" The receptionist's voice cut through his thoughts. She was middle-aged, with gray-streaked hair pulled back in a tight bun, and she held a clipboard.

"Yes." Adrian stood too quickly, nearly tripping over his backpack.

"The examination room down the hall. Dr. Thorne will see you now."

The hallway was narrow, lined with identical doors and the same fluorescent lighting. Adrian walked until the receptionist pointed to a door on the left. His hand shook slightly as he knocked.

"Come in." The voice was male, clipped, efficient.

Dr. Thorne sat behind a desk covered in neatly organized stacks of papers and a tablet. He was younger than Adrian had expected, maybe thirty-five, with dark hair and wire-rimmed glasses. His white coat hung on a hook behind the door. He didn't smile.

"Mr. Vale. Please, sit." Thorne gestured to the chair across from the desk. "I'm Dr. Aris Thorne, coordinator of the experimental medicine program. You filled out the preliminary application?"

"Yes, sir." Adrian sat, gripping his backpack straps.

Thorne pulled up something on his tablet, swiping through several screens. "You're a freshman. Biology major. Part-time employment at a convenience store. Renting an off-campus apartment. Is that correct?"

"Yes, sir."

"And you're interested in participating in our male fertility trial?"

Adrian's throat tightened. The flyer had been vague about specifics, but the application form had been brutally direct. *Daily specimen collection. Medication administration. Regular monitoring. Invasive procedures.*

He'd filled it out anyway.

"It pays well," Adrian said, which was true. The compensation structure was detailed in the materials: a base stipend of $400 per month, plus an additional $50 for each completed testing session. With daily sessions, that would amount to nearly two thousand dollars a month.

Thorne set down his tablet. "Let me be clear about what we're offering and what we're asking for. This is a research trial. We're testing a compound designed to increase sperm count and motility in males with suboptimal fertility parameters. You qualify based on your age and baseline health metrics."

"I read the materials."

"Good. Because the requirements are extensive." Thorne pulled a thick document from one of his organized stacks. "This is the consent form. You'll need to review it carefully. The trial requires daily visits to our facility for specimen collection. You'll be taking the experimental medication twice daily. We'll be monitoring your blood work, your hormone levels, your overall health."

"How long?"

"Six months minimum. Potentially longer if the trial is extended."

Adrian's heart sank. Six months. But even divided across six months, the money was enough. More than enough.

"And the daily visits?" he asked. "What do they involve?"

Thorne's expression didn't change. "Specimen collection. You'll be providing samples for analysis. Daily. Here. We have a dedicated facility for that."

The way he said it, the clinical detachment in his voice, made Adrian's skin crawl. But he didn't have a choice.

"Is there anything in the consent form I should know about?" Adrian asked. "Any risks?"

"The medication is relatively new. We don't have long-term data. Side effects may include headaches, nausea, changes in mood or appetite. The specimen collection is non-invasive but requires regular timing. You'll need to be here at the same time each day."

"What if I can't make it?"

"Non-compliance results in termination from the trial and forfeiture of all compensation. You'll also be responsible for returning any payments received."

The threat was clear. Adrian nodded.

Thorne slid the consent form across the desk. It was at least twenty pages long, printed in dense legal language. Adrian took it, his hands still shaking.

"You should read it thoroughly," Thorne said. "Take your time. I'll be in the next room if you have questions."

He left, closing the door behind him.

Adrian sat alone with the consent form. The first page was all legal definitions and disclaimers. The second page listed the trial's purpose and methodology. The third page detailed the medication's known effects and potential side effects.

Page four was where it got explicit.

*Daily specimen collection will involve masturbation to ejaculation with collection of semen into provided specimen cup. Procedure must be completed within designated time window. Specimens will be analyzed for sperm count, motility, morphology, and biochemical parameters. Participant will be expected to provide samples regardless of personal discomfort or embarrassment.*

Adrian's face burned. He'd known it would be something like this—the application had been clear enough—but seeing it written down, formalized, made it real in a way he hadn't anticipated.

He turned the page. More legal language. Confidentiality clauses. Liability waivers. Terms of payment.

The money was enough to cover his rent. His tuition. His food. Everything he needed to survive this year.

But the cost felt higher than the price tag.

Adrian read through the rest of the document, though his mind kept drifting. The language was careful, clinical, designed to make the invasive requirements sound routine. By page eleven, he was barely processing the words anymore. He just needed to sign. He needed the money.

He stood, opened the door, and found Thorne at a desk in the outer office, typing on his computer.

"I've read it," Adrian said. "I have questions."

"Of course." Thorne turned his chair to face him. "What would you like to know?"

"Is there any chance the medication could... I don't know. Change me? Make me different?"

Thorne's expression remained neutral. "We're studying its effects on fertility parameters. We don't anticipate systemic changes beyond that. Why do you ask?"

"I just... I've never done anything like this before."

"I understand. This is a significant commitment. But I can assure you, the trial is well-regulated. Your safety is our primary concern."

That wasn't quite true, Adrian thought. His safety was a concern, but data collection was clearly the priority. He was a participant, not a patient.

"Can I think about it?" Adrian asked. "Come back tomorrow?"

Thorne's fingers paused over his keyboard. "The trial has limited slots. If you're interested, I'd recommend making a decision today."

The pressure was subtle but effective. Adrian looked at the consent form in his hands, at the dense pages he'd barely absorbed, at the man who held all the power in this transaction.

"Okay," Adrian said. "I'll do it."

Thorne smiled for the first time—a small, professional expression that didn't reach his eyes. "Excellent. I'll need you to sign the consent form, and then we'll get you set up with your first payment and the medication schedule."

He pulled out a pen and slid it across the desk.

Adrian signed his name on the signature line. His handwriting was shakier than usual. He signed again on the next page, and the next, and the next, until he'd initialed every clause and signed every required line.

When he was finished, Thorne took the form and the pen. "Welcome to the trial, Mr. Vale. You'll be starting tomorrow. Here's your medication schedule and your testing appointment times." He handed Adrian a printed card. "Report to the third floor, the specimen collection room, at 9 AM sharp. Don't eat or drink anything beforehand. And bring this card with you."

"Thank you," Adrian said, though he wasn't sure what he was thanking him for.

"One more thing." Thorne pulled out an envelope from his desk drawer. "Your first month's base stipend. $400, as promised. You'll receive the additional compensation for each completed testing session via direct deposit on the first of the month."

Adrian took the envelope. It was heavy with cash. Real money. Enough to cover his rent.

"Thank you," he said again.

He left the office, the envelope clutched in his hand, and walked back down the narrow hallway. The waiting room was empty now. The receptionist had left her desk.

Outside, the afternoon sun was brutal, turning the asphalt parking lot into a shimmering heat mirage. Adrian sat on the curb, pulled out the envelope, and counted the money. Four crisp hundred-dollar bills.

He could pay his rent. He could buy groceries. He could survive.

The cost felt higher than the price tag, but he'd already signed. He was committed now.

Adrian stood, tucked the envelope into his backpack, and started the long walk home. His stomach cramped again, reminding him he still hadn't eaten. The cafeteria wouldn't have leftovers for another three hours.

He'd have to wait. He was good at waiting.

But tomorrow, he thought, he'd start earning his money. Tomorrow, he'd walk into the specimen collection room and begin the daily routine that would keep him alive.

Tomorrow, he'd discover what he'd really signed up for.

# Chapter 5: The Hum

The alarm clock didn’t wake Adrian. He was already awake, his eyes open to the dark ceiling of his apartment, his heart beating with a rhythmic, forceful strength that felt alien in his chest. Yesterday, this moment had been a dread, a heavy stone settling in his gut before the day even began. Today, it was a spark.

He sat up, and the movement was fluid, silent. The joints that had creaked with the dull ache of chronic fatigue the night before were quiet. He swung his legs over the side of the bed, his feet touching the cold floor. The sensation was sharp, distinct, not the numb, drifting touch of exhaustion he’d grown used to.

He walked to the tiny kitchenette, the linoleum cool under his bare feet. The apartment was silent, but his senses were loud. He could hear the hum of the refrigerator, a low, steady vibration that he had never previously noticed. He could smell the faint, stale scent of the discount noodles he’d eaten for dinner, a ghost of garlic and oil that hung in the air.

His stomach growled. It wasn’t a hollow rumble, not the polite, empty sound of a skipped meal. It was a demand. A hollow, grinding void that pulled at his insides, a physical ache that insisted on fuel.

He looked at the empty refrigerator. The shelves were bare save for a single, bruised apple and a jar of pickles. He touched the apple. It was firm, cold.

*Eat it*, his body said. *You need it. You need to be strong for the collection.*

*But that’s four dollars*, his mind countered. *Four dollars is half a day’s pay. Four dollars is the difference between the electricity bill being paid and the lights going out.*

He stared at the apple for a long time. The hunger was a living thing, a pit in his stomach that seemed to expand with every second. He could feel the heat rising in his blood, that persistent, low-level burn that the medication had left in him. It was a fever without a temperature, a fire without a flame.

He closed his eyes. He thought of the bus fare. He thought of the rent, the three days until it was due, the sixty dollars for the electricity that was already overdue.

He opened the refrigerator door. He took the apple. He ate it in three bites, the juice running down his chin. It wasn’t enough. It was never enough. But it was something.

He swallowed the last piece, the seedless flesh dissolving on his tongue. The hunger didn’t vanish, but it receded, just enough to let him breathe. He washed the apple core in the sink, the water cold against his hands, and placed it in the trash.

He dressed. The movement was effortless. He didn’t feel tired. He didn’t feel heavy. He felt light, wired, a tension in his muscles that was not pain, but potential.

He walked to the bus stop. The air was crisp, carrying the scent of wet pavement and distant exhaust. He could see the individual drops of rain on the leaves of the tree across the street, each one a perfect, suspended sphere. He could hear the distant wail of a siren, the rhythmic thud of a jackhammer three blocks away. The world was sharper, more vivid, as if a layer of fog had been lifted.

He boarded the bus, his hand gripping the cold metal pole. The other passengers were a blur of gray coats and tired faces. They looked heavy, their movements sluggish. Adrian felt like a current running through a stagnant pool.

The ride took forty minutes. He didn’t check the time. He didn’t need to. He knew when he was there.

The clinic was the same as yesterday. The sterile white walls, the humming fluorescent lights, the smell of antiseptic and plastic. But today, the environment felt different. The hum of the ventilation system was a low, constant drone that he could feel in his teeth. The light was too bright, a harsh glare that made his eyes water.

He walked to the counter. The man in the white coat was there, his back to Adrian, typing on a keyboard. The sound of the keys was a rapid, mechanical click.

"Good morning," Adrian said. His voice was steady. He was surprised by the lack of tremor in it.

The man turned. His eyes were tired, the skin under them dark and thin. "Vale. You are on time."

"Always," Adrian said.

He didn’t say it to be defensive. He said it because it was true. He had been awake since four in the morning. He had been waiting.

The man slid a small, clear pill across the counter. It was identical to the one from yesterday. Adrian took it, placed it in his mouth, and swallowed it with a sip of water from the dispenser. The pill was dry, gritty, and tasted of nothing.

"Go to room two," the man said.

Adrian nodded. He walked down the hallway, his footsteps silent on the vinyl floor. The door to room two was closed. He pushed it open.

The room was small, sterile, and empty. A chair stood in the center, a small table beside it. On the table was a sealed, sterile cup, identical to the one he had used yesterday.

He sat down. The chair was hard, cold. He waited.

The door opened. The man in the white coat entered. He carried a clipboard.

"Begin," he said.

Adrian stood. He moved to the table. He picked up the cup. It was light, plastic, and cold.

Yesterday, this moment had been a battle. A war against his own body, against his own shame. He had had to force himself, to push through the exhaustion, the nausea, the feeling of being less than human.

Today, it was different.

He didn’t feel shame. He didn’t feel exhaustion. He felt a strange, detached clarity. His body was a machine, and he was the operator. The process was simple. Mechanical.

He began. The movement was smooth, controlled. There was no struggle, no pause. His body responded with an efficiency that surprised him. The heat in his chest was a constant, a background hum that didn’t interfere, but amplified.

It took less time. Much less time.

When it was done, he sat back down, his breathing steady. He didn’t shake. He didn’t sweat.

He sealed the cup. His hands were steady. He placed it in the designated bin, the slot swallowing the plastic with a soft *thud*.

He walked out of the room.

The man in the white coat was waiting at the counter. He had his clipboard. He was looking at a screen.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

The man looked up. His eyes were focused, not on Adrian, but on the data. He typed something.

"Sample quality is within parameters," he said. His voice was neutral, flat. "Slight variance in motility. Noted as positive."

Adrian blinked. *Positive?*

"Thank you," he said.

The man slid an envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. He felt the weight of the cash, the tangible reality of it.

"You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours," the man said. He was already turning back to his screen. "Do not be late."

Adrian walked out of the clinic. The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass.

He felt a strange, fleeting sense of worth. Not pride. Not happiness. But a sense of recognition. He had been seen. His body had been measured, and it had been found to be *good*.

He walked to the bus stop. The hunger was back, a low, persistent rumble. He had the fourteen dollars. He had the apple. He had the bus fare.

He boarded the bus. The metal pole was cold against his hand. He pulled out the envelope and counted the bills. Fourteen dollars.

He put the money in his wallet and closed it.

He looked out the window as the bus pulled away, the city blurring past in a streak of light and shadow.

The next day was different.

He had a shift at the diner. It started at nine in the morning. The trial started at eight.

He woke up at five. He was awake before the alarm.

He ate nothing. He had no money for food. He had spent the four dollars on the bus fare and a small, stale pastry that he had eaten on the bus.

He walked to the clinic. The hunger was a sharp, stabbing pain in his gut. It was not a comfortable ache. It was a warning.

He took the medication. He went to room two.

The process was the same. Smooth. Controlled. Efficient.

He sealed the cup. He placed it in the bin.

He walked out.

The man in the white coat was there.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

The man looked at his screen. He frowned.

"Variance in cell count," he said. His voice was still neutral, but there was a note of something else. Curiosity? Concern?

Adrian’s stomach clenched. "Is that… is that bad?"

The man looked up. His eyes were tired. "It is within the acceptable range for the second day of treatment. It is noted."

He slid the envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars."

Adrian took it. His hands were steady.

He walked out of the clinic. The hunger was a living thing, a pit in his stomach that seemed to expand with every second.

He walked to the diner. He worked his shift. He flipped burgers, wiped tables, took orders. His body was a machine, and he was the operator.

When his shift ended, he had six dollars in his pocket.

He walked to the grocery store. He stood in front of the shelves, looking at the food. The hunger was a demand. He needed fuel.

He picked up a bag of rice. Two dollars. He picked up a can of beans. One dollar. He picked up a loaf of bread. One dollar.

Four dollars.

He had two dollars left.

He walked home. He cooked the rice. He heated the beans. He ate.

The hunger was satisfied. For now.

He sat on his bed, his head in his hands. He felt the heat in his chest, the low, persistent burn. He felt the strength in his muscles, the clarity in his mind.

He didn’t know what the medication was doing to him. He didn’t know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn’t know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He lay down. He closed his eyes.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

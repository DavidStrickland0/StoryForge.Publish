# Chapter 4: The Weight of Fourteen Dollars

The fluorescent lights of the intake lobby hummed with a frequency Adrian could feel in his teeth. The air was recycled, cold, and smelled faintly of antiseptic and industrial floor wax. It was a smell that scrubbed away the scent of the outside world, the damp pavement and the stale coffee from the diner where he’d worked his last shift.

He stood in line, his hands shoved deep into the pockets of his worn jeans. His wallet was a soft, flapping thing against his hip. He knew the arithmetic of it by heart: rent was due in three days. The electricity bill had arrived yesterday, a red-stamped demand for sixty dollars. He had fourteen dollars in cash and a debit card that pointed to a checking account holding less than twenty. The trial was not just a job; it was the only thing standing between him and eviction, between him and eating the discount noodles he bought in bulk to stretch his food budget to the breaking point.

A woman with a clipboard and a lanyard that read *Intake Coordinator* pointed to a small plastic chair. "Sit down, please. Number four."

Adrian sat. The chair was hard, molded plastic, and offered no comfort. Across from him, a man in a white coat sat behind a counter that looked like it had been manufactured in a factory that specialized in gray. The man didn’t look up as Adrian approached. He tapped a key on a keyboard, the sound sharp and rhythmic in the quiet room.

"Name."

"Adrian Vale."

"Date of birth."

"March fourteenth, nineteen—"

"1999," the man interrupted, his voice flat, devoid of inflection. "We have that. You are enrolled in the male-fertility trial, Protocol 7-B. Do you understand the daily requirements?"

Adrian swallowed. The taste of bile was still at the back of his throat, a reminder of the nausea that had been building since he’d taken the first dose of the medication, a small, clear pill that had tasted like nothing, yet sat in his stomach like a stone. "Yes," he said. His voice sounded thin, reedy.

"Good. You will take the medication at 0800 hours daily. You will report to this facility for specimen collection at 1000 hours daily. Deviation from this schedule will result in a penalty deduction from your compensation. Do you understand the penalty structure?"

Adrian nodded. He didn't need to hear the specifics. He knew what happened when you missed a day. You didn't just lose the day's pay; you lost the week's. And you risked being flagged for non-compliance, which meant a review, which meant a conversation with Dr. Thorne, which meant the possibility of being cut loose. And if he was cut loose, he was done.

The man slid a small, white cup across the counter. It was sterile, sealed in plastic. "Take the cup. You will use it for collection. Return it to the designated bin after sealing. Do not open the seal until you are ready to begin. Do not contaminate the sample. Do not delay."

Adrian took the cup. It was cold to the touch. He held it in his hand, feeling the weight of it, the fragility of the plastic. It was a small object, unremarkable, but in his hand, it felt like a verdict.

"Are you ready to proceed?" the man asked.

Adrian looked at the cup. He looked at the clock on the wall. 09:45. He had fifteen minutes. His stomach churned. The medication was starting to work, or maybe it was just the fear. He felt a sudden, intense heat rise up the back of his neck, a flush that spread across his cheeks. He blinked, disoriented. The air in the room seemed to thicken, the recycled air suddenly warm against his skin.

"I'm ready," he said, though his voice cracked.

He turned and walked toward the private collection room, a small, windowless box at the end of the hall. The door was solid, with a small window that looked out into a hallway that was empty. He locked the door behind him, the click of the latch loud in the silence.

He stood there for a moment, breathing. The heat in his chest was growing, a low, persistent burn. He looked down at his hands. They were trembling. He forced them to still, pressing them against the cool metal of the door handle.

*Just do it,* he told himself. *Just get through it. Fourteen dollars a day. That’s enough. That’s enough to keep the lights on. That’s enough to keep the food coming.*

He sat on the edge of the examination table. The plastic was cold against his skin, a shock that helped ground him. He placed the cup on the table in front of him. He stared at it. The white plastic gleamed under the harsh light.

The nausea hit him then, a wave of vertigo that made the room tilt. He gripped the edge of the table, his knuckles white. His stomach twisted, a cramping pain that radiated through his abdomen. He closed his eyes, forcing his breathing to slow. In. Out. In. Out.

He was a data point. That was what he was. A source. A vessel. He was not a person to them. He was a biological mechanism, a factory for cells. And they needed the product. They needed it every day.

The heat in his body was intensifying. He could feel his pulse in his ears, a rapid, thumping rhythm that seemed to match the hum of the lights. He knew he should be worried. He knew he should be calling someone, or at least noting the symptom. But he didn't have the energy. He didn't have the time. He had a deadline.

He opened his eyes. The room was still. The cup was still.

He began.

It was a process he had dreaded, a ritual of humiliation that felt more invasive than any physical injury. He had to focus, to ignore the way his mind rebelled against the act, to ignore the way his body felt strange, hyper-aware, every nerve ending amplified. The medication was doing something to him. He could feel it in the way his thoughts were sharper, more brittle, like glass. He could feel it in the way his body heat was rising, a fever that wasn't a fever.

It took time. It took effort. And when it was done, he sat there, shaking, his face slick with sweat.

He sealed the cup, his hands moving with a mechanical precision that surprised him. He placed it in the designated bin, the slot swallowing the plastic with a soft *thud*.

He unlocked the door and stepped out into the hallway. The air was cooler now, or maybe it just felt that way because the heat inside him was receding, leaving behind a hollow, drained feeling.

He walked back to the counter. The man in the white coat was still there, still typing. He didn't look up.

"Sample received," he said.

Adrian waited.

"Payment," the man said. He slid a small envelope across the counter. "Fourteen dollars. Plus the weekly bonus for compliance. That is an additional thirty dollars. Total, forty-four dollars."

Adrian took the envelope. It was thick with cash. He felt a sudden, sharp spike of relief, so intense it was almost painful. Forty-four dollars. It was not a lot. But it was real. It was tangible. It was the difference between starving and eating. It was the difference between being kicked out and staying in.

"Thank you," Adrian said. His voice was steady now.

"You are welcome," the man said. He finally looked up, his eyes scanning Adrian’s face. There was no sympathy in them. No warmth. Just a clinical assessment. "You are due to return tomorrow at 0800 hours for medication intake and 1000 hours for collection. Do not be late."

Adrian nodded. He turned and walked out of the clinic, the envelope tucked safely into his pocket.

The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of rain. He stood on the sidewalk, watching the traffic pass. Cars sped by, their headlights cutting through the gray twilight. People hurried past, their faces set in expressions of purpose, of destination. They had places to be. They had lives.

Adrian looked down at his hands. They were still trembling. He clenched them into fists, forcing the tremor to stop.

He had done it. He had survived the first day. He had secured his income. He had kept his body.

But as he walked toward the bus stop, he felt the heat in his chest again, a low, persistent burn that didn't go away. He touched his neck, feeling the pulse that had slowed down but was still there, strong and steady.

He didn't know what the medication was doing to him. He didn't know why his body felt so different, so *aware*. He didn't know why the world seemed sharper, more vivid, as if a layer of fog had been lifted.

He just knew that he was alive. And he was paid. And that was enough.

For now, that was enough.

He boarded the bus, the metal pole cold against his hand. He pulled out the envelope and counted the bills. Fourteen dollars. Thirty dollars. Forty-four dollars.

He put the money in his wallet and closed it.

He looked out the window as the bus pulled away, the city blurring past in a streak of light and shadow. He didn't know what tomorrow would bring. He didn't know if he would make it through the week. He didn't know if he would ever be able to afford a meal that wasn't just enough to keep him alive.

But he knew that he had made a choice. He had sold a part of himself for survival. And he had no choice but to keep doing it.

The bus hissed to a stop at the next corner. Adrian stood, his legs feeling unsteady. He stepped off, his feet hitting the pavement with a soft thud.

He walked home, his head down, his hands in his pockets. The city hummed around him, indifferent, alive.

And inside him, something else was humming too. Something quiet. Something waiting.

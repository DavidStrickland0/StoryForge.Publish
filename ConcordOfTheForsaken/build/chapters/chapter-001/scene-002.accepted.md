The sensation was not like falling. Falling implies a direction, a trajectory, and a terminal impact. This was inversion. One moment, the air in Sub-Level 4 was thick with the ozone smell of burnt copper and the heavy, wet weight of humidity that always clung to the concrete. The next, the air was thin, dry, and tasted of iron and crushed lavender.

Adrian’s knees buckled. He hit the ground hard, the impact jarring up through his spine and into his teeth. The polished floor beneath him was not marble. It was stone, rough-hewn and gray, with deep gouges in its surface where scuff marks had worn the grain away over centuries. It was cold, a biting chill that seeped through his work boots and settled into his bones, but it was a clean cold. It lacked the damp, fungal rot of the subway tunnels. It smelled of dust, old wood, and the faint, cloying scent of beeswax.

He pushed himself up onto his elbows, his hands scraping against the gritty surface. The vibration in his hands had stopped, replaced by a dull, throbbing ache in his temples. His vision was still blurred, the afterimage of the violet light persisting as a double vision that split the world into ghostly duplicates.

He looked up.

He was not alone.

The room was circular, high-ceilinged, and dimly lit by a series of tall, narrow windows that were covered with translucent, oiled parchment. The light filtering through was pale and diffused, casting long, soft shadows across the floor. There were no electrical wires, no exposed pipes, no concrete. The walls were made of dark timber beams and plaster, the wood blackened with age and soot. It looked like the interior of a very old, very expensive house, or perhaps a church that had been repurposed for something more mundane.

Around the perimeter of the room, seated on a curved bench of carved oak, were seven figures.

They did not look like the council of elders he had seen in movies. They did not wear flowing robes of gold and silk. They were dressed in the practical, severe attire of a bureaucracy. High-collared coats of dark grey wool, stiff with starch and formality. Several wore glasses with thick, round lenses. Others had paper clips in their hair or small, leather-bound notebooks resting on their knees. One was adjusting a quill pen with the meticulous care of a surgeon.

They were all looking at him.

There was no anger in their expressions. There was no surprise. There was a profound, exhausted boredom, the kind of fatigue that comes from having heard the same story a thousand times and expecting the next variation to be equally tedious.

Adrian scrambled to his feet, his legs still shaky. He brushed the dust from his cargo pants, a habitual action that felt absurdly mundane in this setting. He looked down at himself. His maintenance uniform was intact. The faded navy shirt, the reinforced knees, the heavy boots. His tool belt was still around his waist, the pliers and Teflon tape jangling softly against his hip. He felt a strange, detached humor. He had come from a world of steel and electricity to a world of wood and parchment, still wearing a shirt that had a small, faded logo of the Metro-Maintenance Authority on the chest.

He looked up at the seven figures.

The one in the center was a woman. She was older, her hair a silver streaked with black, pulled back tightly into a bun. She wore no glasses, but her eyes were sharp, pale blue, and utterly devoid of warmth. She held a scroll of parchment in her hands, the edges yellowed and brittle. She did not look like a queen. She looked like a department head who was waiting for a meeting to end so she could go home.

"Subject," she said. Her voice was clear, carrying easily through the quiet room. It was not a shout, but it had the weight of a gavel strike. "You are awake."

Adrian opened his mouth. No sound came out. He cleared his throat, feeling the dryness of the air in his throat. "Where... where am I?"

The woman’s expression did not change. She glanced at the scroll in her hands, then back at him. "Avarra. The capital of the Kingdom of Avarra. You are in the Hall of Determination."

"Avarra," Adrian repeated. The word felt foreign in his mouth, heavy and syllabic. "I was... I was in a junction box. Sub-Level 4. I was fixing a hydraulic seal."

The woman to his left, a man with a thick mustache and a ledger in front of him, made a small, dismissive sound. He flipped a page in his ledger, the sound of the paper crisp and loud in the silence.

"The origin point is irrelevant," the central woman said. "The ritual is complete. You are here. That is the only fact that matters."

Adrian looked around the room again. He noticed the details now. The way the light caught the dust motes dancing in the air. The intricate carvings on the bench, depicting scenes of labor, of construction, of maintenance. There were no dragons, no knights, no magical symbols. There were images of gears, of levers, of hands working. It was a subtle, ironic decoration for a hall of judgment.

"Why am I here?" Adrian asked. He tried to keep his voice steady, to project the calm authority he used when dealing with difficult tenants in the tunnels. It failed. He sounded small, young, and confused.

The woman in the center, the Council Chair, leaned forward slightly. Her gaze dropped to his hands. They were stained with oil, the nails cracked and dirty. She looked at the tool belt, at the pliers, at the clipboard he had instinctively reached for, only to find it empty.

"You were summoned," she said. "The Council of Avarra requires a hero. A champion. A savior." She paused, her eyes narrowing slightly. "The ritual selected you."

"Selected me?" Adrian blinked. "For what? I’m a maintenance worker. I fix leaks. I clear blockages. I don’t..." He gestured vaguely at the air. "Do that." He pointed to the window, then to the ceiling, then to the rest of the room.

The man with the mustache chuckled. It was a dry, humorless sound. "That is rather the point, isn't it?"

"Point?" Adrian turned to him. "What point?"

"The point," the mustached man said, tapping his quill against his ledger, "is that the prophecy is specific. It speaks of a bearer of the Way, one who walks between worlds. It does not specify that the bearer must be a warrior. Or a mage. Or a noble." He looked up, his eyes gleaming with a sudden, malicious interest. "It only specifies that they must be capable of carrying the burden."

Adrian felt a cold prickle run down his spine. "I don’t know what you’re talking about."

The Council Chair stood up. The movement was slow, deliberate. She smoothed the front of her coat, her fingers brushing against the lapel. She walked down from the raised dais, her shoes clicking softly on the stone floor. She stopped a few feet from Adrian, close enough that he could smell the scent of old paper and ink coming from her.

"You are in a new world, Adrian Vale," she said. She knew his name. She hadn’t asked for it. "You have been brought here by a power you do not understand, for a purpose you are not equipped to fulfill. But you are here. And in Avarra, the law is absolute. The council decides your role. The council decides your fate."

She reached into the pocket of her coat and pulled out a small, leather-bound book. It was thick, the cover worn smooth by use. She held it out to him.

"Sign," she said.

Adrian stared at the book. "Sign what?"

"Your acceptance. Or your refusal." She held the book steady, her expression unreadable. "But know this: there is no third option. You are here. You will be classified. You will be assigned. And you will be sent out."

Adrian looked at the book. He looked at the Council Chair. He looked at the other six figures, who were watching him with the detached curiosity of scientists observing a specimen under glass.

He was a maintenance worker. He fixed things. He didn’t accept destinies. He didn’t sign contracts. He reported tickets.

And yet, as he reached out and took the book from her hand, the weight of it in his palm felt heavier than any tool he had ever held. The air in the room seemed to tighten, the dust motes freezing in their dance. The world he knew was gone. This world, with its stone floors and its silent, judging eyes, was all that remained.

He looked at the quill pen resting on the table. He looked at his own hands, stained with the oil of the world he had just left.

And he signed.

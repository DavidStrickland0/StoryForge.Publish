The ink was still wet, glistening in the dry air, when the silence broke.

It wasn’t a sound of triumph, or even surprise. It was the soft, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of a heavy hand striking a wooden table. The vibration traveled through the stone floor and up through the soles of Caleb’s work boots, a dull, physical reminder of his own insignificance.

The Council Chair, a woman whose face was a map of fine lines and permanent skepticism, did not look at Caleb. She looked at the book. She turned a page with a fingernail that was painted a shade of red so dark it looked like dried blood.

“Caleb Ward,” she said, her voice flat and devoid of inflection. “Twenty-eight years old. Occupation: Maintenance Technician, Grade IV. Origin: Earth.” She glanced up, her eyes sliding over him with the dispassionate precision of a clerk inspecting a damaged shipment. “You have signed the Covenant of Acceptance. Do you understand the implications?”

Caleb swallowed. His throat felt like sandpaper. “I understand I’m stuck here. Is that the implication?”

A ripple of movement passed through the other council members. One of them, a man in robes of deep indigo, stifled a cough. Another, a woman with silver hair pulled back in a tight bun, closed her eyes as if in pain, or perhaps just exhaustion.

The Chair ignored them. “You are to be classified. This is a standard procedure. The magic of Avarra responds to intent, will, and potential. It will determine your Class and your Ability. You will be branded. You will be assigned. And you will be gone.”

“Gone?” Caleb asked. The word felt strange in his mouth. “Gone where?”

“Beyond the city,” the Chair said. “To the frontier. The Council no longer requires your presence here. The record must be closed.”

A heavy iron tray was rolled toward the center of the room by a servant who remained unseen in the shadows. On the tray sat a small, silver bowl filled with a liquid that shimmered with an iridescent, oily sheen. Beside it lay a stylus, made of bone, sharp as a needle.

“Step forward,” the Chair commanded.

Caleb stepped forward. His boots scraped against the stone. He felt the eyes of the council on his back, a physical weight, cold and pressing. He looked into the bowl. The liquid was still, a perfect mirror of the dim chandeliers above.

“What happens if I don’t want a class?” he asked, his voice trembling slightly. He tried to steady it. He had filed a thousand tickets for broken pipes and faulty wiring. He had stood in front of supervisors and managers and explained why a part was backordered. He could do this. He could explain.

The Chair finally looked at him. Her expression hadn’t changed. It was still that mask of bureaucratic boredom. “The classification is not a choice, Mr. Ward. It is a fact. You have been summoned. The magic has already begun to sort you. The ritual merely makes it visible.”

She gestured to the bowl. “Place your hand in the water. Hold the stylus. Touch the water with the tip. Do not speak. Do not pray. Do not resist.”

Caleb looked at the other council members. He wanted to ask for help. He wanted to ask if there was a lawyer, a union, a way to appeal this absurdity. But the High Priest, who had been silent until now, leaned forward. His voice was soft, melodic, and utterly without mercy.

“Resistance delays the result,” the High Priest said. “And delay is a luxury we do not have. The beasts on the frontier do not wait for paperwork.”

Caleb’s hands were shaking. He looked at his own palms, the oil stains still fresh, the cracks in the skin raw and red. He was a man who fixed things. He knew how things worked. Levers and pulleys, pressure and flow. This was none of that. This was something else entirely.

He picked up the bone stylus. It was cold, smooth, and heavy. He dipped his fingers into the iridescent liquid. It didn’t feel like water. It felt like mercury, thick and cold, sliding over his skin with a strange, numbing sensation.

He brought the stylus to the surface of the bowl.

For a second, nothing happened. The room was silent. The dust motes danced in the shafts of light. Caleb held his breath, his heart hammering against his ribs.

Then, the liquid in the bowl rippled.

It didn’t splash. It didn’t boil. It simply expanded, rising up the shaft of the bone stylus in a thin, perfect column. The iridescent sheen darkened, turning into a deep, bruised purple.

The High Priest’s eyes widened. The Chair’s pen stopped moving.

The column of liquid reached the tip of the stylus and froze there, a bead of dark, viscous energy. It hung suspended in the air, defying gravity, pulsing with a faint, rhythmic light.

And then it moved.

It slid down the shaft of the stylus, not toward Caleb, but away from him, flowing across the surface of the bowl and pooling at the base. The iridescent sheen faded, replaced by a dull, grayish haze. The liquid looked dead.

Caleb stared at it. “What?” he whispered.

The High Priest leaned back, his face a mask of polite disappointment. He looked at the Chair. “The resonance is… weak.”

“Read it,” the Chair said. Her voice was still flat, but there was a new edge to it, a sharpness that cut through the air.

The High Priest picked up a different book, a thick volume bound in black leather. He opened it to a random page, his eyes scanning the text with the speed of a machine.

“Class determination,” he recited, his voice echoing in the high-ceilinged hall. “Wayfarer.”

The word hung in the air, heavy and meaningless.

“Ability,” the High Priest continued. “Concord.”

Caleb blinked. “What is that supposed to mean?”

The High Priest closed the book. He didn’t look at Caleb. He looked at the Chair. “Wayfarer is the lowest rank. Below Soldier, below Mage, below even the common Laborer. It is a class for those who walk between worlds but belong to neither. And Concord…” He paused, a flicker of something—amusement, perhaps, or pity—crossing his face. “Concord is the ability to form voluntary bonds. It allows the user to link their will with another. To share intent. To synchronize action.”

He looked at Caleb then, and for the first time, his expression was clear. It was not pity. It was assessment.

“It is a support ability,” the High Priest said. “For those who have no power of their own. It is useless in combat. It is useless in siege. It is, in every practical sense, a waste of a summoning.”

The silence that followed was louder than any shout.

Caleb looked down at his hands. The iridescent liquid had vanished from the bowl, leaving only a dull, gray stain. He felt a strange hollowness in his chest, a sense of emptiness that had nothing to do with the magic and everything to do with the reality of who he was.

He was Caleb Ward. Maintenance worker. Grade IV.

He had been summoned to a world of heroes, of mages, of warriors who could level mountains. And he had been given the power to… talk to people? To hold hands? To agree with things?

The Chair stood up. She buttoned her robe, a precise, mechanical motion. She picked up the leather-bound book Caleb had signed earlier and placed it in a drawer.

“The classification is complete,” she said. She didn’t look at him. She was already turning toward the door, her heels clicking against the stone floor. “The escort will be at the gates in one hour. You are to leave the city by dusk. Do not linger. Do not cause a scene. The records will show you as a failed summoning. You are no longer our concern.”

Caleb opened his mouth. He wanted to argue. He wanted to demand a retest. He wanted to point out that he had just signed a contract, that he had rights, that this was a mistake.

But the Chair was already walking away, her back straight, her posture rigid with the absolute certainty of a system that had processed its data and found it wanting.

The High Priest watched him go, a small, tight smile playing on his lips. He looked at Caleb, then at the dead, gray liquid in the bowl, and then back to Caleb.

“Good luck, Wayfarer,” he said softly. “The frontier is a lonely place. I hope you find someone worth bonding with.”

The door closed behind the Chair. The heavy iron tray was rolled away by the unseen servant. The room was empty, save for Caleb, the High Priest, and the dust motes dancing in the light.

Caleb stood alone in the center of the Hall of Determination, his hands still stained with oil, his heart still hammering, and the weight of his own uselessness settling onto his shoulders like a stone he could never lift.

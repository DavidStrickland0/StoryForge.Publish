# Chapter 18: A Place at the Table

Elias woke because someone was arguing about a bowl.

"It has a handle," Maren said. "That is what the handle is for."

"Your palm is bandaged," Torin answered.

"My other palm is also bandaged. Perhaps you should drink it for me."

Elias opened his eyes. Morning light reached across the infirmary floor. Kaelen was still asleep on his pallet. Elara sat with her back against the wall, holding the disputed bowl while the other two conducted their disagreement over it.

She caught Elias looking and offered him a tired smile.

"Everyone is improving."

Maren took the bowl by its handle before Torin could answer. She managed one mouthful, then put it down carefully. Neither of them mentioned the small victory.

Elias sat up slowly. The new bandage pulled, but the wound beneath it felt less hot. His boots stood together at the end of the bench. Someone had scraped most of the mud from them.

He looked toward the board where they had left the map.

It was still there.

Elara followed his glance.

"They made a copy of the route last night. The bronze hasn't left the room."

"The watchers?"

"Returned before the change of watch. Another pair went out. The officer said he would tell us when both reports were in."

Elias began to swing his legs down, then stopped.

Elara watched the change without commenting on it. She held out a cup instead. The water was cool and tasted of nothing unusual.

He drank it all.

They ate before anyone came to question them. Bread, broth, and a little cheese were set on the board beside the diagrams. Torin moved the food away from the bronze before Maren could accuse him of protecting a map more carefully than a person.

Kaelen woke while Elias was fastening his boots. The mage looked first at his bare wrist, then at the folded coat beside him. The gauntlet had not moved during the night.

"How long?" he asked.

"Long enough for breakfast," Elias said.

Kaelen sat up. He listened for a moment. His expression changed when the only noises that answered were voices from the yard and Maren breaking a piece of bread badly with the sides of her hands.

"I slept through it."

"Through what?"

Kaelen looked at the coat again.

"Nothing, apparently."

He took the bread Maren offered him.

The officer arrived carrying two sheets of paper, each folded around a small sketch of the valley. He laid them beside the map and waited until the party had room to gather around.

"Both watches saw the same thing," he said. "The temple light remained steady. No new fog came out of the channels. Nothing moved from the ruins toward the ridge."

Elara put her hand on the first sketch. "They stayed above the shrine?"

"As instructed."

"And no one touched the key?"

"No one went near it."

She let herself read the rest of the report.

The watchers had recorded times, the direction of the wind, the places where fog still lay, and the shape of the barrier. One had drawn the ditch turn incorrectly. Torin pointed it out, and the officer compared it with the second sketch before amending the first.

Elias read the line about the dark silhouette in the ruins twice. It had not shifted during either watch. The second observer could see that part of its upper shape had slumped against a broken wall.

The officer looked at him.

"We are treating it as a danger until someone can inspect it properly. That does not change what the reports say about the flow."

"It no longer has a supply from the shrine," Elara said.

"That is what we're observing."

Maren shifted her bandaged hands on the table.

"So we're not going back this morning."

"You are not going back this morning," the officer confirmed.

For a while no one said anything. Elias realized he had been waiting for the order ever since he opened his eyes.

The officer unfolded the second sheet. A second list lay inside it: restrictions on entering the ruins, a schedule for observing the lower valley, and a note that the shrine's replacement key must remain in place.

"This goes to the gate and the watch commander. The route copy stays with the observation post. Elara's drawing stays with the instructions for any inspection below the ridge."

"And the original?" Elias asked.

The man looked at the bronze map.

"That is yours to decide. We need access to the marks. We don't need to take it away from you to have that."

Elias ran his thumb along the edge. He had carried it through the ruins, the forest, the road to the watchfort, and back to the Gate of Silence. The metal had become an awkward part of how he stood and slept. He did not want to hand it over because someone asked politely. He did not want to carry it merely because he could not imagine his coat without its weight.

"Let it stay while the copies are checked," he said. "Elara and Kaelen should see each one before it goes out. Then we can decide where it's kept."

Kaelen looked up from the reports. "I can check a copy."

Elias nodded. "You don't have to hear anything to do that."

The mage lowered his gaze to the engraved channels. His right hand stayed on the table rather than moving toward the gauntlet.

They spent the next part of the morning doing work that would have been intolerable the day before. They described which drawing referred to which turn. They measured distances approximately, agreeing where memory failed rather than improving it into certainty. Elara wrote that the replacement crystal arrangement was observed to close the feed after the reservoir emptied; she did not write that three shards would always work wherever a seal broke.

Torin added the marks for the safer return path. Maren dictated a warning about the loose shelf above the ditch and the need to carry enough drinking water for an injured group.

"Put that near the top," she said.

Elara did.

Elias wrote their names beneath the account. He left space for each of them to make corrections. When Torin noticed that the hand in the ditch had climbed before the second stream veered toward it, Elias changed the order.

The finished report was less elegant than a story about five people overcoming the Blight by courage. It contained a damaged key, a frightened mage, mistakes, a hot hammer handle, and a long wait while no one could be certain the next pulse would arrive.

It also contained the ending of the operation.

Elias copied that part carefully. The feed had dwindled. The temple barrier had consumed the remaining corruption. The note had been withdrawn without a surge. The opening had been narrowed and closed. The party had moved away, and independent watches had observed no return of the flow.

He put down the pen.

The officer read the page. He did not ask Elias to make the ending grander.

He did ask where the party wanted the names placed on the copy for the watch commander.

"All of them together," Elias said.

"They are together. I mean above or below the account."

Elias looked at the sheet and felt foolish. He had been ready to argue about credit when the man only needed to know how to arrange a page.

"Below," Elara said. "Where people can read the report before they decide which of us to question."

Torin took a fresh sheet and began copying the observation instructions. His hand was slower than Elara's, but the letters were clear. Maren read each line as he finished it, following the route with one bandaged knuckle above the bronze rather than touching its wet surface.

Kaelen checked the turn from the channel to the temple. He did not put the gauntlet on to do it. He pointed out where a careless copy could make the shrine look like part of the temple buildings, and Torin separated the two marks more clearly.

Elias watched the correction take shape. He remembered how easily he had begun speaking about the barrier as if it were beside them at the shrine. The light had been visible across the valley. The words had shortened the distance until his imagination no longer had to walk it.

"Put the valley between those," he said.

Torin drew the slope. Elara added a note about the crossing made by the corruption, not by people.

The officer waited while they finished. When he took the copy, he held it beside the bronze map and checked the two places for himself.

"No one goes down expecting a wall around the shrine," he said.

"Exactly," Elias answered.

A guard appeared at the infirmary door with another report from the ridge. The officer read it before passing it across the board. The daylight watch had seen the same quiet channel, the same steady temple light, and no movement out of the ruins. The observer had recorded the fog thinning further where the wind reached the streets.

Maren read the page over Elias's shoulder.

"They stayed up there?"

"They stayed up there," the officer said.

The guard hesitated before leaving. "People in the yard are saying you emptied the whole city."

Elias shook his head. "We emptied the reservoir feeding that channel. Tell them the rest of what the officer said too. The damaged streets stay closed until they've been checked."

"They'll want to know when they can go home."

The question took the pleasure out of the room. Elias had been thinking about a bench and breakfast. Other people were thinking about buildings they had left behind, possessions, places where someone might still be waiting for them.

"I can't give them a day," he said. "I won't make one up."

The officer set the new report beside the old ones. "The road is not opening on a rumor. We begin with observation, then inspection. When we know which ground is sound, we tell them."

The guard nodded and left to carry that answer rather than a victory large enough to make the ruins safe by declaration.

Elias looked at the five names beneath their account. What they had accomplished did not become smaller because it could not repair every loss. The people who wanted to go home deserved its exact size.

He checked the last paragraph once more and left it as it was.

"This will do," he said.

By midday, the five of them were permitted to leave the infirmary. The attendant checked Elias's bandage once more and made Maren repeat the instructions for her hands until she stopped trying to answer with a joke.

"You can make jokes while keeping them clean," the woman told her. "It costs nothing extra."

Maren promised.

They stood together in the yard, carrying dry coats and the few possessions that had needed no further cleaning. Torin's sword had been returned to its sheath. Elara's satchel held the loose quartz dust in its cloth fold, her notes, and the chisel. The working fragments remained at the shrine where they belonged.

Kaelen carried the gauntlet in the fold of his coat.

Elias looked at it but said nothing.

The young man noticed anyway.

"I don't want to put it on just to find out whether I'm frightened of it."

"Then don't."

"I'll have to someday."

"Someday can make its own arrangements."

Kaelen considered that, then tucked the coat more securely under his arm.

The smith had a new hammer handle ready for Maren. Torin had taken the old one to him while the rest of them slept. It was plain wood, shaped to fit the head she already owned. There were no runes, no precious fittings, and no claim that it could restrain anything in a drainage channel.

Maren inspected the grain.

"How much?"

The smith named the price. She glanced at Torin, who lifted both hands to show that he had not bought it for her.

She paid from her own purse and watched the man seat the head. When he offered her the old handle, she took it without explaining why.

Outside the workshop, Torin looked at the blackened wood sticking from her bundle.

"Furniture?"

"Reminder."

"Of what?"

"Not doing that twice."

They walked out through the gate onto the road beside the watchfort. The guards knew their names now, but still asked them to show the permission given by the officer. Elias handed it over and waited while the man read it.

He was glad ordinary rules had survived the extraordinary day. He had no wish to make an argument at every door because someone had heard a story about them.

The Gilded Tankard stood among the buildings serving the road outside the fort. Its roof was patched, its yard muddy, and its sign hung crooked from one chain. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney. Someone had set a bucket beneath a leak by the stable wall.

Maren stopped beneath the sign.

"This will do."

Torin glanced toward the smell of cooking. "It might do very well."

They went in together.

The common room was busy enough that their arrival disturbed no one. A woman carried bowls past them with a warning to get out of her way. At the hearth, a man was trying to dry socks without burning them. Two travelers argued over the ownership of a pack that appeared to belong to both of them.

Elias stood inside the door and waited for the noise to become something he needed to control.

It did not.

Maren found a table with a wall at its back. Torin chose a place where he could stretch his legs without blocking the passage. Elara sat near the light. Kaelen put his folded coat on the bench beside him and kept both hands on the table.

The woman with the bowls returned.

"Eating or staying?"

"Eating," Maren said.

"And staying," Torin added, looking at Elias.

Elias counted the coins in his purse before answering. The price of rooms would leave them less than he liked for the road, but there was no road they had to take tonight.

"Staying," he said.

They settled the cost before ordering the meal. Torin wrote it down beside the other shared expenses. Elara offered to divide the rooms in a way that would save another coin, and Maren told her she would pay that coin herself if it meant having enough space to turn over without hitting someone's knees.

Kaelen chose a bed near a window that opened onto the yard. He said he wanted to hear ordinary people in the morning.

No one questioned the preference.

The meal came in bowls that did not need to be balanced on a map. Thick stew, bread with a hard crust, and a plate of pickled vegetables. Maren's hands made tearing bread difficult. She looked at the loaf, looked at Torin, and asked him to cut it.

He did, without making her request into a lesson.

Elias ate slowly. There was enough food that finishing one bowl did not require deciding who should get the last spoonful. Outside, wheels rattled over the road. Each time, some part of him expected a warning call from the yard.

After a while he began to hear only wheels.

Elara took the folded cloth of quartz dust out of her satchel. She set it beside her bowl, looked at it, and put it away again.

"I thought I would want to study it immediately."

"You still can," Torin said.

"I don't want to."

Maren shifted her cup aside. "Excellent. A discovery we can all support."

Elara laughed, and the sound surprised Elias. At the shrine, he had heard only instructions from her, objections, and the effort of making a broken thing hold long enough. Here she looked younger than he had thought she was.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Perhaps the day after."

"Write that in your book," Maren suggested. "A very useful protocol."

For a while their conversation stayed with small matters. The smith's workmanship. Torin's boots, which had survived the ditch better than he expected. The stew, which Maren considered too thick until someone offered her a second bowl. The price of dry clothes.

Elias listened without deciding which of those matters contributed to a plan.

Eventually Kaelen asked about the forest.

The table grew quieter, though no one put down a cup in alarm.

"The keeper," he said. "We never found out what it meant by the key."

Elias remembered the shape among the trees, the feeling that something older than the city had been waiting there. He remembered Kaelen trying to understand it while they were still afraid of everything behind them.

"No," he said. "We didn't."

"Does that make what we did unfinished?"

Elara leaned back from the table.

"It makes what we know unfinished. Those aren't the same thing."

Kaelen turned his cup between his hands. "It might be important."

"It might," she agreed. "We keep the account. We don't change what we saw into an answer because we want one."

Elias looked at the coat on the bench, where the brass gauntlet lay folded out of sight.

"We came back to stop the flow from the city," he said. "We stopped it. We got everyone home. That's the work we promised each other."

Kaelen thought about that for a while.

"All right," he said.

It was a small answer, but he left the cup alone afterward.

The room filled toward evening. A cart driver sat on the empty end of their bench to ask whether the upper road was passable. Torin told him where the ground stayed firm. When the man asked about entering the ruined city, Elias described the restrictions at the watchfort and told him to ask there before taking anyone below the ridge.

He did not offer to guide him.

The driver thanked them, finished his drink, and went to find the gate officer. The question left no debt at the table.

Elara unfolded a clean piece of paper after the bowls were taken away. She wrote the date at the top and the five names beneath it.

"A copy for us," she said. "Of the account we left at the fort."

Maren watched the pen. "Do you have to start now?"

"Only the heading."

She wrote one more line, then blotted the ink and folded the sheet.

Elias caught the words before it closed: *The channel was closed. All five returned.*

He looked across the table and counted without meaning to. Maren, arguing with Torin over who had carried more on the climb. Elara, putting away the pen before it became another evening's work. Kaelen, tired enough to lean his head against the wall, no longer listening for anything under it.

The person fifth in Elara's account was himself.

The innkeeper brought the keys to their rooms. She wanted payment for breakfast in advance if they meant to have it. Elias paid and asked what time it would be served.

"After the kitchen's hot," she said. "Before the road gets busy."

"We'll be here."

She left the keys between the cups.

Outside, the sky beyond the road turned the color of the ashes in the hearth. The watchfort lamps came on one at a time. Somewhere inside its walls, other people were checking the observation reports and taking responsibility for the next watch.

Elias could leave that work where it belonged.

He reached inside his coat and took out the bronze map. They had brought it back after the copies were checked. He laid it flat beside Elara's folded account, its weight secure on the table instead of pressing against a wound.

For a moment he expected everyone to lean over it and decide where they had to go.

Maren put a cup on its bare corner to keep a draft from catching the paper beside it. Torin passed Kaelen the last slice of bread. Elara moved the lamp so the light would not shine in the young man's eyes.

No one asked Elias for an order.

He settled into his chair, feeling the bandage support his side, and took the bread Torin had saved for him.

They would sleep here tonight. In the morning there would be breakfast, clean water, and enough time to decide what came after it.

For tonight, all five had a place at the table.

# Chapter 17: The Road Back

Halfway up the ditch, Elias discovered that relief weighed more than fear.

Fear had kept his feet moving on the descent. Now each stone required a separate decision. He lifted his boot, found a place for it, and waited for the pain in his side to settle before shifting his weight. Behind him, Kaelen's breathing had become shallow again.

Torin stopped at the next bend.

"We can sit there."

He pointed to a shelf above the water. It was barely wide enough for two people, but the ground beyond it rose toward a stand of trees. Maren climbed out first. She used her forearms against the bank rather than her palms, and swore quietly when the hammer at her belt caught a root.

Torin unhooked it for her. He put it on the higher ground, then offered his arm to Elara.

Elias waited for Kaelen to climb ahead of him. The young man planted one foot on the shelf and stopped, staring at the drop back into the ditch.

"I can manage," he said.

"I know. Give me the satchel."

Kaelen handed it over. Without the weight across his shoulder, he made the climb on his next attempt. Torin caught his elbow at the top.

Elias put one knee on the shelf. His side pulled so sharply that he lost his breath. For a moment he could do nothing but hold the edge of the bank.

Maren leaned down. She hooked her forearm beneath his arm.

"Now you," she said.

He gave up trying to make it look like his own strength. Torin took the satchel. Between them they brought Elias onto the grass, where he remained on hands and knees until the black edges left his vision.

Maren sat beside him.

"We're not climbing back down," she said.

"I wasn't planning to."

"You looked ready to inspect something."

He sat carefully. Below the trees, they could see part of the valley. The shrine was hidden by the slope now, but a pale segment of the temple barrier remained visible through the thinning fog.

He watched it before answering her.

"Someone has to know it's still holding."

"Someone has to arrive alive to tell them."

Elara had laid the map on the grass. She was comparing the next turn in the ditch with a bend in the old road above them. Her paper diagram stayed beneath one hand, protected from the breeze.

"We can leave the water here," she said. "Follow the higher road to the ridge. The rest is the way we came."

Torin looked through the trees. "Higher ground, more shelter."

"Less climbing," Maren added.

Elias nodded. It was a small decision, and none of them needed him to make it for them.

They shared the remaining water. Kaelen took two careful mouthfuls and passed the flask to Elara. She offered it to Elias before drinking herself. He waited until she had taken her share.

The flask came back almost empty.

Maren weighed it in her hand.

"Next time we carry less cleverness and more water."

Elara began to object, then looked at the bandage over her cut.

"More water," she agreed.

They rested long enough for Torin to tighten the straps on his pack and move the heavier items from Elara's satchel into it. Elias surrendered the bronze map to him for the next stretch. Giving it away left a cold absence against his ribs, as if something had been removed from the bandage beneath.

Torin noticed where his hand went.

"I'll carry it on the road. You can have it back at the fort."

Elias nodded.

He had wanted to bring this thing back ever since they found it below the temple. He had thought mostly about what the marks would let them do. Now the bronze held the route to a shrine that needed to be left alone. Bringing back instructions for stopping had become as important as bringing back a weapon.

They walked uphill beneath the trees. The road was rutted and overgrown, but it did not disappear into deep water. Torin led them around the soft patches rather than cutting through. Elara marked the detours on her paper, asking him to wait while she caught up.

The party's pace became the pace of the slowest pair. Nobody called it that. When Kaelen stopped, Maren paused to retie a strap. When Elias needed a moment, Elara found something she wanted to mark. After the third time, he stopped pretending not to notice.

"Thank you," he said.

Maren glanced over her shoulder. "For the strap?"

"For the strap."

She went on walking.

They reached the ridge while the light was still high enough to show the city below. The green fog no longer covered the whole lower ground. It lay in patches between walls and beside the flooded streets. The temple shone faintly at one end of the ruins.

Torin put the map on a rock and stood beside it, looking back. No one hurried him.

"I thought we'd have to fight our way out," he said.

"You did fight," Kaelen answered.

"One hand in a ditch."

"Two hands," Maren said. "I was watching."

Torin smiled without turning. "Two, then."

Elara came to the rock. She put one finger on the engraved line connecting the shrine to the temple.

"We need to tell them that this is not a route for a search party. No one should go below the closed channel until we have watched it long enough to know what's safe."

"And no one carries those shards away," Elias said.

"Or tries a bigger stone," Kaelen added.

He was looking down into the city, but his hands stayed away from the gauntlet.

Elias let himself study the ruins once more. The enormous outline they had feared was still there, a dark shape above the broken buildings. It had not called again. Whatever the Blight had made of it, distance concealed too much for them to decide its fate.

The temptation to go down and be certain came sharply. A report that ended with uncertainty sounded like a report unfinished.

Then Kaelen leaned a little against him, not enough to ask for support, enough to reveal how much the young man was spending simply by standing.

Elias picked up the flask and offered him the last mouthful.

"The people at the fort can observe it from here," he said. "We tell them where to stand."

Torin wrapped the map and put it back into his pack.

They turned toward the watchfort.

The road crossed the ridge above the place where they had practiced with the quartz. Elara stopped when she recognized a pale mark on the rock. She touched it with the heel of her uninjured hand.

"We tested the note here," she said.

Torin set down the map. "We did."

"And I thought a test here meant we knew what it would do down there."

Elias looked at the mark. It was no wider than his thumb, an ordinary scar from a piece of steel against stone. The council had given them an approach. The test had shown they could produce the vibration. There had been a space between those two things and what they had actually attempted that none of them had wanted to look at for long.

"It meant we could open the channel carefully," he said. "It didn't tell us how much the crystal could hold."

"I should have said that."

"You did say we needed to control the pressure."

"That isn't the same as knowing the limit."

Maren lowered herself beside the rock. She had stopped trying to carry the hammer by its handle. Torin had tied it to the outside of her pack, where it would not swing against her bandaged hands.

"We all stood here," she said. "No one pushed you into the valley while staying up on this nice dry ridge."

Elara looked toward the road behind them. "I know. But if we're writing down what worked, I want to write down what we assumed."

Elias took the scrap of paper from her. The diagram of the replacement key filled one side. On the other, only the detours on the return path were marked.

"Tell me. I'll put it where it won't get mixed with the angles."

She dictated slowly. They had tested the note with an intact stone, away from the loaded channel. They had not tested the stronger pull. The quartz had broken when they increased the flow, and the replacement had worked at a lower rate. Their observation of success did not make the first calculation correct.

Elias wrote all of it. Maren leaned close to inspect his letters and suggested leaving more space between two lines before Elara added anything else.

Kaelen sat on the opposite side of the rock. He watched Elias finish the note.

"Put in what I did too."

Elias looked up.

"When you told me to go slower, I tried to hold it by pulling harder," Kaelen said. "I thought if I could control the whole weight of it, the opening wouldn't matter."

"You didn't know the stone would break."

"No. But I made it harder to stop."

Elias found room beneath Elara's account. He did not write that Kaelen had failed. He wrote what the mage described, and then what they had done when he could no longer control the flow.

Kaelen read the sentence. "That's right."

Torin sat on the grass while they finished. He had drawn a loose fold of his cloak over the map to keep sun from warming the bronze against his shoulder when they walked again. He looked as tired as the rest of them now that he was no longer carrying a sword in his hand.

"There's a thing I'd like left out," he said.

Elias kept the pen ready. "What?"

"Anything about me being unafraid. I was trying to decide which of you I could carry uphill if it all went wrong. I didn't like any answer."

Maren looked at him. "You could have dragged me."

"Over those stones?"

"I would have complained, but I would have preferred it to staying."

He laughed once. Elara folded the paper when the ink had dried and put it inside her coat.

They sat a little longer, with nothing left to dictate. Elias could see the path where they had approached this rock before the descent. It was the same path. He did not feel like the same person sitting beside it, but he could not have said what had changed except that the others were still there to help him try.

When they rose, he took the first part of the road without trying to walk ahead of Kaelen.

The last stretch was easier ground and harder walking. Once they no longer needed to inspect every patch of water, Elias's attention had room for all the aches he had ignored. His knees shook on the downhill turns. The leather at his shoulder had dried stiff. His bandage dragged against the wound beneath it.

At the approach to the fort, Maren began to laugh.

Elias looked at her. Nothing in the road seemed funny.

She held up the hammer. Its blackened handle had worn a dark streak across her sleeve.

"If the gate guard asks whether we're armed, I don't know what to tell him."

Torin looked at it. "Dangerous to furniture."

Kaelen made a sound that began as a cough and ended in a laugh. Even Elara smiled.

The guard above the outer gate called to them before they reached the last turn. Torin raised an empty hand and gave their names. Elias counted all five aloud when the man asked how many had returned.

The small door in the gate opened. Two guards came out rather than admitting them at once. One carried a spear; the other brought a water jug and a stack of blankets.

Elias accepted the delay. Mud and corruption marked their clothes. They had come from a place the watchfort had reason to fear.

"We closed the feed at the shrine," he told the nearer guard. "The temple consumed what came through. No one is holding it open now."

The guard's expression changed, but he did not lower the spear.

"The officer will want that from you."

"So will anyone who goes back to look."

The second guard offered the jug to Kaelen. When the young man reached for it with a shaking hand, she held it for him while he drank.

Elias watched the simple act with a sudden, unreasonable urge to sit down in the road and stay there.

They were brought into the outer yard. The guards did not claim to know whether every trace of the Blight was gone. They separated the party's wet packs from the blankets, asked where they had been touched by the corruption, and summoned the people who could make better decisions than guesses at a gate.

Maren put her hammer on a bench. Elara asked for the map and her diagrams before anyone took the packs away. Torin helped her get them out.

When an attendant offered Elias a seat, he accepted it.

The relief of lowering his weight onto solid wood was so strong that he missed the first question. The woman before him repeated it without annoyance.

"Where are you hurt?"

He showed her the bandage at his side.

She looked at its stained edge and called for clean water.

For the next while, the party belonged to other people's practical work. Wet coats came off. Cuts were washed. Cloth that had dried against wounds was loosened carefully rather than pulled away. Maren sat with her jaw clenched while the attendant cleaned her palms. Torin held a basin for her and did not offer any advice.

Kaelen refused a place on the pallet until Elias lay down first.

"It's a bench," Elias said.

"Then take the bench."

Elias did. The attendant removed his bandage and found that the edge of the wound had opened during the climb. It needed cleaning and fresh cloth. It did not need another walk down a valley today.

She told him so in terms that left no room for negotiation.

"There is someone waiting for our report," he said.

"They can wait beside your bench."

An officer came while the new bandage was being tied. He stopped at the end of the bench rather than ordering Elias to stand. The man's coat was plain, worn at the cuffs, and carried a dusting of grit from the yard.

"You went to the Gate of Silence."

"All five of us," Elias said.

The officer looked along the room. Kaelen lay on a pallet with a blanket over his legs. Maren sat with both hands open while Torin cleaned mud from her hammer. Elara had taken possession of a stool and a board on which to lay the map.

"All five came back," the officer said.

Elias nodded.

"Tell me how."

He began with the part they could verify: the controlled feed into the temple barrier, the quartz cracking under the stronger pull, the temporary restriction, and the three pieces that replaced the whole key.

When he reached the creature in the ditch, Torin put the hammer down and added what he had seen. He described where the hands climbed and how their flesh had failed when the stream was directed back toward the temple.

Elara drew the angles of the shards. She crossed out her first sketch and made a second beside it.

"This wedge moved," she said. "If anyone inspects the groove, they need to know why the position matters. They do not touch it to make it look tidier."

The officer studied the drawings.

"And the source?"

Kaelen lifted his head.

"We stopped hearing pressure beneath the channel. We took the chisel away and waited. We narrowed the opening and waited again. Then we walked out of range. Nothing followed us."

"You can be sure of that?"

"I can be sure I wasn't holding it after we left."

The officer considered him.

"That is the distinction I need."

Elias felt a tension leave his chest. He had expected to defend a victory or confess a failure. The man wanted facts useful enough to keep the next group alive.

"The temple barrier remains," Elias said. "The city is not safe to walk into just because the fog thinned. There are damaged buildings, flooded channels, and things we couldn't inspect from the ridge."

"Then no one enters the city yet."

The officer turned to the attendant and asked for another board. He put Elara's drawings on it, weighted the corners, and made a list beside them.

Two watchers to the ridge before the light failed. They would observe from the route the party had used, not go down to the shrine. A relief pair after them. Reports would describe changes in the temple light, the fog, and movement in the ruins. Anyone approaching the closed groove later would take Elara's diagram and leave the key intact.

He read the instructions back aloud.

Torin corrected which ditch led to the higher road. Elara corrected the position of the upper turn. Maren pointed out the slippery shelf where she had hauled Elias up. The officer amended his list each time.

Then he looked at Kaelen.

"Do you need to go with them?"

The question held Elias still. He had asked too much of the young man more than once. He could not answer this for him and call the answer freedom.

Kaelen looked at the map.

"No," he said. "They can watch the barrier without me. If something changes, they report it before they touch anything."

The officer wrote that down too.

"Then you stay here."

He took the observation list and left the party's drawings on the board. Elias watched him cross the yard, speak to the guards, and assign the work. Within a few minutes, people who had not stood at the shrine were gathering blankets and water for the ridge watch.

There was no magic in the transfer. There was paper, enough detail to use it, and someone accepting the responsibility.

Elias lay back.

For the first time since the quartz broke, he was not the only person counting the distance between pulses.

Elara remained at the board. Her hand shook each time she tried to make a small mark.

"It can wait," Maren said.

"I might forget the angle."

"Then tell me. I'll draw it."

Maren looked at her own bandages and grimaced.

"Torin will draw it. I'll criticize."

The soldier came over without complaint. Elara talked him through one final sketch. His lines were less neat, but the groove, the three shards, and the wedges were all in the right places.

She signed her name beneath it with an awkward grip and let the charcoal fall from her fingers.

A clerk from the stores came to the infirmary with a wooden tray and a short list. He wanted to know which items belonged to which member of the party, and which needed to remain with the fort until they were dry enough to handle safely.

It was the first question Elias had been asked all day that did not concern a failure capable of killing someone. He found it harder to answer than he should have.

The clerk pointed to the sword beside Torin's chair. Torin claimed it. Maren claimed the hammer without rising, and told the man not to scrape the char from its head into anyone's bedding. Elara claimed the chisel and her satchel, then asked for a clean cloth in which to keep the loose quartz dust.

"The three pieces that matter aren't here," she added. "They stayed in the groove."

The clerk wrote that down in the margin, though it was not part of the stores list. Elias liked him for taking the trouble.

When they reached the brass gauntlet, Kaelen had fallen asleep. The clerk waited with his pen raised.

"His," Elias said, pointing toward the pallet. "Wrap it in his coat. Don't buckle it onto him."

"I wasn't going to."

Elias looked away. The man had asked whose property it was, not what made it dangerous. Elias was still answering the questions he expected instead of the ones being spoken.

"Thank you," he said.

The clerk nodded and moved on.

The party's wet clothes lay on the bench in a heap. Once the owner of each coat had been established, the clerk asked whether there was anything in the pockets that should not go to the drying room.

Torin stood to check. Out came the empty pouch that had held their cord, a small packet of food wrapped too poorly to keep the ditch water out, and a button that Maren insisted had not been hers when the day began.

Elara found the first sketch from the ridge folded inside her satchel. Water had blurred one edge of it. She laid it beside the later diagram, pinning the old assumptions beside the positions that had worked.

Elias emptied his coat last. He set out a piece of damp cloth, two loose coins, and a scrap of paper he could no longer read. The map was already safe on the board. He looked through the pockets again because the absence of bronze felt like something lost.

"You've done that one," the clerk said gently.

"Yes."

Elias folded the coat and handed it over.

The man carried the wet things away. Torin sat back down and began to list what needed replacing: cord, clean cloth, some of the food, Maren's hammer handle, and the buckle on Elara's satchel strap that had bent during the climb.

"It can wait until tomorrow," Maren said.

"I'm only making the list."

"Tomorrow's list?"

"Yes."

"Then put tomorrow at the top."

Torin looked at her, then wrote it. Elara watched him do so, with an expression Elias could not quite read. When the soldier put the charcoal down, she took neither it nor the list from him.

Elias leaned back on the bench. He could imagine the next morning in small, manageable pieces: checking his bandage, finding the smith, paying for bread. He had not allowed himself to think that far in ordinary terms since the journey into the ruins. He had planned returns, expeditions, strategies, all of them conditional on getting past another danger first.

The clerk came back with dry blankets and told them where their clothes would be left. Elias listened. He made himself remember the place instead of another route through the city.

Across the yard, the officer read their route instructions to the observation pair one more time. Torin rose halfway at the sound of his own correction, then saw the man pointing to the amended turn and sat down again.

The instructions had reached someone else intact.

Elara saw that too. She folded her hands in her lap, keeping the cut one uppermost, and leaned her head back against the wall.

The attendant brought food that required no ceremony: broth, bread torn into manageable pieces, and a small bowl of soft vegetables. Maren ate left-handed, badly. Torin steadied the bowl until she glared at him, then let her finish on her own.

Kaelen took the gauntlet off.

Elias heard the buckle loosen and looked over. The brass lay on the folded coat beside the pallet. The skin underneath was marked, but no green light remained in the seams.

"Does it hurt?" Elias asked.

"Less off than on."

"Then leave it off."

Kaelen flexed his hand. "I keep expecting to hear something."

"You can tell me if you do."

The mage looked at the gauntlet for another moment, then pulled the blanket over his shoulder.

"And if I don't?"

"Then you don't have to keep trying."

Kaelen lay down. Soon his breathing settled into the slow, uneven rhythm of someone falling asleep despite himself.

Elias finished the broth. It tasted mostly of salt, and he was grateful for every mouthful. Outside, boots crossed the yard. Someone called for a replacement strap. The watchfort continued its ordinary work around a day that had felt to Elias like the end of every ordinary thing.

Maren came to the bench, holding her damaged hammer under one arm.

"The smith can replace the haft," she said. "Tomorrow."

"Good."

She looked at him for a while.

"You told Kaelen he could stop."

Elias glanced toward the pallet. "He had to."

"So do you."

He had been preparing to ask how soon the watchers would return. He shut his mouth instead.

Maren saw it and smiled briefly.

"There. You can learn."

She found a seat close enough to Elara that the scholar could lean against her if she slept. Torin stayed near the door, cleaning the edge of his sword. Elias thought of telling him to rest as well, then saw that the movements had slowed. The soldier was doing a familiar task in a safe room because his hands had not yet accepted there was nothing more urgent to do.

Elias understood that too.

The light at the doorway faded. An attendant lit a lamp and turned its wick low. She checked Elias's bandage without asking him to sit up.

From beyond the yard came the voice of a guard announcing the relief watch. Another voice answered. Elias listened until he could distinguish neither from the ordinary movement of the fort.

The map lay beside Elara's diagrams. It was within reach if anyone needed it. For once, Elias did not need to keep it against his ribs.

He closed his eyes while the lamp was still burning.

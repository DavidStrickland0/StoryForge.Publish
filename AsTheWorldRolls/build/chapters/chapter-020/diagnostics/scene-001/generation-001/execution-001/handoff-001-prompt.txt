The food arrived in heavy clay bowls, the bread dense and dark, the stew thick with root vegetables and chunks of meat that looked like something Elias had once eaten and forgotten. He ate. The hunger was a dull ache in his stomach, a reminder that his body was still his, still demanding fuel to keep the gears turning.

But as he chewed, his eyes drifted to the window. The twilight outside was deepening into a bruised purple, the last light bleeding into the horizon. The caravan was due at dawn. That gave them less than eight hours.

He pushed the last of the stew down and set the spoon against the rim of the bowl. The sound was sharp in the quiet corner of the room.

"We can't wait until morning to test the resonance," Elias said. His voice was low, but it cut through the murmur of the tavern.

Torin looked up from his own bowl, a piece of bread halfway to his mouth. He stopped chewing. "What?"

"The seal at the hydraulic throat. It’s not just a lock. It’s a tuned instrument. If we strike it wrong, if we use too much force, the frequency will shatter the bond entirely. The Blight won’t flow into the barrier; it will burst out. We need to find the exact tone before we go out there."

Maren set her spoon down. Her hands were still, but Elias noticed the way her fingers twitched against the wood of the table, a small, rhythmic tapping that she seemed unaware of. "We have the quartz. We have the chisel. We can test it here. On the stone table, or on the hearth."

"No," Elias said. He stood up. The chair scraped back, a harsh shriek that made the other patrons glance over. He ignored them. "Not on this stone. It’s granite, or close to it. The resonance is different. The seal is a specific type of slate, layered with quartz veins. We need to test it on something that matches the harmonic profile. The ridge outcrop behind the tavern. The stone there is the same."

Torin stood up, rolling his shoulders. He was already moving toward the door. "I’ll go with you."

"No," Maren said. Her voice was firm, but there was a tremor in it that matched the twitching of her fingers. "I’ll go. I’m the healer. I know how to handle the… the delicate work. The chisel work. I can do it."

Elias looked at her. He saw the way she held her breath, the slight tightening of her jaw. He knew what her tremors were. They were a side effect of the Blight exposure, a neurological aftershock that came and went. He had seen it in the watchfort, when she had tried to steady Kaelen’s pulse. It was there now, hidden beneath her professional composure.

"Maren," Elias said gently. "You know what happens when your hands shake during the resonance. The chisel slips. The frequency changes. If you miss the tone even by a fraction, the stone could crack. And if it cracks, the Blight comes out."

"I can control it," she said.

"You can control it when you’re not shaking," Elias said. "And you are shaking. Right now. Look at your hands."

Maren looked down. Her fingers were vibrating against the table, a fine, rapid flutter. She tried to still them, but the tremor only increased, as if her body was fighting against her will. She pulled her hands back, tucking them into her sleeves. Her face was pale, her eyes hard.

"I’ll do it," she said.

Elias didn’t argue. He knew that arguing would only make it worse. He grabbed the quartz stone from his pack, the chisel from his belt. He handed the chisel to Maren.

"Take your time," he said. "We have until dawn."

The ridge outcrop was a short walk from the tavern, a rocky promontory that jutted out over the valley. The air was cold, carrying the scent of pine and wet earth. The stone here was dark, layered, with veins of white quartz that caught the moonlight.

Elias set the quartz stone on a flat ledge. He adjusted the angle, tapping it with his finger to find the natural resonance. It hummed, a low, clear note that vibrated in his chest.

Maren held the chisel. She looked at the stone, then at Elias.

"Where do I strike?" she asked.

"Here," Elias said, pointing to a small depression in the stone. "Lightly. Just enough to make the quartz vibrate. Don’t break it."

Maren nodded. She raised the chisel. Her hand was steady for a moment, then the tremor returned. The chisel wavered in the air.

She closed her eyes. She took a breath. She struck the stone.

The sound was wrong. It was a sharp, cracking noise, not the clear hum Elias had heard. A small chip flew off the stone, landing on the ground with a tiny click.

Maren stared at the chisel. Her hand was shaking violently now, the tool rattling against the leather of her grip.

"I missed," she whispered.

Elias looked at the stone. The chip was small, but it was there. The resonance had been disrupted. The stone was no longer stable.

"We can’t use this one," Elias said. He picked up the quartz stone. "Maren, put the chisel down."

Maren looked at him. Her eyes were wide, filled with a shame that was worse than the fear. She had wanted to be useful. She had wanted to prove that she was more than just a healer, that she could do the precise, delicate work that saved them. And she had failed.

"I’m sorry," she said.

"It’s not your fault," Elias said. He took the chisel from her hand. It was cold, and it felt heavy. "Your hands are shaking. It’s the Blight. It’s in your nerves."

Maren nodded. She pulled her hands into her sleeves again, hiding the trembling. "Then who will do it?"

Elias looked at the stone. He looked at the chisel. He looked at Maren.

"I don’t know," he said. "But we have to find someone. Before dawn."

He turned back to the ridge. The moon was high now, casting long shadows across the rocks. The valley below was dark, the trees swaying in the wind.

Somewhere in that darkness, the Blight was waiting. And they had no one to stop it.

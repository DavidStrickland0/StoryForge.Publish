The heavy oak door groaned on its hinges as Torin shouldered it open, the night air rushing in to cool the hearth’s heat. The smell of wet pine and damp earth hit Elias immediately, a sharp contrast to the tavern’s stale woodsmell and the underlying scent of spilled ale. He stepped out into the courtyard, his boots finding the flagstones slick with evening mist. The moon was hidden behind a thick blanket of clouds, leaving the world in a gray, indistinct twilight.

Maren followed closely behind him, her left hand gripping the strap of her satchel so tightly her knuckles were white. Her fingers twitched, a subtle, rhythmic tremor that she was trying to mask by shifting her weight from one foot to the other. She did not speak. Her jaw was set, her eyes fixed on the stone wall of the tavern behind them as if she were still trying to hold it in place.

Elara moved past them both. She did not look at Maren. Instead, her gaze was already moving outward, scanning the treeline at the edge of the clearing where the path to the ridge outcrop began. The air was cool, carrying the scent of damp soil and the faint, metallic tang of the stone that lay hidden beneath the grass.

"Stay on the path," Elias said, his voice low. "The shale is loose after the rain. If we slip near the edge, the vibration will be wrong."

"Understood," Elara replied. Her voice was steady, devoid of the tension that seemed to radiate from the rest of the group. She adjusted the pack on her back, checking the strap for the third time. "Where exactly do you need this, Elias?"

"The outcrop is about two hundred yards into the woods, past the dead tree. The stone is quartz-veined. It’s the only place within a mile that has the right density to carry the hum without shattering the surface." Elias looked at Maren. "Maren. You don’t have to be there. Elara can do it."

Maren stopped walking. She turned to face Elias, her eyes hard. "I am the healer, Elias. I know the body’s limits better than anyone. I know what my hands are doing. If I break the rhythm, I will stop. I will not force it."

"Then we lose time," Elara said, turning to look at Maren. Her expression was not angry, but it was firm. "And we lose the chance to test the frequency before the moon is fully up. I’ve been chiseling stone since I was ten. My hands don’t shake, Maren. Yours do. I can feel the stone; you can only feel your fear."

The silence that followed was heavy. Maren’s lips pressed into a thin line. She looked at Elias, searching for a verdict, a shield, a way to keep her pride intact. Elias looked at the chisel in his hand, the simple tool that was supposed to be the key to their survival. He looked at Maren’s trembling hand, then at Elara’s steady stance.

"We are not here to judge your worth," Elias said, his tone neutral. "We are here to build a wall. And that wall requires a steady hand." He handed the chisel to Elara. The metal was cold in her grip, and she closed her fingers around it with a practiced ease that Maren’s had never achieved.

Elara turned away, walking toward the trees without looking back. Torin followed her, his heavy boots crunching on the loose gravel. Elias watched Maren for a moment longer. She was standing alone in the courtyard, her shoulders hunched against the cold. He saw her take a deep breath, her chest expanding, and then he turned and walked toward the forest.

The path into the woods was narrow, lined with ancient oaks whose branches twisted overhead, blocking out most of the sky. The ground was soft, covered in a thick layer of leaves that crunched underfoot. Elias walked quickly, his mind calculating the steps, the distance, the variables that could go wrong.

The quartz stone was a pale, translucent crystal that Elias had pulled from the ruins of the temple. It was the size of a fist, rough-hewn but dense. It sat in a leather pouch at his hip. He could feel its weight against his leg, a constant reminder of the power they carried and the danger it posed.

When they reached the outcrop, the trees parted to reveal a jagged formation of stone rising from the earth. The rock was gray and pitted, but a single vein of white quartz ran through it, shining faintly in the gloom. The surface was uneven, fractured by age and weather. It was a difficult place to work.

Elara knelt beside the stone. She placed the quartz crystal on the surface, pressing it down until it sat flush against the rock. She lifted the chisel, positioning the tip against the side of the crystal.

"Hold it," Elias said. He stepped forward and placed his hands on either side of the quartz, his palms flat against the cool stone. He could feel a faint vibration in his hands, a residual hum that seemed to linger in the material.

Elara drew her breath in and held it. She began to tap the chisel against the quartz. The sound was sharp, clear, and piercing. It cut through the silence of the forest, a single, bright note that hung in the air.

Maren, who had remained standing in the shadows of the trees, watched Elara’s hands. They were steady. The chisel moved in a precise, rhythmic pattern, striking the same spot, maintaining the same angle. The sound was consistent, a steady drone that Elias felt in his teeth.

Elara’s face was calm. Her brow was not furrowed with concentration, nor was her body tensed with effort. She was simply working. The stone did not crack. It did not shatter. Instead, a faint, high-pitched whine began to rise from the crystal, a sound that was almost inaudible to the human ear but was clearly present in the vibration that Elias felt through his palms.

"Keep it up," Elias said.

Elara nodded, her eyes closed, her focus entirely on the rhythm of the chisel. The sound grew stronger, a sustained tone that seemed to vibrate the very air around them. Elias watched the quartz. The stone was beginning to glow, a soft, pale light emanating from within the crystal.

Maren stepped forward. She watched the light with wide eyes. She had never seen this before. The power in the stone was immense, a force that could tear the world apart or hold it together, depending on the frequency.

"Is it working?" Maren asked, her voice quiet.

Elias looked at the stone, then at Elara. "Yes," he said. "It is working."

Elara opened her eyes. She looked at Elias, then at Maren. There was no triumph in her expression, no satisfaction in having proven her worth. There was only a quiet acknowledgment of a job done.

She stopped the chisel. The sound faded, the light dimming until the quartz was dark again. She set the chisel down on the ground, carefully, as if it were a fragile thing.

"It holds," Elara said. "The frequency is stable. The stone is ready."

Elias picked up the quartz stone. It was warm now, vibrating slightly in his hand. He could feel the energy within it, a potential that was waiting to be unleashed. He looked at Elara, who was watching him with a steady gaze.

"Thank you, Elara," Elias said.

She nodded. "It is what needs to be done."

Maren looked down at her hands. The trembling had stopped, but the stiffness remained. She did not look at Elias or Elara. She simply turned and began to walk back toward the trees, her steps silent on the leaf-littered ground.

Elias looked at the quartz stone in his hand, then at Elara, who was already gathering her tools. He understood the shift. The role had been taken. The gap in the plan had been filled.

They stood for a moment in the silence of the forest, the air thick with the scent of pine and the lingering hum of the resonance. The moon was still hidden, but the stars were beginning to appear, pinpricks of light in the darkness above.

"Let’s go," Elias said. "We have work to do."

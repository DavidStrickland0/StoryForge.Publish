The silence in the common room was heavy, thick with the smell of woodsmoke and the unspoken weight of the hour. Elias held the chisel at his side, the metal cold against his palm, while the fire crackled in the hearth, casting long, dancing shadows against the polished wood of the table. Maren stood near the window, her hand resting on the back of a chair for support, her fingers twitching slightly against the grain. She had not looked at Elara. She did not need to. The challenge had been laid out, and the silence was the answer.

Elara moved around the table. Her steps were quiet, deliberate, the leather of her boots making no sound on the floorboards. She stopped on the other side of Elias, close enough that he could feel the shift in the air, the sudden sharpening of intent. She did not speak. She did not need to. She reached into her pack, a canvas thing that had seen a dozen roads, and withdrew a small, leather-bound pouch. From within it, she took a stone.

It was not the quartz from Elias’s pack. This one was rougher, unpolished, with a jagged edge that caught the firelight and threw it back in sharp, white fragments. She placed it on the table with a soft *clack* that seemed to echo in the quiet room.

Elias watched her. He had seen her work before, in the chaos of the temple, where she had moved through the smoke with a precision that bordered on the supernatural. But this was different. This was not about cutting through flesh or stone with force. This was about listening. About finding the single, fragile frequency that would hold the seal together long enough for the barrier to feed.

Elara picked up the chisel from Elias’s hand. The exchange was silent, a passing of trust that felt heavier than the metal itself. She held the chisel in her right hand, the tip pointing down, and brought it to the surface of the rough quartz. She did not strike. She tapped. A single, sharp tap. Then another. And another.

The rhythm was not a beat. It was a breath. A slow, steady exhale. The chisel moved in a shallow arc, the tip grazing the stone with a pressure so light it was almost imperceptible. But the sound that came from the quartz was not a tap. It was a hum. A low, resonant thrum that seemed to vibrate in the bones of the room.

Elias felt it in his teeth. Maren’s head turned. Even Torin, who had been rubbing his neck by the door, stopped and looked.

The hum grew. It was not loud, but it was dense, a physical weight in the air. Elara’s eyes were closed, her jaw set, her arm moving with a steady, mechanical grace that Maren’s trembling hand could not match. The chisel did not strike the stone; it coaxed it. It found the frequency, the specific resonance that the quartz was built to hold, and it held it there.

The air around the stone shimmered, a subtle distortion, like heat rising from summer pavement. The hum deepened, a sound that was less heard than felt, a vibration that settled into the chest and the stomach.

Maren took a step back. Her hand dropped from the chair. She watched Elara with an expression that was not anger, not anymore, but a complex mix of relief and something colder. Recognition. The realization that her strength, her will, was not enough for this. That the body had limits, and that the work required a different kind of steadiness. A stillness that Elara possessed in her marrow.

Elias watched the shimmer in the air. He watched the way the dust motes in the firelight seemed to slow, to hang suspended in the field of the hum. He knew, with the certainty of a man who has seen the world break and mend and break again, that this was the key. Not the force. Not the will. The precision.

Elara opened her eyes. The shimmer faded. The hum died, leaving a ringing silence in its wake. She set the chisel down on the table, her hand steady, her face pale but composed.

She looked at Elias. She did not smile. She did not need to. The message was clear. The gap was filled. The operator was found.

Elias picked up the chisel. He looked at Maren. She was still, her hands clasped in front of her, her eyes fixed on the floor. He did not offer a word of comfort. There was no comfort to be found in this. There was only the work. And the work was done.

"We leave in an hour," Elias said. His voice was flat, practical. He turned to Torin, who was watching the door, his hand resting on the hilt of his sword. "We need to be there before dark. We need to test the resonance. We need to see who can do the work."

He looked at Elara. She nodded once, a small, sharp movement.

Torin stepped forward, his boots heavy on the floor. He looked at Maren, then at Elara, then at Elias. He did not speak. He did not need to. The hierarchy was clear. The operator was Elara. The sensor was Kaelen. The leader was Elias.

Elias slung the chisel over his shoulder. He looked at the window. The light was fading, the sky a bruised purple, the stars beginning to prick through the dark. The night was coming. And with it, the work.

He turned to the group. "We go now," he said. "We have no time to lose."

The room was silent. The fire crackled. The chisel was cold in his hand. The stone was still on the table. The work was ahead. And they were ready.

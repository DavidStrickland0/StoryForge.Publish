Elara did not wait for Elias to finish holding the chisel up like a trophy or a warning. She simply reached across the scarred oak table, her fingers closing around the handle with a firm, quiet grip. She did not yank it away; she simply applied enough pressure that Elias’s fingers, still warm from the conversation but no longer needed, released their hold.

Elara lifted the chisel. She turned it over in her hand, examining the bevel of the steel against the dying light of the hearth. It was a simple tool, a wedge of iron and wood, but in her hand it looked less like a weapon and more like a key.

“I know the angle,” Elara said. Her voice was low, stripped of the defensive edge that had colored her earlier words about safety. It was the voice she used when she was working on a lockpick or tuning a string. “I’ve been doing this since I was twelve. My father had a mill that jammed every winter. I used to be the one who coaxed the gears back into alignment. You don’t force a mechanism, Elias. You listen to where it’s stuck, and you give it exactly the nudge it needs. No more.”

Elias watched her. He had seen her hands before, steady and deliberate, but he had not seen them applied to the quartz stone and the chisel in the specific context of a resonance test. He had assumed the tremor in Maren’s hand was a unique symptom of the Blight’s proximity, a mark of the strain. He had not considered that Elara’s steadiness was not merely an absence of tremor, but a presence of control.

Maren stood by the hearth, her left hand hanging loosely at her side. The tremor was still there, a fine, rapid vibration that made the shadows of the fire flicker strangely across her face. She did not argue. She did not reach for the chisel. She simply looked at Elara, and for the first time since they had left the forest, the healer looked tired. Not the sharp, adrenaline-buzzed exhaustion of battle, but the deep, bone-weariness of someone who has been holding up a weight they can no longer lift.

“You’re sure?” Elias asked. It was not a challenge. It was a verification.

Elara set the chisel down on the table, then picked it up again. She tapped the flat end of the handle against the table, a soft, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick*. She held the quartz stone in her other hand, feeling its cold, inert weight.

“I’m sure,” Elara said. “The stone doesn’t care who holds it. It cares about the frequency. And I can hold the frequency.”

Torin, who had been rubbing the back of his neck with a grimace that spoke of a stiff joint or a headache, stopped his movement. He looked at Elara, then at Elias. The tension in his shoulders eased, just slightly. The group’s dynamic had shifted. The gap in the plan, the hole in the middle where the chisel-work had to go, was no longer a void waiting to be filled by a struggling healer. It was a role being taken by someone who could do it.

Kaelen, seated by the hearth, did not look up. He was staring at his own hands, which were resting on his knees. The brass gauntlet on his wrist was dark and quiet, the runes etched into the metal dull and lifeless. He had been listening. He had heard the shift in the air, the way the argument had dissolved into a practical decision. He did not need to look up to know that the danger of Maren’s tremors breaking the resonance was now managed. He was already thinking about the next step, the part of the operation that required him to be awake, focused, and willing to let the resonance flow through him.

“We leave in an hour,” Elias said, his voice flat and final. “We walk to the ridge outcrop. We find the quartz deposit. We test the resonance. If Elara can hold the frequency, we proceed. If she can’t, we rethink.”

Elara nodded. She tucked the chisel into the leather sheath at her hip, then reached for the quartz stone, tucking it into her belt pouch. She stood up, her movements fluid and efficient. She did not gloat. She did not smile. She simply accepted the role because it was the right thing to do, and because she was the one who could do it.

The group began to gather their packs. The air in the tavern was cool, the smoke from the hearth curling toward the ceiling. Outside, the sky was darkening, the first stars appearing above the black spires of the village in the distance. The world beyond the tavern was quiet, waiting for them to step out into it.

Elias looked at Kaelen. The young man was still watching his hands. His lips were pale, his eyes distant. He was recovering, but the cost of the last operation was written in the lines around his mouth, the shadow under his eyes. He was a cauterized wound, Maren had said. A tool that had been used until it nearly broke.

Elias needed to know if Kaelen was ready. Not for the chisel work, which was now Elara’s burden, but for the resonance. For the part of the plan where Kaelen had to be the bridge, the sensor, the one who held the frequency in his mind and guided the Blight into the barrier.

“Kaelen,” Elias said.

The young man looked up. His gaze was clear, but tired. He met Elias’s eyes, and for a moment, there was a silent exchange between them. Elias saw the fear there, the deep-seated dread of being consumed by the very power he was supposed to control. But he also saw the resolve. The decision to be useful. The choice to hold the line.

Kaelen nodded. It was a small movement, barely a shift of his chin, but it was enough. He was ready. Or at least, he was willing to be.

Elias turned to the door. The night air was cold and crisp, smelling of pine sap and wet loam. The road to the ridge outcrop was short, a gentle slope through the trees. They would be there before dark. They would test the resonance. They would see if Elara’s hand was steady enough to hold the frequency, and if Kaelen’s mind was strong enough to guide it.

The gap in the plan was closed. The operator was chosen. The sensor was ready.

They stepped out into the night, the tavern door swinging shut behind them with a heavy thud. The firelight faded, replaced by the silver glow of the stars. The world was quiet, but it was not empty. It was waiting. And they were going to meet it.

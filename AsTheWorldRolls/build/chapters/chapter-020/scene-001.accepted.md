The water glass sat empty on the polished wood, a small, clear circle of reflection in the dimming light of the common room. Elias set it down, the sound of the base striking the table cutting through the low murmur of the other patrons. He felt the residual ache in his side, a dull throb under the leather of his armor, but his mind was already elsewhere. It was elsewhere, drifting back to the stone, the channel, the way the resonance had felt less like a force and more like a conversation with the earth itself.

He turned to Torin, who was leaning back in his chair, his broad shoulders stretching the seams of his tunic. Torin was rubbing his neck, the movement slow and deliberate, a physical reminder of the tension they had all been carrying for days.

"We have a gap," Elias said. His voice was low, meant only for the table. "The plan as it stands has a hole in the middle."

Maren looked up from the small vial of amber salve she was examining, her brow furrowing. She was wiping her hands on a cloth, a habit she had developed to keep them from shaking, though the tremor was subtle enough that only someone who knew what to look for would notice. "Which part?" she asked, her tone professional, clipped.

"The chisel work," Elias said. "At the ridge outcrop. The test site."

Elara, who had been sitting quietly at the periphery of the conversation, her eyes scanning the room with a sharp, alert intelligence, looked up. She had been listening. She always listened.

"The frequency," Elias continued, leaning forward. "The seal requires a precise, sustained vibration. Not a strike. A hum. The chisel has to be tapped against the quartz stone with a consistency that allows the resonance to build gradually. If the rhythm breaks, the seal shatters instead of weakening. If the angle shifts by a degree, the energy dissipates into the rock and we lose the sync."

He looked at Maren. "You’re the one with the steady hand. The one who can hold the stone steady while the chisel does the work. It’s your role in the plan."

Maren met his gaze. She did not flinch, but Elias could see the tension in her jaw, the way her fingers tightened almost imperceptibly on the edge of the table. She was a healer. She worked with precision, yes, but her precision was chemical, biological. It was the precision of a dosage, of a suture, of a diagnosis. It was not the mechanical, rhythmic precision of a craftsman.

"I can do it," Maren said. Her voice was steady, but there was a thinness to it, a lack of the usual resonance. "I can hold the stone. I can tap the chisel. I understand the mechanics you’ve described, Elias. The frequency is a matter of timing. I can learn the timing."

Elias studied her. He knew she was trying to reassure them. He knew she was trying to reassure herself. He could see the slight tremor in her left hand, the one that wasn’t holding the cloth. It was a small vibration, barely perceptible, but it was there. It was the cost of the last few weeks, the cumulative weight of the Blight, the fear, the exhaustion. It was a physical manifestation of the strain they had all been under.

"The outcrop is unstable," Elias said, his tone remaining factual, devoid of accusation. "It’s a ridge of loose shale and fractured quartz. The stone will vibrate. The ground will shift. If your hand slips, even by a fraction, the chisel will strike the stone at the wrong angle. And if the angle is wrong, the resonance will backfire. It will tear the seal apart. And the Blight will pour out."

The silence at the table was heavy. The other patrons were still talking, laughing, the sounds of a normal life continuing around them, but the air inside the circle of their table felt thin, stretched tight.

"I understand," Maren said. She set the vial of salve down. She looked at Elara, then back to Elias. "I understand the risk. But I am the one who has to do it. I am the healer. I am the one who stabilizes. I can stabilize the stone."

Elara leaned forward. She had been quiet, watching the exchange with a detached, analytical curiosity. Now, her eyes fixed on Elias.

"You’re asking her to do it because it’s the logical choice," Elara said. Her voice was calm, clear. "Because she’s the one with the medical training. Because she’s the one you trust to hold a steady hand. But you’re not asking her if she can. You’re telling her she must."

Maren stiffened. She looked at Elara, and for a moment, there was a flash of irritation in her eyes. "I am capable, Elara."

"No one is saying you aren’t," Elara said. She turned back to Elias. "But capability is not the same as safety. And safety is what we need. We need someone who can do the work without their body betraying them. We need someone whose hands are steady, not because they are trying to hold them steady, but because they are steady by nature."

Elias looked at Maren. He saw the way her jaw tightened, the way her eyes refused to look away from his. He saw the determination there, the fierce, stubborn resolve to prove herself, to be useful, to not be the weak link. He respected that. He respected it deeply. But respect was not enough. Respect did not stop the resonance from backfiring. Respect did not hold the stone when the tremor came.

"We need to test it," Elias said. "Before we go to the hydraulic throat. Before we risk the barrier. We need to go to the ridge outcrop. We need to test the resonance. We need to see who can do the work."

Maren’s face hardened. "I can do the work."

"You can try," Elias said. "And if you can, then you will do the work. But if you can’t… if your hand slips… then we need to know. Now. Not when it’s too late."

The group looked at each other. The unspoken tension hung in the air, a physical weight. They were a group of survivors. They were a group of friends. But they were also a team, and a team required reliability. It required someone who could be trusted to do the job, no matter how hard it was, no matter how much it cost them.

Maren stood up. She brushed the dust from her tunic, a small, automatic gesture. She looked at Elias, and her eyes were bright, fierce.

"Take me to the outcrop," she said.

Elias nodded. He stood up, his joints creaking slightly. He looked at Torin, who was already reaching for his bracer. He looked at Kaelen, who was still sitting, pale and quiet, his eyes distant, watching the flame in the hearth as if it were a memory.

"We leave in an hour," Elias said. "We need to be there before dark. We need to test the resonance. We need to see who can do the work."

He picked up the chisel from the table. He held it up, examining the edge in the firelight. The metal was dull, but sharp. It was a simple tool. A simple tool that could save their lives, or destroy them.

He looked at Maren. He looked at Elara. He looked at the chisel.

The gap in the plan was there. The hole in the middle. And it was waiting to be filled.

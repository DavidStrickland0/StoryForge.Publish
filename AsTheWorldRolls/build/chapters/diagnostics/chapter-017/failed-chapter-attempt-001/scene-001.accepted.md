Maren did not look up from Kaelen’s chest when she spoke. Her fingers were pressed tight against the skin that had been translucent hours before, now slowly regaining its human opacity like ice thickening under a sudden freeze. The warmth she poured into him was not merely heat; it was a tether. She anchored herself to his fading pulse with her own, feeding him the steady rhythm of her breath until his chest rose and fell in a syncopated, laboring cadence.

“Stop shivering,” she said quietly, though the command was directed at him even as he slept. “You are not cold.”

The barrier above them hummed, a low vibration that traveled through the stone floor and into their boots. The light that usually held the clearing in a soft, bruised violet glow had dimmed by nearly half. It did not flicker; it simply receded, retreating inward toward the quartz stone at the center until only the faintest rim of illumination remained. Outside the barrier, the wind was picking up again, carrying the scent of copper and wet earth from the ruined city beyond. The world outside was growing darker, colder, more aggressive.

Torin sat on the flat rock near the fire, his large hands folded over a piece of hard bread that had gone stale by the second. He did not eat. He watched Maren’s face, which was pale and drawn, her lips chapped from the dry heat she was generating. His gaze slid past her to Kaelen, whose skin still bore the faint tracery of dark veins beneath the surface, though they were no longer pulsing with the sickly green luminescence that had marked his condition before.

“Maren,” Torin said. “If you push too hard, you’ll drain yourself dry.”

“I am not empty,” she replied without breaking her focus. Her eyes remained fixed on Kaelen’s brow. “He is leaking. The magic is leaving him through the skin like smoke from a wound. I am catching it before it falls to the ground.”

Elias Thorne leaned back against the stone, his eyes closed, though he was not asleep. He had been listening to the change in the barrier’s resonance. The hum had shifted from a steady, protective thrum to something more strained, like a voice trying to hold a note that no longer fit its throat. He felt the weight of the bronze map in his pack, the same map that showed the six shrines and the channels beneath the city, now useless if they could not move west before the Blight reached the forest’s heart.

He opened his eyes when Torin spoke again. “You’re right,” Elias said. “She is pushing too hard.”

Maren did not stop. Her hands tightened slightly on Kaelen’s chest, and a thin vein appeared on her own forehead, dark and sudden against her skin. She was drawing from herself as much as from the ambient magic in the air. The warmth she generated was no longer just heat; it was a counter-current to the cold that seeped through the barrier’s weakened seal.

Kaelen stirred. His eyelids fluttered, but he did not open them fully. A sound escaped him—something between a sigh and a groan—that made Maren pause for a fraction of a second before she resumed her work. The warmth in his body was still there, but it felt thinner now, like water running through a cracked pipe.

“Listen,” Elias said softly. He did not mean to command; he meant to remind them that silence was part of the job. “Kaelen is asleep because he has nothing left to give. If we keep pulling from him while she tries to fill him back up, we are only accelerating his death.”

Torin shifted on his rock, the bread forgotten in his lap. He did not speak. His silence was a kind of answer. He looked at Maren, then at Kaelen, then at Elias. The debate had already begun, but neither side wanted to admit that they were running out of options.

Maren finally lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed, not from crying, but from the strain of holding the barrier’s frequency steady while she tried to reverse Kaelen’s unraveling. She did not speak. She did not need to. The fact that she was still there, still working, said more than any words could.

The barrier above them dimmed again. This time, the light did not recede smoothly; it flickered once, twice, then steadied at a lower intensity. The quartz stone in the center of the clearing glowed with a pale green hue, not the vibrant violet that had marked the barrier before the drain operation. The color had changed because the Blight was no longer being actively consumed by the barrier’s digestive mechanism; it was moving through the channels beneath them, westward, toward the forest.

The group understood what this meant without needing to be told. The barrier was stable, yes, but it was no longer a trap that ate the corruption—it was a shelter that held the line while the Blight advanced beyond it. They were safe for now, but only because the Blight had been diverted away from them. It was not a victory; it was a reprieve.

Elias stood up slowly, wincing as his side wound pulled tight against his ribs. He did not move toward the map or the pack; he simply stood, watching Maren’s hands work. He could feel the change in the air—the way the cold seemed to gather around the edges of the barrier, pressing against its weakened shell. The wind outside had picked up again, and the sound of distant rustling suggested that something large was moving through the ruins beyond their shelter.

Torin stood too, though he did not move closer to Elias. He kept his hands near his sword hilt, as if ready to draw it if needed. He did not want to fight; he wanted to stay still and watch Maren work. But the cold had crept into his bones, and he knew that warmth was a scarce resource now.

Elara sat cross-legged near the fire, her journal closed in her lap. She did not open it. She did not need to. The pages were filled with sketches of binding runes and diagrams of water flow mechanics, but none of them could explain what was happening to Kaelen right now. They could explain the theory, but not the reality of a man whose skin was becoming translucent as he lost his essence to the Blight’s pull.

Maren did not stop working. She did not look up when Elias stood, or when Torin moved closer to the fire. Her hands remained locked on Kaelen’s chest, her breath steady, her voice low and even. “He is stable,” she said finally. “For now.”

Elias nodded once. “Then we wait.”

Torin did not speak. He simply stood beside Elias, his presence a silent affirmation that they were still together, still in this place, still waiting for whatever came next. The barrier hummed above them, the light dim but steady, and Kaelen slept on, his body slowly regaining its opacity while Maren fought to keep him alive.

The wind outside grew stronger, carrying with it the scent of rain and rot. The barrier did not fail. It did not shatter. It held. But it was no longer a shield; it was a dam, holding back a tide that had already begun to move beyond its reach.

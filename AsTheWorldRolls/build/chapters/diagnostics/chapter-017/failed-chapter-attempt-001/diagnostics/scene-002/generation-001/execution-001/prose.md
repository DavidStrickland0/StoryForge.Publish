Torin blocked Elias from reaching the map table with his shoulder, not enough to strike, but firm enough to keep the pragmatist’s hand at bay. The older man had stepped away from the firelight, his silhouette sharp against the gloom of the watchfort interior, and planted himself between the bronze plate on the wall and the door that led out into the clearing. Torin did not speak first; he simply stood there, hands resting near the hilt of his sword, waiting for Elias to say something stupid or sensible.

Elias shifted his weight onto his good leg, the motion bringing a fresh twist of pain to his side. He had stopped wincing openly, keeping his face set and unreadable while he adjusted the angle of his stance. He did not move his hand away from the map, but he refused to step forward again. The silence between them was heavy enough to be felt in the air, thick with the smell of old wood, drying herbs, and rusted iron that seemed to cling to everything inside the gatehouse.

The barrier above them hummed faintly, a low vibration that traveled through the floorboards and up into the soles of their boots. It was no longer a shield; it was a dam, holding back a tide that had already begun to move beyond its reach. Elias knew this because he could see the way the light played across the stone floor, how the pale green glow seemed to pull outward rather than push inward. The barrier was not just weakening; it was being stretched thin by something pulling from the other side of the world, westward, where the forest lay and the source of the corruption waited.

Torin’s hands were large and scarred from border skirmishes that Elias had never seen but could imagine in the rough places on Torin’s face. They hovered near his sword hilt, not gripping it, but ready enough to draw if Elias made a move he did not want. Elias understood the physics of leverage and pressure better than anyone in the group, and he knew exactly how much force Torin would need to apply to shift him aside. He also knew that Torin was not going to use that force unless he believed it necessary, which meant the younger man was already committed to a course of action Elias found dangerous.

Elias had spent his life calculating odds, weighing risk against reward, and he did not like the feeling of being forced into a corner where every option seemed bad. He wanted to leave, or at least consider leaving, before the barrier failed completely. He wanted to take Kaelen out of this place while there was still a chance to do so safely. But Torin would not let him make that decision without a debate, and Elias could see now that Torin’s rigidity was not born of stupidity but of a protective instinct that ran deep in the older man’s chest.

Torin stepped closer, his boots creaking on the stone floor as he moved. He did not look at Elias; he looked past him, toward the barrier where the light flickered and dimmed, then steadied again. The wind outside had picked up, carrying with it the scent of rain and rot that seemed to seep through the walls even though the structure was thick and pitted by years of exposure. Elias could hear the sound of the wind rattling against the slits in the windows, a low moan that sounded like something alive moving through the trees beyond the watchfort.

Elias closed his eyes for a moment, letting the vibration of the barrier settle into his bones. He could feel it pulsing beneath his feet, a rhythm that was not quite his own but one he had learned to recognize over time. It was the same frequency as Kaelen’s magic, and it was the same frequency that fueled the Blight. The group had spent weeks trying to contain it, to push it back, to use it as a tool rather than an enemy. But now the tool was turning against them, and the only way to stop it was to let it through in a controlled surge.

Elias opened his eyes and looked at Torin again. The younger man’s expression was unreadable, his jaw set tight, his brows furrowed as if he were trying to remember something important. He did not speak, but his silence was louder than any argument Elias could have made. It was a statement in itself, a refusal to yield, a declaration that the group would stay and fight until one of them gave up or the barrier broke.

Elias turned away from Torin and walked toward the fire, where Elara sat cross-legged with her journal closed on her lap. She did not look up when he approached, but she nodded once, acknowledging his presence without breaking her focus on the space between Kaelen and Maren. Elias could see the strain in Maren’s posture, the way her hands were locked on Kaelen’s chest as if she were trying to hold him together with nothing but willpower. Her forehead was lined with sweat, veins visible beneath the skin where his magic had burned away the flesh and left only the raw tissue beneath.

Elias did not comment on what he saw; he simply sat down beside Elara, resting his back against the wall and closing his eyes again. The fire crackled softly, sending sparks up into the darkness above them. He could hear the sound of Torin’s breathing, steady and controlled, as if he were trying to keep himself calm enough to face whatever came next. Elias knew that Torin’s fear was not irrational; it was a rational response to the situation they found themselves in. But Elias also knew that their options were limited, and that leaving now would mean abandoning Kaelen to whatever forces were pulling at him from the west.

The barrier hummed above them, the light dim but steady, and Kaelen slept on, his body slowly regaining its opacity while Maren fought to keep him alive. Elias could feel the heat of the fire warming his face, but it did not reach the cold spots where the Blight’s influence had seeped into the stone walls. He could hear the wind rattling against the windows, a sound that seemed to grow louder with every passing moment, as if the world outside was trying to break through.

Elias opened his eyes again and looked at the map on the wall. The lines etched into the bronze plate showed the temple, six smaller shrines, and channels beneath the city.

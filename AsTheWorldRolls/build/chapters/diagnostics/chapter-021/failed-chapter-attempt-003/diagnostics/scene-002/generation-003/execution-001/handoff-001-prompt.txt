Elias watched Elara’s hands as she raised the chisel. They were steady, a fact that seemed to contradict the earlier admission of her own fatigue. He had seen Maren’s left arm twitch involuntarily when she reached for anything too small. That kind of stiffness was not something you could simply ignore in a crisis.

“Wait,” Elias said, his voice cutting through the low hum of conversation around them. The common room was warm, filled with the scent of roasted roots and damp wool, but the air still felt thin after their journey through the forest. “Let me see what you’re planning before we commit.”

Elara lowered the chisel slightly, resting the handle against her palm. She did not look defensive; she looked thoughtful. “I intend to strike the quartz at the same rhythm I used on the ridge,” she said. “Slow, deliberate taps. The frequency doesn’t come from speed. It comes from consistency.”

“Consistency with a trembling hand?” Aldric asked. He had been leaning against the bar, nursing a mug of ale that was long cold. His eyes narrowed as he glanced between Elara and Maren, who sat at the table wrapped in her cloak, watching her hands as if they belonged to someone else.

Maren shook her head once. “I won’t be trying it again,” she said quietly. “Not until my arm is steady enough to hold a needle without shaking.” She did not look away from Elias. “Elara has the mechanical skill. I have the medical knowledge. If we’re going to do this, we’ll need both.”

Torin nodded silently beside her. He had been quiet since they entered the tavern, his gaze fixed on the door as if expecting a threat to burst through at any moment. But now he turned his attention back to the table. “The ridge test worked,” he said finally. “That means the seal isn’t too strong. It just needs the right vibration.”

Elara nodded again. “Exactly. The quartz stone responds to specific frequencies, like a tuning fork struck against glass. If we match that frequency with the chisel, the seal will weaken without breaking apart. Too fast and it shatters; too slow and nothing happens.”

She raised the chisel once more, holding it at an angle that suggested precision rather than force. “I’ve practiced on scrap stone all night,” she said. “I know the rhythm required to hit the right frequency without cracking it.”

Aldric grunted. He wasn’t a man who often voiced approval in public, but there was a certain kind of respect hidden behind his scowl. “Don’t let anyone tell you otherwise,” he muttered. “If you can’t hold steady for a few minutes with practice, you’re not ready for the real thing.”

Elara smiled faintly. There was no arrogance in her expression. Only quiet confidence born of hours spent refining a single skill until it became muscle memory. “I understand,” she replied. “Maren’s right; she’s too valuable to risk on tools that require fine control. And I’m not going to make this harder than it needs to be.”

Elias leaned forward, resting his elbows on the table. The map lay spread out before him, its bronze plate etched with lines showing the temple, six smaller shrines, and the web of channels beneath the city. It had taken them weeks to decipher the meaning behind those lines, but now they understood enough to act.

“The seal is weaker than we thought,” Elias said. “That changes everything.” He tapped a finger against the map near the outline of the shrine they’d been targeting. “We don’t need brute force anymore. We just need to match the resonance.”

Torin’s eyes flicked to Kaelen, who sat at the far end of the table. His brass gauntlet lay beside his knee, cold and inert now that the immediate crisis had passed. The man looked tired, his skin pale under the lantern light, but he was breathing evenly and showed no sign of collapse. Maren had stabilized him with her salves, and though the damage would take time to heal, he was alive and functional again.

Kaelen met Torin’s gaze and gave a small nod. “I can listen,” he said. “Not pull anymore. Just listen. Guide the resonance into the barrier so it eats the Blight without me getting pulled in.”

Maren reached out and gently touched his hand, offering warmth through her fingers. “Your body is already tired,” she whispered softly. “You don’t have to push yourself beyond what’s safe. Just let them guide you where you’re needed.”

Kaelen closed his eyes briefly, then nodded again. “Understood,” he replied. “I’ll stay within limits. No more pulling. Just listening.”

Elara stepped back slightly, lowering the chisel once more. “We’ve got enough information to move forward,” she said. “The ridge test confirmed the method. Now we just need to execute it properly at dawn.”

Aldric cleared his throat. “You’ll eat first,” he said sternly. “Then you rest for an hour. After that, you’re back on duty.” He gestured toward a serving tray near the fireplace where stew and bread waited untouched. “Someone has to keep their strength up if we’re going to be fighting a war against physics tonight.”

Maren pushed her bowl aside with a smile. “We’ll eat,” she agreed. “And then we’ll sleep. But when morning comes, Elara takes the chisel and Kaelen guides the resonance.”

Torin nodded once more. “No arguments. We do exactly as planned.” He looked around the table, making eye contact with each person in turn. “We’ve got this. We’ve done worse than this before.”

Elias stood up slowly, adjusting his leather armor so it didn’t creak too loudly in the quiet room. “Good,” he said. “Because tomorrow isn’t just another day. It’s the moment we decide whether the barrier holds or if it shatters under pressure.” He paused, looking at Elara again. “You’ll lead the operation. Kaelen will be your anchor. Torin and Maren will watch for anything unexpected. Aldric has already told me he’ll send reinforcements if needed.”

Elara nodded. “I understand,” she said. “We’re ready when you are.”

They ate in silence afterward, the sounds of chewing and sipping mingling with the crackle of the hearth fire. Outside, wind drifted through the cracks in the tavern walls, carrying whispers of snow and distant hooves. Inside, the group felt something shift between them—not relief exactly, but clarity. They had failed once, yes, but not because they lacked skill or courage. They had failed because someone assumed a role without accounting for physical limitations. That mistake wouldn’t happen again.

When they finished eating, Torin stood up first. “Time to rest,” he said. “We’ll meet here in two hours.” He headed toward the stairs leading upstairs, followed by Maren and Kaelen, who moved carefully despite his exhaustion.

Elias remained seated for a moment longer, studying the map before folding it back into its protective case. Elara picked up her chisel one last time, turning it over in her hands as if testing its balance. She looked at Elias with something approaching admiration—not pity, but recognition of someone who had earned trust through action rather than words.

“We’ll be back before dawn,” she said softly. “Ready for whatever comes next.”

“Then we’ll wait for you,” Elias replied. “And when you return, we’ll know what to expect.”

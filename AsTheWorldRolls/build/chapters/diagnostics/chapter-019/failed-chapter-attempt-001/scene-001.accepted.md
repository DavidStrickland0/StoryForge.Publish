The first breath did not feel like air filling lungs so much as water trying to enter a cracked vessel. Kaelen’s chest heaved, a dry, scraping motion that tore at his ribs. The sensation was foreign yet terrifyingly familiar, the phantom echo of a life lived before the gauntlet had become an extension of his own arm and the veins beneath his skin turned into conduits for a force he did not command but somehow carried.

He opened his eyes.

The ceiling of the watchfort room swam into focus in jagged fragments. It was low, reinforced with thick iron bands that groaned in places where the wood had rotted away under years of damp and Blight seepage. A single lantern hung from a beam above him, its flame dancing with an erratic flicker that made the shadows stretch and distort on the stone walls. The light was dim, casting long, pale shapes against the rough plaster. The air inside smelled of woodsmoke, old herbs, and the underlying metallic tang of rusted iron that had permeated everything in this place since the Blight arrived.

Maren sat beside his cot.

She was slumped forward slightly, one hand resting on his chest where her fingers were pressed against the cold, translucent skin of his torso. The dark veins beneath were still visible, though they seemed to have receded further into the flesh than Elias had noted moments before. They looked less like rivers and more like bruises healing from deep within. Her face was pale, illuminated by the weak lantern light, her eyes half-closed in exhaustion. She did not look at him as he regained consciousness; she simply watched the rise and fall of his chest with a quiet, practiced intensity that spoke of someone used to monitoring vital signs long before such technology existed.

Kaelen tried to sit up, but his muscles felt like lead weights dragged through wet sand. The effort sent a wave of nausea rolling through him, cold and sharp. He gritted his teeth, forcing himself to lean against the headboard for support. The brass gauntlet on his left wrist felt heavier than it should have, yet strangely stable. It was warm now, no longer the dead weight of ice that had anchored him to the earth while he slept. The runes etched into its surface were dormant, silent, but the metal hummed with a faint, low-frequency vibration—a sound only he could hear, like a distant bell tolling in an empty cathedral.

“Easy,” Maren said, her voice rough from disuse. She didn’t pull away as he shifted; instead, she increased the pressure of her hand on his chest just enough to steady him without crushing him. “Don’t strain yourself yet.”

“I’m awake,” Kaelen muttered, the words catching in his throat. He cleared his dry mouth and tried again, enunciating more carefully this time. “I’m awake.”

Maren nodded once, a small, tired gesture. Her grip remained firm but gentle. “Good. We’ve been waiting for you to respond.”

The simplicity of her statement carried a weight that went beyond words. They had waited. All of them. The silence in the room had been filled with nothing but the crackle of the hearth fire and the occasional shift of someone settling into their exhaustion. Torin stood at the doorway, his silhouette framed by the darkness beyond, one hand resting on the hilt of his sword. Elias was near the hearth, tending to the iron pot that served as a makeshift heat extraction system. Elara sat cross-legged on the floor beside the cot, her small notebook open but blank, her pen idle in her lap.

Kaelen’s gaze drifted past Maren’s shoulder, toward the doorway where Torin stood guard. The man’s profile was sharp against the gloom, his scarred face unreadable in the semi-darkness. He had not moved during Kaelen’s sleep, his posture rigid with a kind of military discipline that bordered on obsession. Kaelen wondered if Torin had slept at all, or if he had simply closed his eyes while keeping one ear tuned to every sound outside the room.

The barrier outside continued its slow, rhythmic thrumming, a deep, resonant pulse that vibrated through the floorboards and up into Kaelen’s bones. It was no longer the steady beat of a heart but something heavier, slower, like the breathing of a creature far larger than any man could comprehend. The violet hue had deepened, bruising the pale white light of the barrier until it looked like fresh blood under moonlight. It pulsed in time with Kaelen’s own heartbeat, a disorienting synchronization that made him feel as though he were part of the same organism as the force holding back the Blight.

Elias stirred near the hearth. He wiped his hands on a rag before moving to the small table where Elias kept his tools and maps. The bronze plate map lay flat under a layer of parchment scraps, its lines showing the temple, six smaller shrines, and channels beneath the city. A water skin sat half-full next to it, its contents clear and still despite the tension in the room.

“Your gauntlet is stable,” Elias said, his voice low and practical. He didn’t look up from the map as he spoke. “No erratic fluctuations. No sudden surges. Just… listening.”

Kaelen glanced at his wrist. The brass band felt cool against his skin now, yet warm enough to suggest life rather than death. The runes were dull gray, dormant but not dead. They waited for a command that hadn’t come yet. “It’s holding,” Kaelen replied. “For now.”

“For how long?” Maren asked, her voice cutting through the quiet tension. She didn’t sound angry, only weary. Her eyes remained fixed on Kaelen’s face, searching for some sign of weakness or instability that might slip past his defenses. There was none to be found, but her gaze lingered anyway, as if she were afraid to blink and lose him entirely.

“As long as it needs me to,” Kaelen said. He meant it, though the words felt hollow in his mouth. The magic inside him was not his own; it belonged to something vast and hungry that had found a way to use him as a conduit. To say otherwise would be a lie, even if it were the only thing keeping them from tearing each other apart.

Elias closed the map with a soft thud. “The barrier is still pulsing, but the rhythm has changed. It’s pushing back harder than before. That means the seal at the Gate of Silence is failing faster than we calculated.”

“Then why haven’t we moved?” Torin asked, his voice flat and devoid of emotion. He had stepped into the room without making a sound, his boots landing softly on the wooden floor. His sword was sheathed, but his stance betrayed readiness. “We can’t stay here forever while the thing outside gets stronger.”

“Because moving Kaelen breaks the resonance protocol,” Elias replied without turning around. “And breaking the resonance destabilizes the gauntlet. We don’t know how long he can hold the flow before collapsing again. If we move him now, we risk everything.”

Torin’s jaw tightened visibly. “I said I’d strike him if he lost control. That doesn’t mean I want to leave him behind.”

“No one wants that,” Maren said sharply. She finally looked up from Kaelen, her expression hardening into something that resembled anger more than concern. “But you don’t get to decide the terms of this battle alone, Torin. We’re all in this together.”

Torin opened his mouth to argue, but he stopped when Elara spoke up from the floor beside the cot. “There’s no point in arguing about what we should do until Kaelen tells us what he needs,” she said quietly. Her voice was small but steady, carrying a weight that silenced the others. She closed her notebook and tucked it into the folds of her cloak. “He just woke up. Let him catch his breath before we start demanding answers.”

Kaelen nodded slowly, though he couldn’t help but feel a flicker of relief at the interruption. The debate between Torin and Elias had been real, but it had also been exhausting, each side convinced of its own righteousness while ignoring the fact that neither could act without risking the other’s life. Now that he was awake, the dynamic shifted slightly, even if only by degrees.

The air in the room grew warmer, not from the hearth fire but from something else entirely. It started as a subtle shift—a faint warmth spreading through the space that made Maren let out a short, involuntary sigh. Her fingers loosened their grip on his chest just enough for him to feel the change without breaking contact. The lantern above flickered brighter, its flame steadying for a brief moment before dimming again.

Kaelen felt the same warmth spreading through his own body, though it came with a cost. His skin grew less translucent, the dark veins fading until they were barely visible beneath the surface of his flesh. It was not healing; it was simply masking, a temporary stabilization achieved by drawing on reserves he did not possess and could not replenish. The warmth faded quickly, leaving behind a chill that settled deep in his bones and made him shiver.

“Thank you,” Kaelen whispered to Maren, though he wasn’t sure who exactly he was thanking. She had used magic to stabilize him during his sleep, but now that he was awake, the effect wore off almost instantly. The cost of her intervention was already visible in the way her hands trembled slightly as she pulled them away from his chest.

The room smelled of old wood, drying herbs, and rusted iron. The single lantern hanging from a beam above the heavy oak table cast long, wavering shadows against the thick walls. Outside, the barrier pulsing with a bruised violet hue vibrated through the floorboards, a deep thrumming that rattled Kaelen’s teeth. He had pushed his body past its natural limits to wake up, and now the recoil was hitting him like a physical blow.

“Keep your head down,” Maren said, her voice tight. She stood close enough that he could feel the heat radiating from her skin, or rather, the lack of it. Her hands were shaking visibly at her sides, the tremors fighting against her will to hold them steady. “Elias says the seal is holding for now, but if you try to pull too hard, the vibration will shatter the quartz.”

“I’m listening,” Kaelen said, keeping his tone flat. He tested the brass gauntlet on his wrist. It was warm now, no longer cold and inert, humming with a low-frequency vibration that felt less like a foreign engine turning over and more like a pulse synced to his own heartbeat. But the warmth came at a cost. His skin felt tight, stretched over bone, and the dark veins beneath his translucent flesh glowed faintly in the dim light. If he pushed too far, they would burst again.

Elias stood near the hearth, poking the dying embers with a piece of iron. The heat extracted from the fire was circulating through the room, keeping Maren’s core from freezing entirely, but the man himself looked weary. His shoulder injury throbbed with every movement, and he kept glancing at the map on the table, tracing the lines with a charcoal pencil that had seen better days.

“Flow velocity is dropping,” Elias called out without looking up. “The barrier is eating slower than before. If we don’t feed it soon, it will stagnate.”

Kaelen closed his eyes and reached for the pull in his chest. It wasn’t a hunger, not exactly. It was more like a current running through him, waiting for a switch to be flipped. He didn’t feel fear; he felt calculation. This was what he was built for. He was the valve. He was the conduit. The rest of them were just mechanics and muscle keeping the machine from breaking apart.

He exhaled sharply and let his hand drift toward the center of the room. The air between them thickened, growing heavy with static charge. A visible shimmer rippled across the floor, and the lantern’s flame bent away from the invisible wind he generated.

“Kaelen?” Maren whispered. “Don’t—”

“I need to stabilize the veins,” he interrupted, his voice steady despite the nausea rising in his gut. He pushed a little more. The violet light of the barrier outside dimmed for a fraction of a second before brightening again, as if it were breathing. Inside the room, the temperature dropped slightly where the barrier’s magic leaked, but he countered it with the heat stored in the floor and the walls.

His skin tingled. The fissures on his forearms, now dry scars, pulsed with a dull ache. He could feel the Blight moving west through the channels beneath them, a slow, viscous tide that felt like drinking water through a straw while the straw was being pulled from both ends. If he didn’t reinforce the seal, the pressure would build until it burst the stone at the Gate of Silence, flooding this room with corruption and turning the group into walking corpses before they could even step out the door.

“Hold him,” Torin said quietly from the doorway. The soldier’s large scarred hands rested on the hilt of his sword, but he didn’t move closer. He knew when a man was ready to fight or when he needed to be held back. Kaelen wasn’t fighting anyone in this room; he was fighting the physics of his own nature.

Elias stepped forward, placing a calloused hand on Kaelen’s shoulder. “If you push too hard, the resonance will break the seal,” Elias warned. “We can’t afford another leak.”

“I know the protocol,” Kaelen replied. He didn’t look at Elias. He kept his eyes fixed on the spot where the wall met the floor, watching for any sign of instability. The barrier’s rhythm was shifting from a steady beat to something slower, heavier, as if it were digesting too much too fast. If they didn’t feed it now, it would starve and collapse.

“Torin, watch Maren,” Elias ordered. “If she starts shaking too badly, I’ll have to take over the hearth.”

Maren nodded, though her hands continued to tremble as she moved a small clay pot containing amber-colored salve toward the table. The smell of beeswax and dried lavender filled the room, masking the scent of copper and rot that clung to their clothes. She had used her last reserves to keep Kaelen alive through the worst part of his sleep. Now she needed rest, but she couldn’t afford it yet. If Kaelen collapsed, they would all die together in this room.

Kaelen focused on the pull again. The sensation was like being dragged underwater, forced to inhale against a closed throat. He gritted his teeth and let the current carry him forward, just enough to reinforce the seal without breaking it. The air grew warmer as he stabilized the temperature around Maren, shielding her from the cold seeping through the stone walls. Her tremors subsided for a moment, allowing her to take a breath she hadn’t realized she’d been holding.

He didn’t say thank you again. It wasn’t something he did lightly. He simply acknowledged the exchange with a nod and returned his focus to the barrier. The violet light outside pulsed in time with his own heartbeat, a slow, rhythmic thrum that felt less like a warning and more like a confirmation of what they were doing.

Elara sat on the floor near the hearth, her notebook closed beside her. She watched Kaelen’s movements with sharp, observant eyes, noting every subtle shift in his posture. “The flow is stabilizing,” she said softly. “But the pressure differential is increasing. We need to act before dawn.”

“We can’t move him yet,” Torin said, stepping into the room and closing the heavy oak door behind him. The sound of the latch clicking was loud in the quiet space. “If we move him now, the resonance will destabilize.”

“I know,” Kaelen replied, his voice flat. He didn’t argue. He simply kept pushing, keeping the barrier stable while his own veins threatened to burst under the strain. The dark veins beneath his skin glowed faintly in the dim light, mapping the flow of magic through him like a network of rivers.

Elias glanced at the map again, tracing the lines that showed the temple, six smaller shrines, and channels beneath the city. “The Gate of Silence is failing faster than we calculated,” he said. “If the seal breaks, the surge will flood this room.”

“Then we keep it open,” Kaelen said. He didn’t explain why. He didn’t need to. The group knew the stakes. They had all agreed to stay and wait for dawn, even though Torin wanted to move immediately. Even though Elias argued that moving would break the resonance protocol. Even though Maren was physically failing from exhaustion.

The barrier outside groaned softly, a sound like stone settling in a cave. It wasn’t structural failure; it was a change in frequency. The seal at the Gate of Silence was weakening under the increasing pressure differential. If they didn’t act now, it would shatter and flood the room with corruption.

Kaelen pushed harder. The air grew warmer. The lanterns brightened. The barrier’s violet pulse slowed to match his own rhythm. He felt the weight of their decision pressing down on him, but he didn’t falter. He was the valve. He was the conduit. He was the only reason they were still alive in this room.

“Good,” Elias said, nodding at the map. “Keep it steady.”

Maren sat back down near the hearth, pulling her cloak tighter around herself. She didn’t speak, but she acknowledged him with a small tilt of her head. He could feel the warmth radiating from her body, a physical manifestation of her care and commitment. It wasn’t magic anymore; it was something deeper, something that couldn’t be measured or quantified.

Torin leaned against the doorframe, his sword still in hand. “If anyone tries to leave,” he said, “I’ll stop them.”

“Understood,” Kaelen replied. He didn’t need to elaborate. The group knew what was at stake. They knew that moving him now would break the resonance protocol and destabilize the gauntlet. They knew that staying meant waiting for dawn, even though it felt like an eternity.

Elara stood up and walked over to the table, picking up her chisel and quartz stone. She examined the glowing runes around the temple entrance, which had awakened in windows, streets, and broken towers throughout the city. Only the spires and the towering shape remained visible above the fog. The forest was ancient, with gnarled trees and roots forming natural barriers. The edge of the forest revealed open, rolling hills beyond.

“The resonance is shifting,” Elara said, her voice barely audible. “The seal at the Gate of Silence isn’t holding the flow back anymore. The pressure differential is increasing the velocity.”

“Then we act before dawn,” Elias said, looking at the map again. “We follow the flow westward through the channels toward the forest.”

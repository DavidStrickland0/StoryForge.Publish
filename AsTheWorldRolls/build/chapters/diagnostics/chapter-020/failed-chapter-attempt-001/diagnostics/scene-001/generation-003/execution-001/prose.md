The food arrived before the silence did.

Two clay bowls of thick stew, ladled from a pot that seemed to have been simmering since the dawn. The steam rose in gentle, aromatic plumes, carrying the scent of root vegetables, cured meat, and herbs Elias couldn’t name. Beside each bowl sat a heel of dark bread, dense and crusty, and a small clay cup of something amber and sharp.

Elias pushed his glass aside. The water had served its purpose, flushing the taste of travel dust and iron from his mouth. Now, his hand moved to the bread. He tore a piece, the crust flaking slightly, and dipped it into the stew. The heat of the broth warmed the inside of his fingers, a pleasant, grounding sensation that contrasted with the lingering chill of the forest air clinging to his skin.

Around him, the common room of the Pick and Axe hummed with a low, steady rhythm. It was the sound of normalcy, a stark and welcome departure from the jagged, screaming silences of the Blight’s domain. Men in leather aprons argued over dice rolls. A woman with a child on her hip laughed at something a broad-shouldered miner had whispered. The noise was not chaotic; it was a tapestry of overlapping voices, clinking pottery, and the scratch of quills on parchment, weaving together into a fabric of safe, mundane existence.

Torin ate with the mechanical efficiency of a man who had learned that hunger was a distraction best eliminated quickly. He did not speak, but his eyes remained open, scanning the room, cataloging exits, assessing the patrons. His posture was relaxed, but his shoulders were square, a habit of war that no amount of clean ale or warm bread seemed to fully dissolve.

Maren ate more slowly. She tore her bread into smaller pieces, tasting the stew with a critical, almost analytical expression. She was wiping her hands on a clean cloth Aldric had provided, her movements precise and deliberate. There was a professional rigidity to her posture, a reminder that she was not merely a traveler seeking sustenance, but a practitioner of her craft observing a new environment. Her eyes kept drifting to the window, watching the twilight deepening into true night, as if expecting the shadows to lengthen and reach for the glass.

Kaelen did not touch his food.

He sat with his hands resting on the table, fingers curled slightly around the edge of the wood. His gaze was fixed on a point somewhere above the hearth, but his eyes did not track the firelight. They were wide, unblinking, and distant. The tremor in his hands had subsided, replaced by a stillness that was far more unsettling than the shaking. It was the stillness of a machine that had been switched off, or perhaps a body that had simply forgotten how to move.

Aldric approached the table again. He held a sheaf of papers, his ledger tucked under his arm. His expression was neutral, the bureaucratic mask firmly in place, though there was a slight softening of the lines around his eyes that had not been present during the initial negotiation.

"The stew is on the house," Aldric said, his voice carrying just enough weight to be heard over the ambient noise without raising to a shout. "As is the room for the night. The escort contract will be discussed at first light. For now, I ask only that you sign the waiver. It is standard procedure. It protects you, and it protects me."

Elias took the pen. The ink was black and slightly viscous. He uncapped it, the scent of pine resin and iron oxide sharp in his nose. He looked at the document. It was dense with text, columns of fine script listing liabilities, indemnifications, and clauses that seemed to loop back on themselves in an attempt to cover every conceivable disaster.

He signed. He did not read it. He had learned, in the weeks since the city fell, that reading the fine print of a dying world was a luxury he could not afford. The act of signing was a ritual, a small, human acknowledgment of consequence. He capped the pen and handed the quill to Torin.

Torin signed. Maren signed.

When it came to Kaelen, the silence stretched.

Aldric looked at the young man. Kaelen had not moved. His head was turned slightly to the left, his jaw tight. He was not asleep; his breathing was shallow but steady. But he was not present.

"He is with us," Maren said. Her voice was quiet, but it cut through the room's hum. She did not look at Aldric; she looked at Kaelen. "He is just... far away right now. He will sign. But not now."

Aldric’s eyes flickered to Maren, then back to Kaelen. He calculated the risk. He saw the tremor in Maren’s hand, the way she gripped the edge of the table to steady herself. He saw the exhaustion in Elias’s eyes, the tension in Torin’s shoulders. He was a business man, but he was also a man who had survived the first weeks of the Blight by keeping his head down and his customers fed.

"The waiver remains unsigned for him," Aldric said, his tone businesslike, devoid of pity. "When he is ready, he may sign. In the meantime, consider the clause suspended. But the room is paid for, and the food is eaten. That is the agreement."

He turned and walked away, his feet silent on the polished wood.

The room seemed to exhale.

Elias looked at Maren. She was staring at Kaelen with a mixture of frustration and fear. She reached out, her hand hovering over his shoulder, but she did not touch him. She pulled her hand back, tucking it into her sleeve.

"I need to check his pulse," she whispered. "His breathing is too shallow. If he has lost consciousness, his heart rate will be erratic. I need to confirm he is stable before we discuss the contract."

She moved to the other side of the table. She took Kaelen’s wrist. Her fingers were steady as she counted the beats, her lips moving silently, counting under her breath. One, two, three...

Elias watched her. He saw the tension in her neck, the way her eyes were narrowed in concentration. He knew what she was looking for. Not just a pulse, but a rhythm. A life.

After a moment, she let out a breath she seemed to have been holding for a long time.

"He is stable," she said. "But his mind is not here. I can feel the disconnect in his physical responses. He is dissociating. It is a defense mechanism. He is pulling away from the pain, or the memory, or the resonance. I cannot force him back. If I try, I risk triggering a shock response. We must wait."

"Wait for what?" Torin asked. He was cleaning the remnants of the stew from his bowl with the last of his bread. "Dawn? We have a job to do. A caravan to protect. The entity is still out there, Maren. It is not going to wait for Kaelen to find his way back to his own skin."

Maren looked up. Her face was pale, the candlelight catching the sweat on her forehead. "I am not a physician who can simply fix a broken mind, Torin. I am a healer. I can treat wounds. I can set bones. I can stabilize a body. But I cannot pull a soul back from the edge of the void. Not without risking his death. And I will not risk his death for the sake of a contract."

Elias leaned back in his chair. The wood creaked under his weight. He looked at the window. The night was fully settled now. The stars were beginning to appear, faint pinpricks against the darkness. Beyond the stars, far to the east, the black spires of the village were just visible against the horizon, a jagged silhouette against the fading twilight.

They were still there.

He picked up the amber cup and drank. The liquid was sweet and strong, burning a path down his throat. It was good. It was warm. It was real.

"We do not have to wait for him to sign," Elias said. His voice was low, calm. "We have the waiver. We have the food. We have the room. We are here. We are alive. That is enough for tonight."

He looked at Maren. "Let him sleep. Let him drift. But we cannot let the situation drift with him. We need to prepare for what comes in the morning. We need to understand what we are walking into. The map shows the route. But the map does not show the minds of the people we are about to meet. The caravan. The merchants. The guards. They have their own fears. Their own expectations. We need to be ready to listen, not just to fight."

Maren nodded slowly. She placed a hand on Kaelen’s head, her touch light, almost feather-like. "I will stay with him. I will monitor his breathing. If he changes, I will wake you."

"I will keep watch," Torin said. He pushed his chair back. "The room has a window. I can see the street. If anyone comes, I will know."

Elias stood up. His joints popped, aching with the effort. He was tired, but the exhaustion had changed. It was no longer the sharp, hungry panic of the forest. It was a deep, bone-weariness, a heaviness that felt almost like peace.

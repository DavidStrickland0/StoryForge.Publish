Aldric set the ledger down with a deliberate thud, the sound cutting through the low hum of the tavern. He did not look at the group. He looked at the wall. He looked at the window. He looked at the ledger again, as if the document had offered a new, more appealing perspective.

"The waiver," he said, his voice flat and dry. "Clause four. Liability for property damage during the intake of services. And clause seven. The prohibition against introducing unregistered fauna into the common room."

Elias blinked. He looked down at the table. There was no fauna. There was only a pitcher of water, a stack of napkins, and the faint, lingering scent of the mud they had walked through.

"We are not bringing in any creatures," Torin said, his voice low and careful. He was leaning back in his chair, his broad shoulders pressing against the wood. "Just five people. And some gear."

"Gear," Aldric repeated. He turned to face them. His eyes were sharp, missing nothing. "You call that gear? I see a chisel. I see a stone that looks like it has seen the inside of a volcano. I see a man with a brass gauntlet that is currently vibrating at a frequency that is making my teeth ache. And I see a healer who is not a physician."

Maren stiffened. Her hand moved instinctively to her satchel, a reflex born of habit and the fear of having her tools questioned.

"I am a healer," Maren said. Her voice was steady, but Elias could see the tension in her jaw. "I treat the sick. I stabilize the injured. That is what I do."

"Here," Aldric said, tapping the counter, "we distinguish between those who practice medicine and those who dabble in it. The Guild of Alchemists does not recognize your seal. The Magistrate does not know your name. Therefore, for the purposes of this establishment, you are a risk. You are a variable. And variables, in my experience, tend to introduce fire or poison or both."

Kaelen was silent. He sat hunched over, his hands resting on the table, fingers twitching slightly against the wood. The brass gauntlet on his wrist was still, the runes dark. But Elias could feel the vibration in the air, a low thrum that seemed to come from the stones of the tavern itself. It was the residue of the Blight, or perhaps the memory of it, clinging to the metal.

Elias reached into his pocket and pulled out the liability waiver. He unfolded it. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, and smelled of ink and oil.

He read the first line. *The undersigned acknowledges that the Pick and Axe is a place of hospitality, commerce, and rest.*

He read the second line. *The undersigned acknowledges that the proprietor reserves the right to refuse service to any individual whose presence is deemed a threat to the structural integrity, financial solvency, or general sanity of the establishment.*

Elias looked up. "You want us to sign that we might hurt your sanity."

"I want you to sign that you might hurt my beer," Aldric corrected. "Sanity is a bonus. Beer is a commodity."

Torin let out a breath that was almost a laugh. It was the sound of a man who had been holding his breath for days. "We will sign."

"Good," Aldric said. He pulled a quill from behind his ear. "And I want the room cleaned before you sit. I don't care if you are exhausted. I don't care if you are bleeding. You will wipe the mud off your boots, you will wash your hands, and you will not bring that smell into the common room. The other patrons are paying for atmosphere. I will not have it spoiled by the scent of a dead forest."

The other patrons.

Elias turned his head. The common room was small, lit by a dozen tallow candles and the warm glow of the hearth. It was not empty. It was not quiet.

There were six other tables occupied. A pair of miners, their faces lined and gray, were drinking dark ale and talking in low voices. A woman in a traveling cloak was eating a bowl of stew, her eyes fixed on the door. A young man, perhaps no older than Kaelen, was sharpening a knife, the rasp of steel on whetstone a rhythmic counterpoint to the fire crackling.

They were watching.

The miners had stopped talking. The woman with the stew had put down her spoon. The young man had paused his sharpening.

They were looking at the five of them. At the mud on their clothes. At the blood on Kaelen’s side. At the brass gauntlet. At the chisel. At the stone.

Elias met the eyes of the miner on the left. The miner’s gaze was flat, unreadable, but there was a hardness there. A recognition.

They knew.

They knew what they were. Not just travelers. Not just adventurers.

They were the ones who had walked out of the Blight.

Elias picked up the quill. The ink was black, wet, and smelling of iron. He signed his name. *Elias Thorne.*

He passed the quill to Torin.

Torin signed. *Torin.*

Maren signed. *Maren.*

Kaelen did not move.

Elias looked at him. Kaelen’s eyes were open, but they were not seeing the table. They were not seeing the room. They were seeing something else, something far away, something that hummed and pulsed in the dark.

"Kaelen," Elias said softly.

The name hung in the air.

Kaelen’s head turned. Slowly. His eyes focused, for a moment, on Elias’s face. There was a flicker of recognition. A flicker of fear.

Then the dissociation took him again. His eyes went blank. His hand tightened on the edge of the table. The wood creaked.

Aldric watched. He said nothing. He did not need to.

Elias took the quill. He signed Kaelen’s name. *Kaelen.*

He handed the paper to Aldric.

Aldric took it. He read the signatures. He checked the date. He checked the time. He checked the weather, which was raining, according to a small window at the back of the room.

He rolled the paper up. He tucked it into his pocket.

"The room is on the second floor," Aldric said. "Two beds. One of them is broken, so you will have to share. Or you can pay for another room, which is three times the price, and I will not be recommending it to you."

"We will take the two beds," Elias said.

"Very well."

Aldric walked away. He went to the bar. He poured a measure of water into a clean glass. He set it on the table.

"Drink," he said. "And try to look like human beings for the next ten minutes. I have a reputation to maintain."

Elias picked up the glass. He drank.

The water was cold. It was clean. It tasted like nothing.

It was the best thing he had ever tasted.

He looked at Maren. She was watching the miners. They were whispering.

He looked at Torin. Torin was rubbing his shoulders, the way a man rubs a bruise.

He looked at Kaelen. Kaelen was staring at the wall.

And he looked at the chisel.

The chisel was on the table. It was a simple tool. Steel handle. Sharpened tip.

It was the key.

It was the thing that would open the seal. It was the thing that would feed the barrier. It was the thing that would keep them alive.

And it was sitting on a table in a tavern, in a town that had forgotten the name of the Blight, in a world that was trying to move on.

Elias picked up the chisel.

He held it up. The candlelight caught the edge. It glinted.

He needed to test it.

He needed to know if it still worked.

He needed to know if the resonance was still there.

He looked at Maren.

"We need to do a test," he said.

Maren looked at him. Her eyes were tired. Her hands were shaking.

She reached for the chisel.

Her fingers trembled.

The chisel wavered.

She pulled her hand back.

"I can't," she whispered.

The room was silent.

The miners had stopped whispering.

The woman with the stew was watching.

The young man was watching.

Elias looked at the chisel. He looked at Maren.

He looked at Elara.

Elara was standing by the door.

She had not spoken. She had not moved.

She was watching.

Her hands were steady.

Her eyes were clear.

She reached out.

She took the chisel from Elias’s hand.

Her grip was firm.

The chisel was still.

Elias looked at her.

She looked at the chisel.

She looked at the stone.

She looked at Kaelen.

And she understood.

She knew what had to be done.

She knew how to do it.

And she was ready.

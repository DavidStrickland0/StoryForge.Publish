The lantern’s flame shuddered, throwing a jagged shadow across the stone floor. Elias blinked, the darkness of sleep dissolving into the dull, gray light of the gatehouse. He didn’t move for a moment, his body still heavy with the inertia of unconsciousness, before the reality of the stone beneath his back pressed against his spine.

Torin was still there. The large man sat with his back against the doorframe, his arms crossed over his chest, his eyes half-closed. He looked less like a warrior and more like a statue that had forgotten how to stop sitting. Maren was still beside Kaelen, her hand hovering near the boy’s wrist, her expression unreadable in the dim light.

Elara was gone.

Elias sat up, the motion stiff and painful. His joints popped in a sequence that sounded like dry twigs snapping. He rubbed his face, the rough texture of his beard scraping against his palms. The air in the gatehouse smelled of old wood, dried herbs, and the faint, metallic tang of rust. It was a clean smell. A safe smell.

He looked at the quartz stone in his hand. It was cold, inert, a mere piece of rock. The chisel lay on the floor nearby, its tip dull and unremarkable. In the last few hours, these objects had been the difference between survival and annihilation. Now, they were just tools.

Torin opened one eye. He didn’t speak. He simply looked at Elias, then at Kaelen, then back at Elias. A silent question. *Are we alive?*

Elias nodded.

Torin let out a long, slow breath through his nose. He uncrossed his arms, the movement deliberate and heavy. “The perimeter is clear,” he said, his voice rough. “Elara went to the kitchen. She said the fire was still going, which is a miracle, and she’s making something that smells like burnt bread and hope.”

Elias let out a breath he didn’t realize he’d been holding. Burnt bread. The thought was absurdly mundane. It anchored him.

Maren lowered her hand from Kaelen’s wrist. “His pulse is steady,” she said, her voice quiet. “The tremors have stopped. For now.”

“For now,” Torin repeated. He stood up, stretching his back with a series of audible cracks. He towered over them, a silhouette of muscle and scar tissue in the dim light. “We need food. We need water. And we need a place that isn’t this drafty hole in the hill where the wind sounds like a dying goat.”

Elias nodded, swinging his legs over the side of the straw pile. His legs felt weak, unsteady. He gripped the edge of the doorframe to steady himself. “The map,” he said, his voice hoarse. “The western edge. There was a way station. A refuge.”

Torin looked at him, then at the door. “Then we walk.”

They stepped out into the corridor. The watchfort was silent, the thick walls muting the sounds of the world outside. The hallway smelled of damp stone and old grease. Footsteps echoed as they walked, the sound of their boots on the flagstones sharp and rhythmic.

They found Elara in the small kitchen, a square room with a stone hearth and a single window looking out onto the forest. The fire was low, embers glowing orange in the ash. Elara stood before a wooden table, her back to them, her shoulders hunched.

She turned, and Elias saw that her hands were shaking. Not the violent tremors of Kaelen’s magic, but the subtle, persistent vibration of exhaustion. She held a loaf of bread, torn in half, the crust dark and cracked.

“I found it,” she said, not looking at them. “In the pantry. It’s old. It smells like it’s been there for a year.”

Torin took the bread, breaking off a piece. He chewed slowly, his jaw working. “It’s dry,” he said.

“It’s bread,” Elara said.

“It’s very dry.”

Elias took a piece from her hand. It was hard, dense, and tasted of dust and time. But as he swallowed, the warmth of it spreading through his stomach, he felt a small, fragile solidity return to his limbs.

They ate in silence. The bread was tough, the water from the clay jug stale, but it was sustenance. It was the fuel for the next step. When they finished, Torin wiped his hands on his leather bracer, leaving a faint smear of dust on the hide.

“We should leave before dusk,” he said.

Elias looked at the window. The light outside had shifted, the gray of the overcast sky deepening into a bruised purple. The forest beyond the wall was a wall of shadow, the gnarled trees standing like sentinels.

“The path is clear,” Maren said. She had been sitting on a stool, her arms wrapped around her knees. She looked up, her eyes dark and serious. “But the forest… it’s quiet. Too quiet.”

Elias knew what she meant. The forest had a presence, a memory. It had watched them, remembered them, and let them pass. But the Blight had been trying to reach it. Had the balance been disturbed?

“We go quickly,” Elias said. “We stay together. And we don’t stop.”

They gathered their gear. The quartz stone went into Elias’s pocket. The chisel went into his belt. Kaelen’s gauntlet was secured, the runes dim and dormant. Maren checked her vial of salve, the amber liquid swirling in the glass.

They stepped out of the gatehouse. The air was cool, carrying the scent of pine and damp earth. The path down the slope was visible, a ribbon of trodden dirt leading away from the watchfort and into the tree line.

Torin walked in front, his hand resting on the hilt of his sword. Maren walked beside Kaelen, her hand on his shoulder, a subtle, grounding touch. Elara walked behind, her eyes scanning the edges of the path. Elias walked last, his mind turning over the map, the channels, the resonance.

They began the descent. The world outside the watchfort was vast and indifferent. The trees loomed above them, their branches interlacing to form a canopy that blocked out the sky. The path was narrow, the ground uneven.

After an hour of walking, the trees began to thin. The dense forest gave way to scrubby undergrowth, and then to open, rolling hills. The wind picked up, blowing across the grass, creating a sound like distant waves.

And then they saw it.

A structure, small and squat, sitting on a rise in the land. It was a tavern, or what was left of one. The roof was sagging, the walls patched with stone and timber. A sign hung from a post, the wood warped and faded. It read: *The Broken Crown*.

Elias stopped. He looked at the sign, then at the others.

“The refuge,” he said.

Torin frowned. “It looks abandoned.”

“It’s a tavern,” Maren said. “They’re always half-abandoned.”

Elara laughed, a short, sharp sound. “That’s the most terrifying thing I’ve heard all day.”

Elias looked at the tavern. The windows were dark, the door closed. But there was a light in the kitchen window, a flicker of orange fire. And there was a smell, drifting on the wind, that was distinct and undeniable.

Baking. Fresh, warm bread.

Torin’s stomach growled. He looked at Elias.

“Is this a trap?” Torin asked.

Elias didn’t answer. He just looked at the light in the window.

“No,” he said. “It’s just a tavern.”

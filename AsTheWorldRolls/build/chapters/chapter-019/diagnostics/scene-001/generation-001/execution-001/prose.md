The walk from the hydraulic throat to the watchfort’s outer perimeter took four hours. It was a long, silent procession that Elias would later recount with a strange, detached clarity. They moved not as a tactical unit, but as a single, weary organism. Torin kept one hand firmly on the small of Kaelen’s back, not pushing, but preventing him from sagging into the earth. Kaelen’s head lolled against Torin’s shoulder, his breathing a shallow, ragged rhythm that seemed to cost him effort. Every few minutes, his fingers would twitch against Torin’s tunic, a spasm that faded as quickly as it came.

Maren walked on the other side, her eyes fixed not on the path, but on Kaelen’s face. She checked his pulse at intervals, her thumb resting lightly over his wrist, then withdrawing before the damp air could chill the skin. Elias walked behind them, carrying the quartz stone in his pocket. It had gone completely cold, a heavy, inert weight against his thigh. He could feel its mass, but none of the hum, none of the vibration. It felt like carrying a piece of gravel.

The forest had changed. Or perhaps their perception of it had. The gnarled trees still formed a natural barrier, their roots twisting into the soil like the fingers of buried giants, but the oppressive weight of the Blight was gone. The air no longer tasted of copper and rot. It smelled of wet loam, pine sap, and the distant, clean scent of rain on stone. The mist that had swallowed the world for three weeks had lifted, leaving behind a pale, overcast sky that offered no sun but gave back the world’s shape.

They emerged from the tree line onto the gentle slope leading to the watchfort. The structure sat at the bottom of the hill, its thick, dark walls pitted by wind and rain, the slit-like windows staring out like empty eye sockets. A single lantern burned in the main gate, its flame steady and yellow in the gray light.

Elias stopped. He didn’t want to move. His legs felt like they belonged to someone else, stiff and unresponsive. He looked up at the watchfort, then back at the forest. For the first time in weeks, the forest did not look like a throat waiting to swallow him. It just looked like trees.

"Torin," Elias said. His voice sounded thin in the quiet air.

Torin didn’t turn. He didn’t need to. "I know," he said.

They reached the gate. The heavy oak doors were already open, a gap wide enough for a man to slip through. Inside, the gatehouse smelled of old wood, drying herbs, and rusted iron. It was a smell Elias had memorized in their first days of refuge, a smell that had become synonymous with safety. Now, it smelled like home.

Maren guided Kaelen through the gap first. Torin followed, his broad shoulders brushing the doorframe. Elias stepped in last, and the heavy doors groaned shut behind him, cutting off the sight of the open hill and the distant, black spires of the village that still pierced the sky. The sound of the world outside—wind, birds, the creak of branches—was muffled, distant.

Inside, the air was still. It was cooler than the outside, carrying the faint, metallic tang of the iron fittings in the walls. Elias leaned against the stone wall, sliding down until he was sitting on the floor. He pulled his knees to his chest, wrapping his arms around them. He was shaking. He hadn’t realized he was shaking until he felt the tremor travel from his fingers up through his elbows and into his shoulders.

Torin knelt beside him. He didn’t offer a hand to pull Elias up. He simply sat down on the floor, his back against the wall, and looked at Elias with an expression that was stripped of all its usual sternness. It was just a look. A long, steady look that asked nothing.

Elias met his gaze. He blinked, his eyes burning. "We did it," he said. The words felt small. Inadequate.

"We did it," Torin agreed.

Kaelen was lying on a pile of straw in the corner, Maren sitting beside him, her hand resting on his forehead. Kaelen’s eyes were open, but they were unfocused, staring at the wooden beams above. He looked so young. The lines of exhaustion around his eyes, the pallor of his skin, the way his body collapsed in on itself—it was a physical manifestation of the hollowing Elias had witnessed. The magic had taken something out of him. Whether it would come back, Elias did not know.

Maren looked up. Her face was tight, the muscles in her jaw clenched. She had that look, the one Elias had seen in the city, when she was holding back a wave of emotion that she refused to let break the surface. She gave a slight nod to Elias, a silent acknowledgment that Kaelen was stable. For now.

Elara was nowhere to be seen. Elias assumed she was checking the perimeter, or perhaps she had gone to the kitchen to see if there was anything left to eat. The silence in the gatehouse was heavy, but it was a different kind of silence than the one in the forest. The forest silence had been predatory, watchful. This silence was tired. It was the silence of a held breath finally being released.

Elias looked down at his hands. They were clean. There was no Blight sludge on his fingers, no green-black residue under his nails. He flexed his fingers, watching them move. They were just hands. They had held a chisel, a quartz stone, and a map. They had not held a weapon. They had not killed anything.

He thought about the stranger. The one they had encountered in the city, the one whose identity remained a shadow in the corners of their minds. The stranger had known about the barrier. The stranger had known about the channels. The stranger had watched them with eyes that were not green, but a pale, washed-out gray. Elias did not know what the stranger wanted, or if the stranger was even still alive. The thought was a cold stone in his stomach, something he chose not to chew on. It was not the time.

"Torin," Elias said again.

"Hmm."

"Do you think it’s over?"

Torin was quiet for a long time. He looked at Kaelen, then at the door, then at his own hands. "The Blight in this place is gone," Torin said. "The source is drained. The barrier is fed. As far as this stretch of the world goes, yes."

"But the spires are still there," Elias said. He pointed toward the window, where the black spires were visible against the gray sky. "The entity. The thing in the temple. It’s still out there. We didn’t kill it. We just... starved it. For now."

Torin nodded slowly. "We bought time," he said. "That’s what we do. We buy time."

Elias looked at Kaelen. The young man’s chest rose and fell, slow and steady. He was sleeping, or pretending to. His face was peaceful, the tension that had gripped his features for days finally easing. Elias felt a sudden, sharp pang of protectiveness. Not just for Kaelen, but for the group. For Maren, who had held them together with her salve and her steady hands. For Elara, who had held the chisel with a steady hand when her own were trembling. For Torin, who had been the wall when the world tried to break them.

They were not heroes. They were not the kind of heroes who marched into the light and stayed there. They were survivors. They were the kind of people who found a way to keep breathing when the air was thick with rot. And now, they were tired.

Elias closed his eyes. The smell of the gatehouse wrapped around him, the scent of iron and herbs and old wood. It was a solid, real smell. It was the smell of a place that existed, that had walls, that had a door. It was the smell of a pause.

He did not know what came next. He did not know where they would go, or what they would find. He did not know if the stranger was watching, or if the Blight was merely sleeping, waiting for the next crack in the world to slip through. He did not know if Kaelen would wake up whole, or if the magic had taken a piece of him that would never return.

But for now, in the quiet dark of the gatehouse, with the sound of Torin’s breathing beside him and the steady rhythm of Kaelen’s pulse under Maren’s hand, Elias Thorne let himself rest. He let the weight of the world sit on his shoulders, just for a moment, before he had to lift it again.

The lantern in the gate flickered, casting long, dancing shadows across the stone walls. Outside, the wind picked up, rustling the leaves of the ancient trees. The forest breathed. The world turned.

And for the first time in weeks, Elias Thorne slept without dreaming of the fog.

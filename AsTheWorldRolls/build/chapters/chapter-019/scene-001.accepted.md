Kaelen opened his eyes to the sensation of cold radiating from his own limbs, a sharp contrast to the heavy warmth pooled in his chest where Maren’s hands still gripped him. The darkness around his vision was not empty; it was textured with the soft glow of the hearth and the faint, bruised violet pulse that bled through the stone walls from the barrier. He tried to sit up, but a wave of dizziness rolled over him, pulling him back against the pillow. His skin felt tight, almost like parchment stretched over bone, though the dark veins were receding, fading into the pale flesh as if ashamed of their presence.

“Easy,” Maren said, her voice low and rough with sleep that had been dragged out by worry. She didn’t let go of his hand. Her fingers were cold now, lacking the magical heat she had poured into him earlier, but they were warm to the touch because he was still drawing some residual energy from them. “You’ve been out for a while.”

Kaelen swallowed, and the action felt like swallowing dust. He blinked, trying to focus on the faces surrounding his cot. Torin stood in the doorway, broad silhouette framed by the dim light of the lantern there. His hand rested near his sword hilt, not in threat but in habit, a soldier’s posture even when guarding against the fog outside rather than an enemy at the gate. Elias was by the hearth, watching the flames dance in the iron pot that held the heat extraction system. The blue flame under the grate was steady now, feeding the embers without burning hot enough to consume the room’s air. Elara sat on the far side of the bed, her book closed beside her, her eyes fixed on Kaelen’s face with a mixture of scholarly curiosity and deep concern.

“I heard you breathing,” Maren said, tightening her grip just enough to remind him she was there. “You were quiet. Too quiet.”

Kaelen managed a nod, the movement slow and deliberate. The effort drained him further, but he felt a strange sense of relief wash over him, not because he was tired of being unconscious—being unconscious had been a kind of mercy—but because he was back in the loop of perception. He could feel the tension in the room, the way Torin’s muscles were coiled like steel cables, the low murmur of Elias checking the grate, the quiet vigilance of Elara. The barrier hummed faintly, a sound that had been discordant earlier but now felt more like a heartbeat syncing with his own. It was still there, pulsing with that bruised violet light, but it wasn’t threatening to shatter anymore. The pressure had stabilized, at least for now.

“How long?” Kaelen asked, his voice cracking on the word.

“Since the last time you slept,” Elara said softly, not looking up from her journal. “We’ve been waiting.”

Kaelen tried to push himself upright again, and this time Maren helped him sit fully against the pillows. His chest rose and fell with shallow breaths, each one a conscious effort. The cold in his bones had receded somewhat, replaced by a lingering warmth that felt less like magic and more like survival. He looked at Maren’s face, still pale but no longer trembling. She had taken off her cloak and sat there in just the wool shirt beneath it, her arms crossed tightly against the chill. Her eyes were red-rimmed, not from crying—Maren didn’t cry—but from exhaustion and the strain of holding him together with nothing but will and a fading reservoir of heat.

“You’re awake,” Torin said, stepping forward into the room. He didn’t smile, but his shoulders dropped a fraction. “That’s good.”

“Good enough,” Kaelen replied. He looked at Elias, who was still tending the fire. The heat extraction system worked quietly, pulling warmth from the embers and channeling it into the stone floor beneath Maren’s feet. It was a clever trick, one that saved her magic while keeping them alive. “How bad is it outside?”

Elias glanced up from the grate, wiping soot from his forehead with the back of his hand. “The barrier holds,” he said. “It’s stable enough for now. But we can’t stay here forever.”

Kaelen nodded slowly. He could feel the weight of that truth in the air, even without seeing the fog pressing against the walls. The Blight was still moving west, pulling at the edges of their world, and they were sitting on a knife’s edge of time and magic.

“I can help,” Kaelen said, his voice gaining a little strength. “I can pull some of it through me.”

Maren shook her head immediately. “Not yet. You just woke up. Your body isn’t ready to take that kind of load.”

“Then what do we do?” Torin asked, his hand tightening on his sword hilt again. “The seal’s still groaning. We need to know if it’ll hold until dawn.”

“We wait,” Elias said, his tone firm but not unkind. “Maren’s magic is drained. Kaelen can’t take another surge until he’s stable. Torin, keep watch. Elara, check the resonance readings again. If the frequency shifts, we act then.”

Kaelen closed his eyes for a moment, listening to the quiet rhythm of the room. The barrier hummed in the background, a low thrum that had become less frantic, more like a slow tide breathing against the shore. He could feel the pull of the Blight still there, faint but present, like a distant song he couldn’t quite forget. It wasn’t gone; it was just waiting, patient and hungry, for a chance to push through again.

He looked at Maren’s face again. She hadn’t moved since he opened his eyes. Her hand was still on his chest, pressing down lightly, as if anchoring him to the bed. She didn’t say anything, but the warmth in his skin told him she was still there, still holding him together with nothing but her will and a fading supply of heat. He knew what that meant. It meant she had given everything she had left, and now she was just keeping him alive by force of habit and love.

“Thank you,” he whispered to her.

She didn’t answer, but her grip tightened just enough to show she heard him.

Kaelen took a deep breath, feeling the air fill his lungs with the stale scent of old wood, herbs, and rusted iron. The room was quiet now, the kind of quiet that comes after a storm has passed but before the sun rises. Outside, the fog pressed against the walls, invisible and waiting. Inside, they were still, watching the clock tick down toward dawn.

He could feel the barrier’s pulse again, steady now, almost rhythmic. It wasn’t perfect—the violet light still flickered at the edges—but it was holding. And for now, that was enough.

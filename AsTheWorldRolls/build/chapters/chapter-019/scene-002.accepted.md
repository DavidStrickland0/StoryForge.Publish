The silence in the gatehouse was a heavy thing. It pressed against Elias’s eardrums, thicker than the fog had ever been, but it did not crawl. It did not whisper. It simply existed, a blank slate of quiet that he was still learning to recognize as peace.

Torin shifted beside him, the leather of his bracer creaking in a dry, rhythmic sound. He didn’t wake. He was too tired for sleep, too wired for rest, caught in that limbo where the body demands stillness but the mind refuses to let go of the watch. His hand rested on the pommel of his sword, not as a threat, but as an anchor.

Elias opened his eyes. The lantern’s flame had burned low, the wick hissing softly. He looked at Maren. She was still there, her hand hovering just above Kaelen’s wrist, her face a mask of professional vigilance. Her eyes, when they met Elias’s, held a question he didn’t want to answer yet.

*We’re alive,* the look said. *But what now?*

Elias sat up. The motion was slow, deliberate. Every muscle in his back protested, a deep, grinding ache that spoke of hours spent hunched over the quartz stone, channeling resonance until his bones felt like they were made of glass. He rubbed his face, his fingers coming away with the dust of the watchfort’s old wood.

"We can’t stay here," he said, his voice rough.

Torin’s eyes fluttered open. "The barrier is holding."

"The barrier is holding," Elias agreed. "But the entity in the spires isn’t. We starved it, Torin. We didn’t kill it. And right now, it’s hungry."

Maren lowered her hand from Kaelen’s pulse. The boy’s breathing was even, shallow, but steady. For now, the magic had stopped pulling at him. "He’s stable," she said, though the word sounded like a concession rather than a victory. "But his body is running on fumes. If he tries to do it again, he might not come back."

"Then he doesn’t do it again," Torin said. The words were blunt, lacking their usual careful weight. He stood up, stretching his stiff limbs. The sound of his joints popping was startlingly loud in the small room. "We need supplies. We need food. And we need to know what’s out there."

Elias nodded. "The map showed a way station near the western edge of the city. A place of refuge. If the Blight has moved on, people might have started moving back. Or at least, trying to."

"Or they’re dead," Maren said quietly.

"Or they’re alive and scared," Elias corrected. "There’s a difference."

They packed their gear in silence. The chisel, the quartz stone, the bronze map—each item felt heavier now that the immediate crisis had passed. They were tools of war, and war was a thing that happened in the past tense. For now.

They left the gatehouse, stepping out into the twilight. The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of pine and damp earth. The forest stood silent around them, the gnarled trees stretching their branches into the darkening sky. There was no green light in the windows of the ruined houses in the distance. No jagged shapes moving in the fog. Just the dark, and the stars beginning to prick through the haze.

The path to the way station was not far, perhaps a half-day’s walk if the roads were clear. But the roads were not always clear.

They walked for an hour before they saw the lights.

They were small, flickering things at first, easily mistaken for stray sparks or the reflection of the moon on wet leaves. But as they crested the gentle slope beyond the forest’s edge, the lights resolved into a cluster of warm, amber glows. A tavern. Or what passed for one in these frontier lands. It was a sprawling structure of timber and stone, with a thatched roof that looked perpetually damp and a sign hanging from a heavy iron chain. The sign was blank, save for a carved symbol of a crossed pick and axe.

The **Pick and Axe**.

Elias had seen the name mentioned in the city before the fall. A way station for traders, miners, and adventurers. A place where the frontier’s rough edges were smoothed out with ale and gossip.

As they approached, the door swung open.

The man who stepped out was not what Elias had expected. He was not a burly tavern keeper with a scarred face and a sword at his hip. He was a small, neat man, wearing a clean white apron over a blue shirt. His hair was combed back severely, and he held a ledger in one hand and a quill in the other.

"Welcome to the Pick and Axe," he said, his voice crisp and professional. "I am Aldric. Are you here to register for the escort contract?"

Torin blinked. "The what?"

Aldric gestured to the ledger. "The caravan from the capital arrives at dawn. Three wagons, two guards, and a merchant who is very particular about his tea. I have a vacancy for a security detail. Five members, I believe. Are you the group?"

Maren stepped forward, her hands clasped tightly in front of her. "We are. But we’re not here for a contract. We’re here to rest."

Aldric’s expression did not change, but his eyes narrowed slightly. He looked at Elias, then at Torin, then at Kaelen, who was leaning heavily on Torin’s shoulder, pale and trembling.

"I’m afraid I must insist on the paperwork," Aldric said. "It’s not just a formality. It’s a liability waiver. A blood-debt registry. And a health inspection."

"A health inspection?" Torin asked, his voice rising an octave. "We just walked through a forest full of Blight-Touched. We don’t have time for a health inspection."

"Regulation is regulation," Aldric said, tapping the quill against the ledger. "Section 4, Paragraph 2: *No party may enter the common room or the stables without presenting a clean bill of health from a certified physician.*"

Maren’s eyes widened. She looked at Elias, then at Torin, and finally back at Aldric. "I am a certified physician."

"You are a healer," Aldric corrected, his tone polite but firm. "I have here a list of the ten most respected healers in the province. Your name is not on it. I require a stamped seal from the Guild of Alchemists, or a notarized letter from the local magistrate."

"We don’t have a notarized letter," Elias said, trying to keep the edge out of his voice. "We have a bronze map that shows the location of a temple that contains a god. We have a man who is currently dissociating from reality because he used magic to stop a plague. Do you think we have time to find a magistrate?"

Aldric looked at Kaelen. He looked at the blood on Torin’s gauntlet. He looked at the dirt on their boots, the dried mud on their armor. And then, with a sigh that seemed to come from the very core of his being, he closed the ledger.

"Very well," he said. "You may enter. But you will sign the waiver. And you will pay for the room. And you will clean up before you sit at the table."

Torin let out a breath he seemed to have been holding for hours. "Deal."

They stepped inside.

The common room of the Pick and Axe was a sight to behold. It was not the dark, grimy hole Elias had imagined. The wood was polished, the tables were scrubbed, and the air smelled of bread, beer, and rosemary. There were a dozen other patrons, sitting in small groups, their faces illuminated by the warm light of the hearth. They looked up as the five of them entered, their eyes wide with recognition.

*Adventurers.*

Elias felt a surge of something he hadn’t felt in weeks. Not fear. Not anger. Just... presence. They were here. They were alive. And they were in a room where people were drinking tea and discussing the price of grain.

Aldric led them to a table in the corner, near the hearth. He set a pitcher of water and five glasses on the table. "The food will be ready in ten minutes," he said. "You may wash your hands. And Kaelen, I suggest you sit down. You look like you’re about to faint."

Kaelen didn’t answer. He was staring at the wall, his eyes unfocused. But he sat. He sat, and he breathed, and for the first time in a long time, he didn’t feel like he was drowning.

Elias looked at the water. He looked at the clean table. He looked at Torin, who was rubbing his shoulders with a grunt of relief. He looked at Maren, who was carefully wiping a spot of mud from her sleeve.

He picked up a glass. He drank.

The water was cold, and it tasted like stone. It tasted like home.

And for the first time in weeks, Elias Thorne felt like he was going to survive.

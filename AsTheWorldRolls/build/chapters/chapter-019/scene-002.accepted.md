Kaelen felt the heat radiating from Elias’s hands before he saw it. The soldier was crouched over the iron pot, his fingers buried deep in the soot and ash near the grate, but he wasn’t tending a fire for warmth. He was siphoning thermal energy from the stone foundations into the metal vessel, drawing heat from the earth itself to feed Maren. It was a crude mechanical trick, efficient but exhausting on the user’s constitution. Elias hadn’t spoken since they’d settled Kaelen in the bed, his face buried in the work, shoulders tense with the effort of pulling warmth out of cold stone.

Maren shifted, her hands pressing against the rough wool of the blanket covering Kaelen’s legs. Her fingers were ice. The warmth she had been pouring into him hours ago was gone, drained by the sheer act of holding his magic together while it bled through his skin. Now that he was awake and breathing, the demand on her had dropped, but the cost remained. She was cold, shivering slightly despite the effort to suppress it. Elias’s trick kept the room warm enough not to freeze them solid, but it didn’t replace the biological heat Maren needed to sustain him.

Kaelen looked at Elias. The man’s leather armor was dark with grime, and his bandaged side was pulled tight against a wound that had reopened several times in the last day. He smelled of wet woodsmoke and old iron. Elias caught Kaelen’s gaze and offered a grim nod. He didn’t look up from the pot, but he shifted his weight, adjusting the flow of heat to match Maren’s fading reserve. It was a silent agreement, a practical exchange of labor that spoke louder than words in this place.

Maren cleared her throat, the sound raspy and unused. “He needs more,” she said, gesturing with her chin toward Kaelen. Her voice lacked its usual steel; it was thin, stripped bare by exhaustion. “The seal is holding, Elias, but the pressure outside is still building. If we wait until dawn without reinforcing the flow, the resonance will spike again. We need to push back against that tide.”

Elias stopped his fiddling with the grate and finally looked at her. His eyes were red-rimmed, not from sleeplessness but from the strain of maintaining a heat extraction system while keeping a wound clean. “It takes time to recalibrate the frequency,” he replied. He didn’t argue about moving or waiting. The decision had been made in the silence of the night: they would stay, they would reinforce the barrier, and then they would move. The only question was how long they could hold the line before the mechanics broke them.

Kaelen reached out with his left hand. His fingers brushed the brass gauntlet on his wrist. It was warm now, no longer cold and inert as it had been when he first woke. The runes etched into the metal were dull, dormant, but they hummed faintly against his pulse—a low vibration that felt less like a siren song and more like a heartbeat syncing with his own. He could feel the Blight’s pull in the air, a distant pressure against the barrier’s skin, but he could also feel Maren’s heat fading, Elias’s strain mounting. It was an imbalance that demanded correction.

He closed his eyes for a moment, letting the hum of the gauntlet guide him. The connection wasn’t just magical; it was physical, a thread of energy running from his heart to the barrier and back again. He could sense the seal at the Gate of Silence groaning under the load of the westward tide. It wasn’t a loud noise, more like a deep thrumming in the bones of the world, a reminder that the system was under stress.

“Maren,” Kaelen said, his voice rough from disuse. He didn’t sit up fully; he just shifted his weight to face her properly. “I can channel.”

She stared at him, her hands still pressed to his legs. There was a flicker of surprise in her eyes, then it hardened into something that looked like resolve mixed with fear. “You know what you’ll do if you push,” she said. “You’ll crack open your own veins.”

“I’ve already done that,” he replied. He gestured to the dark lines under his skin, now receding but still visible. “The fissures are sealed enough to hold a little more pressure. If I can stabilize the flow through the gauntlet, we don’t need to wait for dawn. We can push back against the surge.”

Elias stood up from the grate and walked over to the bed. He placed his hand on Kaelen’s shoulder, not in comfort but in assessment. “You’re talking about pulling the tide back into the barrier,” he said. “That means you’ll be draining your reserves faster than Maren can replenish hers. You’ll burn out before sunrise.”

“I’m not burning,” Kaelen said. He looked at Elias, then at Maren. “I’m not trying to save myself. I’m trying to make sure we don’t leave here with a broken seal behind us.”

Maren nodded slowly, her grip tightening on his blanket. She didn’t argue. She knew that if the seal broke again, the barrier would shatter, and they’d be out in the open once more, hunted by whatever was moving through the western channels. The Blight wasn’t just a storm; it was an active force seeking a breach, and the only way to stop it was to feed the trap properly.

“Then do it,” Maren said, her voice flat but not unkind. “But if you start fading, Torin is hitting you.”

Torin stepped into the room from the doorway, his sword in hand though he made no move to draw it. He stood near the entrance, his broad frame blocking the view of the corridor beyond. He didn’t speak, but his presence was enough. He had already said that if Kaelen lost control, Torin would strike him to break his focus. It wasn’t a threat; it was a duty.

Kaelen took a deep breath and closed his eyes again. The gauntlet warmed under his skin, the runes flaring with a soft light that felt almost human, like a heartbeat. He reached out with his mind, not his muscles, and touched the barrier’s surface. It was thick now, pulsing with the violet hue of consumed corruption, but it needed more fuel.

He pushed the energy through the gauntlet, pulling from the Blight’s flow and directing it into the barrier’s stomach. The sensation was like swallowing ice water—cold, sharp, and filling him up until his chest felt tight. He could feel Maren’s coldness seeping back into her hands, Elias’s heat extraction system working harder than before. The air in the room grew heavier, charged with static and the faint ozone smell of magic being drawn from the earth.

The barrier responded instantly. The violet light intensified, spreading outward from the edges of the dome until it filled the entire chamber. The groaning stopped, replaced by a steady hum that vibrated through the floorboards and into their bones. It was a good sign—the trap was digesting faster now, but the cost was high.

Kaelen’s skin grew translucent again, the dark veins mapping his arms becoming more prominent as he drew on the Blight’s energy. He didn’t flinch; he just held the line, keeping the flow steady while Maren’s warmth faded and Elias’s heat extraction struggled to keep up.

“Hold him,” Torin said quietly, stepping forward with his hand resting on Kaelen’s shoulder. “If you start slipping, I’m not waiting for the next strike.”

Kaelen nodded, though he didn’t need to speak. He focused on Maren’s cold hands, using a fraction of his energy to warm them without draining too much of his own reserve. It was a delicate balance—giving enough heat to keep her from freezing but not so much that he collapsed under the strain.

Elias adjusted the grate, increasing the flow of heat from the earth into the pot. The iron vessel began to glow with a faint orange light, radiating warmth toward Maren’s body. It wasn’t magic; it was physics, simple and reliable. Elias knew how to use the tools he had, even if they weren’t perfect.

Maren leaned back against the bedpost, her breathing slowing as the heat returned to her limbs. She looked at Kaelen with a mixture of relief and worry. “You’re doing too much,” she said. “We don’t need you to carry the whole load.”

“I know,” he replied. “But if we wait until dawn, the seal will break.”

“Then we’ll move before dawn,” Elias said. He wiped soot from his forehead with the back of his hand and looked at Kaelen. “You’ve got enough energy to push us out of here. We don’t have time to argue about the mechanics. Just keep the flow steady.”

Kaelen nodded again, feeling the weight of their trust on his shoulders. He didn’t feel heroic; he felt like a conduit, a bridge between two forces that weren’t meant to mix. But he was doing what needed to be done, and for now, it was enough.

The room settled into a new rhythm, the barrier humming softly as it consumed the Blight’s tide. Outside, the fog pressed against the walls, but the barrier held firm. Inside, they were safe, united by the shared burden of survival. And Kaelen, with his gauntlet warm and his magic flowing, was ready to keep them there until the sun rose.

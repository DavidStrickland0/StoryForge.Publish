Elara stepped away from the chest of tools and moved toward Kaelen’s bed. She did not approach him with the reverence usually reserved for saints or relics, but with the brisk, utilitarian motion of someone adjusting a measurement before recording it. Her boots clicked softly on the stone floor, a sound that seemed deafening in the silence where the barrier’s discordant hum used to be.

She knelt beside the bed, ignoring the cold radiating from his skin. The air around him tasted metallic, like licking a battery terminal after a lightning strike. His breathing was shallow, rhythmic, but barely there. Elara reached out and placed her hand over his left wrist, where the brass gauntlet rested. It was still, heavy, and cold enough to burn her fingers if she held it too long.

“Torin,” she called softly, not looking up from Kaelen’s chest. “Check the gauge on the hearth. If the heat drops below ambient, we lose the gradient he needs.”

Torin shifted behind Maren but remained silent, his broad shoulders blocking the doorway to the main hall. He knew better than to interfere with Elara’s work; even when his instincts screamed about the fragility of the situation, he respected the scholar’s domain. That was a discipline she had earned through years of studying the old ways while others burned out trying to fight them with brute force.

Elara closed her eyes and listened to the silence between Kaelen’s breaths. She could feel the faint vibration of his magic in her fingertips, not as a pulse or a hum, but as a dull pressure, like water pressing against an ear drum underwater. It was weak, barely holding shape. If she pushed too hard, he would collapse completely; if she pulled back too soon, the Blight’s tide would reclaim him entirely.

She withdrew her hand and opened her leather-bound journal. The pages were filled with charcoal sketches of binding runes and diagrams showing how water flow mechanics interacted with crystalline structures. She traced a line with her finger, marking a frequency that matched the resonance she felt in the air.

“Elias,” she called again, louder this time. He was standing near the entrance, watching the barrier’s edge where the violet light pulsed against the black spires outside. “The seal is holding, but the gradient is wrong. We need to stabilize Kaelen before we move.”

Elias turned from the entrance, his boots scuffing against the stone. He looked tired, the lines around his eyes deeper than they had been earlier that night. He nodded once, a short, sharp movement that acknowledged her assessment without needing further explanation.

“Torin,” Elias said, his voice low and gravelly. “Keep Maren warm. If the temperature drops another degree, she’ll lose consciousness before dawn.”

Torin stepped forward and placed a hand on Maren’s shoulder again, squeezing gently. She didn’t stir; her head rested against Kaelen’s pillow, her breathing slow and even. Elias walked over to the table near the hearth and spread out his bronze plate map, the lines etched into the metal catching the dim light of the single lantern hanging from the beam above.

Elara returned to Kaelen’s side and began to work again. This time, she didn’t just listen; she applied a small resonance frequency to his gauntlet. She tapped the brass with her fingernail, creating a faint vibration that matched the natural rhythm of his magic. It was a delicate operation, like tuning a broken instrument without breaking it further.

The gauntlet responded instantly. The dull pressure in her fingertips shifted from a desperate struggle to a steady, controlled flow. Kaelen’s skin stopped turning translucent; the dark veins beneath faded slightly, retreating into his flesh as if ashamed of their presence. His breathing deepened, becoming more even, though still shallow.

“Good,” Elara muttered, closing her journal and setting it aside. “He’s stable for now.”

She stood up, rubbing her temples. The effort had drained her significantly; her knees felt weak, and her vision blurred at the edges. She was a scholar, not a warrior, and her strength lay in knowledge, not endurance. But knowledge could be applied only when the body remained functional.

“Elias,” she said again, turning to face him. “We can’t afford to waste time on Kaelen’s recovery if we don’t stabilize him first. But we also can’t stay here forever; the barrier is eating faster than it can digest.”

Elias nodded, his eyes fixed on the map. “The route through the drainage system is clear enough for a single person, but we’ll need to move fast once we reach the ridge. The slurry is unstable; one wrong step and we’re ankle-deep in black water within seconds.”

Elara stepped back and let him take over the conversation. She moved to the chest of tools and gathered her supplies: chisels, quartz stones, solvent vials, and a piece of clear quartz wrapped in cloth. She needed these for the operation at the Gate of Silence, but she also needed to ensure they had enough time to execute it before the seal failed entirely.

She paused at the door, looking out into the main hall. The barrier’s violet light pulsed against the walls, casting long shadows that seemed to move on their own. Outside, the wind howled louder, a sound that grew more frantic with every passing moment. Inside, the group remained still, united by a shared purpose and a fragile hope that they might survive another night together.

She turned back to Kaelen’s bed and placed her hand on his shoulder again. He didn’t wake; he barely stirred at all. But she could feel the faint warmth of his body returning, a slow, steady process that promised recovery without promising victory.

“Torin,” she said quietly. “If Elias and I need to move quickly, you’ll have to watch Maren. She won’t be able to channel magic for much longer.”

Torin nodded, his hand tightening on Maren’s shoulder just enough to keep her upright. He was a soldier, trained to take orders without question, but his eyes betrayed a flicker of concern. He knew what happened when someone burned out completely; he had seen it happen before, in skirmishes far from the watchfort.

The fog on the hills had thinned to a bruised purple haze, but the air inside the gatehouse remained stale with the scent of damp stone and old wood. Elias pushed the bronze map further across the rough-hewn table, the metal clicking softly against the grain. It was a heavy thing, etched with lines that seemed to shift in the dim light of the single lantern, though he had long since stopped believing they were actually moving.

"Look here," Elias said, tapping a point on the map where a thick line diverged from the main channel. "The schematic shows a direct path from the western gate to the shrine. A straight line. Flat ground."

Torin leaned over the table, his large hands resting on the wood. He was still polishing his sword, the rhythmic *shhk-shhk* of the whetstone providing a grounding sound in the quiet room. "And?"

"And the map is three centuries old," Maren said. She stood by the slit window, her back to them, watching the light change outside. "The ground isn't flat, Torin. We saw it from the ridge. The river has cut a new bed. The old road is a swamp."

Elias frowned. He traced the line on the bronze plate with his finger. The metal was cold, smooth. "If the river has shifted, the channel entrance at the Gate of Silence should be exposed. Or buried. We need to know which before we walk into the mud."

"We don't know," Torin said. He stopped polishing and set the sword on the table, the steel ringing faintly. "We know the fog is burning off. We know the entity is closer. We don't know what the terrain looks like two miles east."

"Then we look," Elias said.

He stood up, the pain in his side flaring as he stretched. He ignored it. He walked to the narrow window, the one that looked out over the slope leading down from the watchfort. The world outside was a watercolor of greys and dull greens. The forest edge was a dark wall, the trees gnarled and silent. Beyond that, the hills rolled away into the mist.

"Torin," Elias said. "The angle from here. If the shrine is west of the city, it’s behind the ridge we climbed yesterday. The fog is clearing on the north face."

Torin joined him at the window. He was tall, broad-shouldered, and his presence made the small gatehouse feel even more cramped. He squinted through the glass, his face a mask of concentration. "I see the tree line. I see the ridge. I don't see a road."

"There is no road," Maren said, joining them. Her voice was calm, but there was an edge to it. "There is a way. There’s a difference."

Elias looked at her. "Explain."

Maren pulled her cloak tighter around her shoulders. She was smaller than the others, but her eyes were sharp, missing nothing. "When we were in the city, before the fog took everything, I saw the drainage ditches. They run parallel to the main street. If the main street is gone, the ditches might still be there. They’re stone-lined. They won’t wash away as easily as dirt."

Torin rubbed his jaw. "Stone-lined ditches. In a city that’s been rotting for three weeks. That sounds like a trap."

"It’s a path," Elias corrected gently. "And it leads to the channel entrance. If the map is right, the channel connects to the shrine. If the ditches are right, they lead to the channel. We follow the ditches."

"And if the ditches are full of water?" Torin asked.

"Then we wade," Maren said. "Or we wait for it to drain. We have time until dawn."

Elias looked back at the map. The line for the channel was etched deep into the bronze. It didn’t show the surface. It didn’t show the mud, or the rot, or the things that lived in the fog. It only showed the bones of the city.

"We go to the ridge first," Elias said. "We find a high point. We look for the ditches. If we see them, we follow them. If we don’t, we go around the ridge and look for the shrine from the other side."

"And if we see the entity?" Torin asked.

Elias met his gaze. "Then we turn back. And we find another way."

Torin was silent for a moment. He looked at the map, then at Elias, then at Maren. He nodded slowly. "The ridge. We go to the ridge. We look. We decide."

It was a simple plan. It was a safe plan. It was the best they had.

Elias turned away from the window and walked back to the table. He picked up his pack, the leather creaking. He checked his belt, making sure his dagger was secure. He checked his water skin. It was half full. Enough.

"Kaelen is outside," Maren said softly.

Elias looked at the door. The wood was thick, old, and sturdy. Through the slats, he could hear the wind. A low, moaning sound. The wind was picking up.

"He needs to be inside," Elias said. "He’s cold. And he’s shaking."

"He’s ready," Torin said. He was already moving toward the door, his hand on the latch. "He’s been ready since we cleaned the gauntlet. He’s just... waiting."

Elias followed him. The air outside was biting, carrying the scent of pine and damp earth. The slope down from the watchfort was steep, the grass slick with dew. Kaelen was sitting on a flat rock near the base of the wall, his back against the stone. He was hunched over, his knees drawn up to his chest. The brass gauntlet was on his left wrist, the runes faintly glowing in the twilight.

He looked up as they approached. His face was pale, his eyes dark. But he was steady. He wasn’t trembling.

"Where do we go?" Kaelen asked. His voice was quiet, but clear.

Elias crouched down, bringing himself to Kaelen’s level. "To the ridge. We need to see the path."

Kaelen nodded. He stood up, his movements fluid and controlled. He didn’t stagger. He didn’t look at the gauntlet. He just looked at Elias.

"Is it safe?" Kaelen asked.

"No," Elias said. "But it’s manageable."

Kaelen smiled. It was a small, tired smile, but it was real. "Then let’s go."

Torin handed Kaelen a strip of cloth from his pack. Kaelen took it and began to wrap his hands. The ritual was familiar. The preparation was familiar. They were doing this. They were moving.

Elias looked at the ridge in the distance. It was a dark shape against the lighter sky. The fog was clinging to the valleys, but the peaks were clear. They could see it. They could reach it.

He signaled to Maren, who was gathering their supplies. He signaled to Torin, who was checking his sword one last time. He looked at Kaelen, who was tying the cloth around his wrists.

They were a team. They were a unit. They were ready.

Elias took a deep breath, the cold air filling his lungs. It was clean. It was sharp. It was alive.

"Let’s go," he said.

And they began to walk.

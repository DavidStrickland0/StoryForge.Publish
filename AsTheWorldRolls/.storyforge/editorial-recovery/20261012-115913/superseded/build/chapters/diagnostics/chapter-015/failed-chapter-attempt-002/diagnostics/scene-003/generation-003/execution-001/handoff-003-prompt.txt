The violet light held its shape, no longer flickering like a dying candle but burning with a steady, internal rhythm. Elias stood at the edge of the clearing, his boots sinking into the damp earth as he watched the barrier consume the stream of Blight. It was not a violent ingestion; it was a digestion, slow and methodical, as if the barrier were a great, silent engine processing fuel.

The hum in the air had changed. The chaotic shriek that had accompanied the initial surge had settled into a low, resonant thrum that Elias could feel in the soles of his boots and the marrow of his bones. It was less a sound and more a vibration of reality itself shifting to accommodate the new flow.

"Steady," Maren murmured from beside Elara. She kept her hands on the mage’s shoulders, not because she feared for Elara’s safety, but because Elara needed that anchor to maintain the chisel’s precise angle. A single degree of deviation in the strike could shatter the quartz or dissipate the resonance entirely. Maren was the fulcrum, the heavy weight that allowed Elara to remain delicate without tipping.

Kaelen stood with his eyes closed, his head tilted slightly back as if he were listening to a song sung from another world. The brass gauntlet on his left wrist had warmed significantly since the initial shock. The cold, inert metal of before had given way to a steady, pulsing heat that felt like holding a live ember in a leather glove. He did not pull away; he did not scream. Instead, he seemed to breathe with it, letting the Blight’s presence wash over him, filtered and regulated by his own will.

Torin remained the only one standing completely still, sword sheathed at his hip but hand resting on the hilt. He had been a sentinel against sudden external threats—the leaping shadows of the fog, the snapping jaws of unseen beasts. Now that those were absent, he found himself guarding against the internal collapse. He watched Kaelen’s face, looking for the first sign of strain, the moment when the boy would crack under the pressure. So far, there was none. Kaelen looked calm, almost peaceful, his breathing slow and deep as if he were sleeping while standing up.

"We hold this," Elias said, his voice cutting through the hum with a clarity that surprised even him. "Five minutes. Maybe six. Let the barrier stabilize at this new baseline. Then we see if it holds."

He did not look at the others for confirmation; they were already aligned on this, their minds locked in the same narrow channel of focus. There was no argument to be had here. The operation was a machine, and machines did not negotiate with their operators. They obeyed or they broke.

"The flow is consistent," Elara whispered, her eyes scanning the fissure in the shrine’s base where the dark fluid poured upward. She held the chisel steady against the quartz stone, watching the angle between them. "The frequency isn’t wavering. The barrier is eating faster than before, but not breaking."

"And the pressure?" Elias asked, turning his gaze toward Kaelen without breaking his own visual lock on the horizon.

Kaelen opened his eyes. They were clear, unclouded by panic or exhaustion. "It feels like weight," he said quietly. "But not crushing. Like carrying a heavy backpack up a hill. If I stop walking, it slows me down. If I keep walking, it doesn’t break my legs."

"That’s the goal," Elias said. "You are the conduit, but you must walk through the pressure, not let it crush you. Torin, if he stumbles, do not catch him with your arm; step in behind him and brace his hips."

"I know the drill," Torin replied, though there was no heat in his voice. He had been bracing Kaelen since the beginning, his large hand a constant presence on the boy’s shoulder or back. The physical contact was grounding, a reminder of the solid world that existed outside the Blight’s influence. It did not break Kaelen; it reminded him he was still part of the party, not just a vessel for power.

Maren finally removed her hands from Elara’s shoulders, stepping back to watch Elias and Torin. "He’s holding the line," she said. "The gauge in my gut says we’re good. The chisel angle is steady. We can push another minute or two."

Elias nodded. He had been watching the barrier with a focus that bordered on obsession. The violet light was not flickering, but it had also stopped expanding at its edges. It had settled into a fixed shape, a defined boundary between the world of the living and the creeping green fog beyond. Within that boundary, the Blight was being consumed, transformed from a corrosive poison into something the barrier could use to reinforce its own structure.

"This is it," Elias said softly. "We have bought the time we needed."

"And what now?" Torin asked, lowering his hand from Kaelen’s shoulder just enough to let the boy shift his weight. The movement was slow, careful. Kaelen did not flinch; he simply adjusted his stance, taking a small step forward as if the ground had shifted beneath him but remained firm underfoot.

"Now we prepare for the final push," Elias said. "But first, we rest. Not for long, but enough to let our minds catch up to what our bodies are doing."

The group did not move immediately. They stood in the clearing, bathed in the violet light that seemed to pulse with a quiet confidence. The fog beyond the barrier was still there, thick and oppressive, but it no longer pressed against them like a living thing. It was separated by the wall of light, reduced to a distant threat rather than an immediate danger.

Kaelen flexed his fingers inside the gauntlet. The brass warmed further, not burning him but embracing him with a gentle heat that felt almost comforting compared to the cold dread he had carried for weeks. He took a deep breath, the air tasting of ozone and old stone. "It’s quiet," he said, more to himself than to anyone else.

"Yes," Elias agreed. "For now."

He glanced at Elara, who was still holding the chisel, her expression unreadable but focused. "Keep the angle," he said. "Do not adjust until I give the word. Maren, stay ready in case we need to drop back down a notch if Kaelen shows any sign of strain."

Maren gave a single nod, her eyes scanning the horizon for any sign of the fog trying to breach the seal. The danger had not vanished; it had merely been managed. And that was enough for now. They were not heroes standing victorious on a battlefield; they were technicians maintaining a critical piece of infrastructure in a world gone mad.

The hum grew louder, a deep thrumming sound that seemed to vibrate through the very stones of the shrine and up into their chests. It was not unpleasant, not at first glance. It felt like the song of a machine that had finally found its proper frequency.

Elias looked at his companions: Kaelen, who was no longer afraid but rather integrated with the power he carried; Torin, who stood ready to protect the line without needing to shout orders; Elara, whose focus had sharpened into something almost mechanical; and Maren, whose strength held them all together.

They were not just surviving anymore. They were functioning. And for the first time in weeks, that felt like a victory worth celebrating.

The morning light was pale and thin, filtering through the gaps in the heavy canvas awnings that lined the market district. It cast long, distorted shadows across the cobblestones before the first wagon wheels had even begun to turn.

Elias pulled his cloak tighter around his shoulders, the fabric scratching against a side that still throbbed with a dull, persistent ache. He stood near the edge of the Gilded Tankard's outer wall, watching the caravan assemble in the street. It was a procession of heavy wooden carts, each laden with crates secured by iron bands and thick ropes. The air smelled of wet wool, horse manure, and the sharp tang of sulfur paste that Maren had insisted they carry.

Torin stood at the head of the formation, his hands on the reins of the lead draft team. He wore a leather apron stained with grease and oil, a practical garment that spoke of a man who preferred to get his hands dirty rather than argue over theory. Beside him, Maren was already checking the tension on the rigging, her fingers running along the line of iron-bound cable they had purchased the previous day.

"Stable," she murmured, tapping the rope with a knuckle. "No slack that isn't accounted for."

Elias stepped forward, joining them before Silas Vane’s wagon could even roll free. The merchant was already moving his own cart to the rear of the line, where he had instructed a pair of guards to stand watch over his personal goods. He looked tired, but his eyes were bright with a new kind of energy—the satisfaction of a deal struck and a road cleared.

"Ready?" Elias asked, glancing at Torin.

Torin nodded, though his brow was furrowed in that familiar way that meant he was running silent calculations in the back of his mind. "The wagons are locked to the lead team," he said. "We’ve got three days’ worth of buffer on the water purification system. The sulfur paste is stowed in the third cart, right behind Elara’s supplies."

"And the crate?" Maren asked, not looking up from her inspection of the rigging.

"The sealed container is mounted to the lead wagon," Torin confirmed. "Double-latched. Elias and I will stay at the front. If anyone comes out of nowhere, we’re the first to see it."

Elias nodded. He had expected this arrangement, but hearing it spoken aloud felt like a weight lifting from his chest. They weren’t improvising anymore. They were moving as a unit. "Good," he said. "Silas is at the back. If anything happens, he’ll signal us. We keep the line intact until we know what’s coming."

Maren finally looked up, her expression unreadable but her posture relaxed. "We’re not just walking into danger with blinders on," she noted. "We’re walking into it with eyes open and a plan in place."

Elias smiled faintly. "That’s the idea."

They moved then, stepping out from the shelter of the tavern wall and onto the dusty road that wound through the valley. The first wagon creaked as Torin gave the reins a sharp tug, and the team of four horses responded with a sudden burst of motion. The rest of the caravan followed in quick succession, a rolling procession of wood and iron that seemed to absorb the morning’s chill rather than resist it.

As they moved, Elias took his place at the front, beside Torin. He kept one hand near his sword hilt, feeling the familiar weight of the steel against his palm. The road ahead was still unknown; the valley stretched out before them like a ribbon of gray and green, dotted with distant hills and shadowed ravines. There were no signs of Blight-Touched creatures yet—no flicker of movement in the brush, no low growl carried on the wind—but the silence felt heavy, charged with anticipation.

Behind him, Maren walked with a steady gait, her eyes scanning the flanks of the caravan. She moved with a quiet efficiency, checking the tension of the rigging, listening for irregularities in the horses’ breathing, and keeping one hand near the pouch at her belt where she kept her antiseptic paste.

Elara had claimed the seat on the second wagon, tucked away beneath the canvas cover that shielded her from the sun and the wind. She was already working, her stylus moving across a fresh sheet of parchment as she adjusted their route plan based on the latest timing notes. Every now and then, she would glance back toward Elias and Torin, nodding or making a brief comment that Elias caught only in fragments.

Silas Vane remained at the rear, his cart trailing behind the last of the wagons. He kept a low profile, but he was clearly alert, his hand resting on the hilt of a short blade that hung at his hip. Occasionally, he would raise a hand to signal Torin or Elias, passing along information about the road ahead or warning them of potential hazards they might have missed.

The caravan moved with a rhythm that felt almost mechanical in its precision, each wagon following the one before it like a single organism moving through the landscape. There was no need for shouting or coordination; the system itself ensured that everyone stayed in place, that no one fell behind, and that the line remained intact.

Elias found himself thinking less about the dangers ahead and more about the sense of purpose that had taken root within them. They were not just a group of adventurers who had stumbled into an adventure; they were a team that had prepared, that had trained, that had learned from their mistakes and adapted to the world around them.

And now, as the sun climbed higher and the road stretched out before them like a ribbon of gray and green, he felt a quiet confidence settle in his chest. They were ready.

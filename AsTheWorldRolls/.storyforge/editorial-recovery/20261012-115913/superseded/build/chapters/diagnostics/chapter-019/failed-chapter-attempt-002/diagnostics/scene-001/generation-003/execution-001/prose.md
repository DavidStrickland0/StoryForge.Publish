The heavy oak door of the tavern swung shut behind them, sealing out the chaotic warmth of the Gilded Tankard—the smell of roasting meat, stale ale, and the metallic tang of Blight residue—and plunging the party into the cool, dry air of an early autumn morning. The light was a pale gold, cutting sharply through the mist that still clung to the cobblestones of the market district before burning itself off over the river.

Silas Vane walked briskly ahead, his boots clicking against the stone with a nervous rhythm that spoke louder than any words could have. He kept his eyes fixed on the path, avoiding the gaze of anyone who lingered too long near the city gates or stared at the caravan’s flank. His hand rested lightly on the pommel of a short sword tucked into his belt, not for defense, but for a prop to lean against when the conversation turned awkward.

Behind him, the party moved as a single unit. Torin carried the bulk of their supplies on his back: the iron-bound rope they had secured from the market stall, the crate of high-grade flint, and the bundles of antiseptic paste and oilskins that Maren had insisted upon. He adjusted the straps with a practiced efficiency, ensuring the weight was distributed correctly to minimize fatigue over the long miles.

Elias walked beside Silas, his side still throb-bing with a dull ache from the previous day’s skirmish, but he kept his posture straight. He watched the man ahead, noting the way Vane’s shoulders hunched slightly whenever a group of laborers shouted past them. The anxiety was not performative; it was rooted in the nature of their job. Silas wasn’t just transporting water; he was transporting a liability that could burn through a wall of wood and steel if provoked.

The caravan itself was a modest affair compared to the merchant’s tales of grand expeditions. It consisted of three wagons, their wheels reinforced with iron rims and their bodies braced with extra cross-beams. Between them rode two large crates, wrapped in oilskin and bound with the thick hemp rope Torin had inspected. One crate bore the seal of the Temple Springs, marked with a silver sigil that shimmered faintly even in the dim morning light. The other was unmarked, containing Silas’s personal effects and provisions.

The lead wagon was empty, pulled by a team of four mules that snorted at the sight of the Blight residue on Elias’s bandages before trampling near it with aggressive indifference. A young driver sat atop the box, checking his reins and keeping an eye on the road ahead. He looked young for someone guiding livestock through the valley—perhaps sixteen or seventeen—but his hands were steady.

“Alright, then,” Silas said, breaking the silence without turning around. “We have two hours before the first crest.”

Elias nodded. “Torin and Maren are ready at the front gate. Elara and Kaelen will stay with the rear convoy to monitor the seals.”

Silas hesitated before stepping over a puddle that reflected the pale sky. “The rear convoy is… necessary,” he muttered, then corrected himself smoothly. “We don’t want anything happening to the back of the line while we’re dealing with the front. It’s protocol.”

Elias glanced at him sideways. “Protocol or fear?”

Silas didn’t answer immediately. He adjusted his hat, pulling it low over his eyes. “Fear is… inefficient,” he said finally. “But it’s better than underestimating a customer who might demand a refund for spoiled water.”

Torin chuckled from the back of the wagon where Maren had taken up space beside him, examining a coil of rope against a crate. “You’re overthinking this, Silas. We’ve got a plan. A schedule. And we know exactly what we’re dealing with.”

Maren added dryly, “And if someone tries to steal the crate from behind us, Elara has a few counter-spells ready. If someone tries to sabotage the front, Elias and I will be there before they even make contact.”

Silas let out a short laugh, though it lacked real humor. “You’ve got that right,” he said, then turned his attention back to the road. “Just remember: if the Blight-Touched show up early, we don’t engage unless you’re ready for full commitment. We move forward or we retreat together. No heroics on your own.”

“I wouldn’t dream of it,” Elias said, keeping his tone light despite the weight of responsibility pressing down on him. “We stick to the plan.”

They walked in a loose formation around the wagons, heading toward the northern ridge where the caravan would begin its ascent. The air was crisp and smelled faintly of woodsmoke and damp earth as they passed fields where farmers were already beginning their daily chores. A few villagers waved from doorsteps, offering quiet greetings that Elias returned with polite nods.

As they approached the first stretch of open road leading into the valley proper, Elias felt a sense of calm settle over him—not because the danger was gone, but because it had been accounted for. They had the plan. They had the supplies. And they had each other.

Torin paused beside one of the wagons, checking the tension on the lead ropes. “We’re good to go,” he called out, his voice steady. “Maren’s checked the shackles. The iron-bound cable is secure.”

Maren stepped forward, holding a small vial of clear liquid that smelled faintly of sulfur and ash. “And the antiseptic is ready for emergency use if needed,” she added, tossing the vial to Elias. He caught it easily, though he knew better than to drink from it unless absolutely necessary.

Silas cleared his throat. “Alright, let’s move. The water waits for no one.”

Elias gave a brief nod and stepped forward, joining the group as they began their climb toward the first ridge. The road wound upward through the foothills of the mountain range that loomed in the distance, its peaks shrouded in low-hanging clouds that seemed to shift whenever someone looked directly at them. As they ascended, the landscape grew steeper and more rugged, with patches of moss clinging to rocks that had long since given up their hold on soil.

Behind them, the city gates receded into the distance, a silhouette of stone and wood against a sky that promised nothing but quiet days ahead—or perhaps something far worse. Ahead lay the first real test of their journey: the surge.

They moved forward together, a team bound not just by circumstance but by choice. They were ready.

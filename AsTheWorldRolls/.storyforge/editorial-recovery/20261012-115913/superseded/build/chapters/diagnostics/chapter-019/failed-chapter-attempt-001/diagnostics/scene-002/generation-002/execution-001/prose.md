The air on the ridge was thin, chilled by the early dawn, but the static charge humming in Elias Thorne's skin was a more immediate warning. He stood at the point of the formation, his blade drawn, feeling the pull of something invisible yet heavy as lead. Behind him, the wagons rattled with the nervous energy of Silas Vane’s hired hands and the party, while ahead lay the shadowed ravine that marked the edge of the valley floor.

Torin was already moving, a blur of grey wool and leather, checking the tension on the ropes lashed to the central wagon where the sealed crate sat. Maren had taken her station on the rear flank, her spear leveled toward the treeline as if she were guarding a sleeping dragon’s lair rather than a merchant convoy. Silas Vane stood near the lead wagon, his face grim, watching the horizon with the practiced eye of a man who had seen too many caravans die in their sleep.

Elias didn't wait for the third warning tremor. The first sign was a scent—not the fresh pine of the ridge, but the cloying, metallic smell of old copper and rotting meat that clung to the wind like a bad memory.

"Three," Elias muttered under his breath, counting the shadows detaching themselves from the tree line.

The first creature emerged from the gloom as if it had been waiting there in human form all night. It was tall and hunched, its limbs elongated by some grotesque stretch of anatomy, and its skin was a mottled patchwork of grey stone and blackened flesh where the Blight had taken root. Its eyes were hollow sockets filled with thick, black ichor that wept silently. It did not roar; it simply moved with a jerky, unnatural precision, drawn by something it could sense but not comprehend.

"Maren," Elias said quietly, his voice steady despite the adrenaline rising in his throat. "Hold your ground."

Maren didn't blink. She shifted her weight, planting her feet firmly against the gravel road as the creature lunged toward the rear wagon. The spear thrust was clean and true; it found purchase in the gap between the creature's ribs with a sickening crunch that made Elias wince instinctively before he realized the sound wasn't his own imagination. The creature recoiled, staggering back a step, its grip loosening on the air as Maren pivoted and drove the butt of her spear into its temple with brutal efficiency.

The second attacker was already airborne when Torin stepped forward from the flank. He didn't shout; he simply swung his greatsword in an arc that cut through the humid morning air like a blade through butter. The creature met the blow with its own arm, but the momentum of Torin's swing was too much for it to withstand. The sword bit deep into its shoulder joint, severing muscle and bone alike, and the creature shrieked—a sound that wasn't quite human or animal, more like grinding metal and wet cloth.

Elias moved forward as the third creature approached him from the front. It was larger than the others, its chest heaving with each labored breath, its mouth open in a silent scream of hunger. Elias didn't hesitate; he closed the distance in two strides, his blade flashing in the grey light. He parried the creature's clumsy claw swipe and drove his point into its throat. The creature convulsed once, then collapsed to its knees, dissolving instantly into a cloud of spores that drifted upward on the wind before vanishing entirely.

"Good work," Elias said, sheathing his sword as the remaining two creatures scrambled backward, retreating toward the ravine they had come from. They weren't fighting because they were afraid; they were retreating because they had no choice. The battle was over before it had truly begun.

The party stood panting for a moment, their adrenaline fading into a dull ache in their muscles. Maren lowered her spear, brushing the dirt from her coat as if she'd just finished scrubbing a spill rather than saving lives. Torin sheathed his greatsword with a soft click, glancing at Elias with a nod of approval that felt like a rare gift from an old friend.

"You did well," Elias said, turning to face them both. "But we're not done yet."

He looked back toward the main wagon where Silas Vane was already organizing his men, shouting orders about securing the awnings and reinforcing the wheels. The crate remained untouched on its trailer, the seal still intact despite the violence around it. Elias walked over to inspect it closely, running a gloved hand along the edge of the wood. There were no dents, no scratches, no visible damage from the creatures' claws or teeth. Yet when he touched the surface, the static charge spiked again, sharper and more intense than before.

"The water," Elias said aloud, as if confirming something to himself rather than explaining it to anyone else. "It's not just stable—it's pulling them in."

Maren stepped forward, her expression unreadable but her eyes narrowed with focus. She crouched beside the crate, examining the seal that held the liquid inside. Her fingers hovered over the waxed cloth wrapping before touching the metal rim of the container. The air around her seemed to ripple like heat haze on a summer day.

"It's not just attraction," she said, her voice low and measured. "It's resonance. Whatever energy is stored in that water—it's resonating with something out there."

Elias nodded slowly, watching as Maren pulled a small vial from her belt containing a clear liquid that shimmered faintly. She uncorked it and held the opening toward the creature’s spore cloud that was still dissipating above them. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, with a sound like wind rushing through a canyon, the spores were drawn into the vial, swirling violently before vanishing completely.

"See?" Maren said, capping the vial with a sharp twist. "The water calls to them. And if we let it go without protection..." She gestured toward the crate. "...they'll keep coming until there's nothing left to draw."

Elias looked at her, then back at the crate. The implications were clear now: they weren't just protecting cargo; they were guarding a focal point for whatever force was driving these creatures mad. If the seal broke—if even a drop spilled—the effect would multiply exponentially.

"We need to move it," Elias said, his tone firm but not panicked. "Away from the wagons. Toward the ridge edge where we can control the distance."

Torin nodded immediately, already pulling at the iron-bound rope that secured the crate to the trailer. He worked efficiently, adjusting the tension until the crate was free enough to be rolled without dragging. Elias and Maren joined in, lifting the container together as Torin guided it onto a makeshift sled carved from one of the wagons' spare planks.

Silas Vane approached cautiously, his men trailing behind him with tools and supplies. "What's happening?" he asked, his voice strained with concern.

"The water is unstable," Elias explained quickly, keeping his eyes on the creatures as they continued to retreat into the ravine. "We need to move the crate farther from the wagons. If we leave it here, they'll just keep coming until—"

"Until what?" Silas interrupted, though he didn't finish the sentence. His face went pale as he realized where Elias was going with that thought.

"Until there's nothing left to draw," Maren finished for him, her voice calm but urgent. "We move now."

Together, they worked in perfect sync, shifting the crate until it was positioned at the edge of the ridge, far enough away from the wagons to minimize risk but close enough that Elias could still monitor the situation. Once the crate was secure, he turned to Silas Vane, who was watching with wide eyes as the last creature disappeared into the ravine.

"Is this normal?" Silas asked, his voice trembling slightly. "Did you always have to fight like this? Or is something wrong with the water?"

Elias shook his head slowly. "Nothing's wrong. But things are... different than expected. We need to be careful moving forward. The creatures aren't random—they're drawn by the water itself."

Silas nodded, though he didn't say anything more. He simply turned back to his men, giving orders about setting up additional barriers and reinforcing the perimeter. Elias watched him go, then looked at Maren and Torin. They were breathing heavily, their faces smeared with dirt and sweat, but their expressions were steady.

"We did good," Torin said finally, wiping his brow with the back of his hand. "Not bad for a caravan that shouldn't exist."

Elias smiled faintly, feeling a sense of relief wash over him as the tension in his shoulders eased. They had faced danger head-on and come out ahead. The creatures were gone, the crate was secure, and they still had the day ahead of them. But as he looked at the horizon where the sun was just beginning to rise, casting long shadows across the valley floor, he knew this wasn't over yet.

"There's more coming," Elias said quietly, pointing toward the ravine behind them. "And we're not done yet."

The sun had climbed high enough that the shadows under the tables were short and sharp, cutting through the ambient noise of the Gilded Tankard. Elias sat back in his chair, the wood creaking under his weight, and smoothed a wrinkled parchment across the table with a calloused thumb. The air between him and Silas Vane felt charged not with magic, but with the friction of two very different kinds of business.

Vane had been leaning against the doorframe for the last ten minutes, watching the party like a hawk guarding its prey. Now he stepped forward again, his boots clicking on the flagstones with a rhythm that suggested he was in a hurry to finish whatever this transaction was becoming.

“Let’s not let the ink dry too slowly,” Vane said, spreading the second sheet of parchment over the first. “The terms for the primary escort are clear enough—gold per mile, protection fee included. But I must insist on the secondary clause.”

Elias didn’t look up immediately. He traced a line along the edge of the paper with his finger. “You mean the provision that allows your associates to join the caravan whenever they please?” he asked, his voice level. “The one that lets them intercept supplies or claim ‘emergency’ stops at will?”

“Exactly,” Vane said, smiling as if he’d been expecting this objection all along. “It’s standard practice in the trade. You never know when a merchant needs extra hands to unload crates or secure a perimeter during a sudden storm.”

Elias finally met his gaze, and for the first time since they’d entered the booth, his expression softened into something resembling amusement rather than suspicion. “Standard practice,” he repeated slowly. “Or standard greed? Because if I’m reading this correctly, your associates aren’t just ‘joining’—they’re inserting themselves into our supply chain without notice.”

Vane’s smile tightened slightly, but he kept his tone light. “Greedy? No, Thorne. We’re thorough. And in this business, thoroughness means knowing where every crate is at all times. If your caravan gets delayed by a minor accident—a collapsed bridge, a bandit raid, a magical anomaly—we need to be ready to fill the gap.”

“Which means,” Elias said, letting the word hang in the air, “you’re planning to steal our cargo before we even reach the first settlement.”

Vane blinked once, then laughed softly. It wasn’t cruel laughter; it was the kind a man gives when someone misinterprets his intent entirely. “Steal? Hardly. We’d rather you keep your goods safe than lose them to thieves or bandits. But yes, we do intend to take custody of any surplus supplies once they’re no longer needed for your journey.”

Elias leaned forward slightly, resting his elbows on the table and staring at the contract again. “Surplus supplies,” he murmured. “That’s convenient wording. Because if we carry too much water or too little food, who decides what counts as ‘surplus’?”

Vane shrugged, crossing one arm over his chest while holding a flagon of ale in the other hand. He didn’t drink; instead, he gestured with the bottle toward Elias as if it were already part of the argument. “You decide,” he said smoothly. “But let me assure you: we won’t take anything unless you explicitly tell us to. And even then, we’ll pay fair market value.”

“Fair market value,” Elias echoed skeptically. “For goods that belong to the party who hired you?”

Vane raised an eyebrow, his expression unbothered. “In the trade, ownership is fluid until delivery. Once you cross the riverbank and begin your ascent toward the mountains, the caravan becomes a shared asset. Your crew handles security; ours handles logistics. It’s efficient.”

Elias shook his head slowly, closing his eyes briefly as if trying to dispel the headache forming behind them. “Efficient,” he said quietly. “Sounds like something I’d read in a merchant guild handbook written by someone who’s never seen a real fight.”

Vane chuckled again, unfazed by the jab. “Perhaps,” he admitted openly. “But even mistakes can lead to greatness—with the right guidance.” He paused dramatically, then added, “And perhaps we might discuss compensation?”

Elias exchanged a glance with Maren before responding. “Compensation is secondary to competence,” he said firmly. “If you want our business, show us why we should trust you with our lives.”

Vane tilted his head slightly, considering this response carefully before replying. “Fair enough,” he conceded gracefully. “But let’s not forget that trust is earned over time—not handed out freely like candy.” He gestured toward the entrance again, where two figures stood chatting quietly near the doorway. One of them glanced briefly at their table before turning away, but his words carried clearly enough for everyone present to hear.

“Did you hear that?” Maren asked sharply. “They’re spreading rumors about us already.”

Kaelen nodded slowly from across the table, where he sat reading one of Elara’s journals aloud while Torin sharpened a dagger nearby. “Yes,” he confirmed evenly. “Something about incompetence and lack of skill.”

Elias sighed deeply, rubbing his temples as though trying to dispel the headache forming there. “Let’s not take it personally,” he advised calmly. “People will always talk when they don’t understand something—or worse, when they’re jealous.”

Maren grunted noncommittally but didn’t respond otherwise. Instead, she focused on Vane, whose smile had returned full force now that the rivals had dispersed into the crowd. “We should hear these rumors out,” she said quietly, though her tone wasn’t accusatory or defensive—it was pragmatic. “If we ignore them now, they’ll keep spreading until someone takes action.”

Elias nodded slowly. “You’re right,” he admitted. “Let’s hear what they say.” He stood up then, pulling his chair back with a scrape of wood against stone that cut sharply through the ambient noise. “Come along,” he said to Silas, gesturing toward the entrance where the two rivals stood arguing amongst themselves near the door.

Vane followed obediently, though his expression remained unreadable as they approached the group. The first speaker was a tall woman with braided hair and goggles pushed up against her forehead, wearing armor that looked like it had been scavenged from various battles past. Her companion was shorter, clad in heavy robes embroidered with symbols that Elias couldn’t quite make out at a glance. Both held themselves with confidence bordering on arrogance, their voices rising above the tavern’s chatter as they turned to face the party.

“Thorne!” the woman called out, her voice bright enough to carry halfway down the block. “We heard you’ve been causing nothing but trouble lately! How does one group of incompetent adventurers manage to survive long enough to spread rumors like wildfire?” She laughed heartily at her own joke, earning a few nervous chuckles from nearby tables while others rolled their eyes or muttered under their breaths.

Elias raised an eyebrow, waiting for the punchline that hadn’t come yet. Instead, he stepped forward, placing himself squarely between the rivals and Silas. “Careful with your words,” he said calmly. “Unless you have proof of our incompetence, those are just opinions wrapped in insults.”

The woman crossed her arms defiantly, stepping closer to the booth where Elias stood. “Oh, we’ve got plenty of proof!” she declared, raising her voice so everyone could hear. “Last time I saw your team around here, they were trying to negotiate with a merchant who wouldn’t even let them inside his shop! That’s incompetence right there!”

Silas interjected smoothly, leaning against the table again as if he hadn’t moved at all during the exchange. “Perhaps she means something else entirely,” he suggested gently. “Merchants often refuse entry to untested adventurers until they prove themselves capable. It’s standard procedure.”

The rival woman blinked, caught off guard by Silas’s intervention. Her companion nodded nervously behind her, lowering their voice but continuing to argue amongst themselves regardless. “Still!” the woman insisted stubbornly. “And then there was that incident with the drunk patron! You managed to calm him down without getting hurt—but only because you didn’t actually try to fight him in the first place. That’s not bravery; it’s cowardice!”

Maren stepped forward then, her expression cool and composed despite the provocation. She crossed her arms over her chest, mirroring the posture of the rival woman but projecting authority rather than defensiveness. “Bravery isn’t about fighting when you can avoid it,” she said clearly, enunciating every word so that even those sitting at distant tables could understand. “It’s about knowing when to hold your ground and when to walk away. My friend here demonstrated exactly that skill.”

Silas smiled warmly, nodding his approval at Maren’s retort before turning his attention back to the rivals. “You seem to miss the point entirely,” he noted mildly. “Adventurers come in all shapes and sizes, each with their own strengths and weaknesses. Some rely on swords and shields; others prefer wits over weapons. Neither approach is inherently superior unless circumstances dictate otherwise.”

The rival woman opened her mouth to respond, only for Silas to cut her off before she could speak again. “Let me finish,” he continued smoothly. “If your team truly believes our group lacks skill, perhaps you’d care to demonstrate why? Show us where we went wrong?” He gestured broadly toward the tavern interior, inviting them to engage in whatever manner they chose.

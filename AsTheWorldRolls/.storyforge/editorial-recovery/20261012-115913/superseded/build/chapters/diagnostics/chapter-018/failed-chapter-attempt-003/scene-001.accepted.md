The afternoon sun beat down on the cobblestones of the market square, turning the air into a shimmering haze that danced just above the stalls. Elias stood at the edge of the crowd, his hands deep in the pockets of his travel-worn coat. Silas Vane lingered near the booth, looking less like a confident merchant and more like a man waiting for a verdict.

The rival adventurers who had caused the stir earlier were nowhere to be seen now—either gone back to their own business or intimidated into silence by Maren’s glare. But the tension at Elias’s table hadn’t vanished; it had settled into something quieter, heavier, the kind of atmosphere that precedes a storm breaking over dry land.

Silas shifted his weight, tapping the rolled parchment with one hand. “So,” he said lightly, though there was an edge to his tone. “You’ve decided?”

Elias didn’t turn away from him yet. He watched the market-goers—merchants haggling over silk bolts, farmers offering crates of root vegetables, children chasing kites that dipped low into the square. The noise swelled and dipped like a breathing thing around them. “We’ve heard your terms,” Elias said evenly. “Supplies, intelligence, maps—we’ll take it all. But on one condition.”

Silas raised an eyebrow, his grin narrowing slightly. “And what would that be?” he asked, though he didn’t sound angry—just curious. Perhaps even a little intrigued. That was Silas’s way; if someone stood their ground, he liked to see how far they’d go.

“Fair payment for everything,” Elias replied. “No dilution of focus. No excuses for why we can’t protect something valuable because it’s not your favorite story.” He paused, letting the words hang in the air before continuing. “If you want our business, you treat us like partners—not clients who need saving from their own stupidity.”

Silas chuckled softly, shaking his head with an amused smile that didn’t reach his eyes quite as much as it usually did. “Partners,” he mused, tasting the word aloud. “That’s a fine idea, Thorne. Very professional indeed.” He stepped closer, lowering his voice so only Elias could hear. “But let me tell you something: most parties think they’re brave until they face real danger. Then they panic, they split up, and they leave their leader behind to die while they run off to save someone else.”

Elias kept his expression neutral, watching the man’s posture shift subtly—shoulders tensing, hands tightening around the flagon of ale he still held. “We’re not most parties,” Elias said simply. “And we don’t plan on leaving anyone behind today or tomorrow.”

Silas laughed again, though this time it sounded forced. “Well now,” he drawled, leaning in again with theatrical flourish. “You certainly have spirit. Most folks would have accepted my terms without question. But since you’ve insisted…” He gestured toward the table where Maren sat cross-legged, her fingers tracing patterns in the dust of Kaelen’s boots while Elara flipped through diagrams marked with red ink and blue lines showing flow paths. “Since you’ve drawn attention to yourself so loudly, perhaps we should adjust our deal accordingly.”

Elias turned back fully toward him then, stepping out from behind the table where he’d been standing just moments ago. “What do you mean by ‘adjust’?” he asked sharply. The way he spoke was direct, leaving no room for misinterpretation or deflection. “Explain yourself before we proceed further.”

Silas spread his arms wide as if displaying something grand behind him. “Adjustment implies change,” he said smoothly. “Change means compromise. Compromise means better terms—for both sides.” He paused dramatically, letting silence stretch between them like a taut wire. “Consider this: if you accept my original terms, you’ll get exactly what we discussed yesterday—potions, maps, weapons, whatever else you might need on your journey onward. But if you insist on renegotiating now, well…” He trailed off, glancing briefly at Elias’s face before returning his gaze to the bustling market beyond them.

“Go on,” Elias said flatly, his tone carrying none of its usual warmth. “Tell me what kind of adjustments you’re thinking about making.”

Silas grinned again, though there was less charm in it this time. “Nothing drastic,” he assured him. “Just small tweaks here and there to accommodate… special requests.” He tapped the flagon with his fingernails. “For instance, perhaps we could include a clause stating that any extra charges incurred during transport shall be deducted from your final payment. Or maybe you’d prefer us to cover those costs upfront? Either way works equally well, doesn’t it?”

Elias frowned slightly, crossing his arms over his chest. “Special requests sound suspicious,” he said quietly. “Especially when they come wrapped in promises of fairness.” He glanced toward Silas’s wagon parked outside the tavern entrance, where two apprentices were loading crates marked with silver sigils onto waiting mules. “What exactly are these ‘special requests’?”

Silas stepped back into the light cast by the market stalls, his face illuminated by shafts of golden sunlight that filtered through the canopy of awnings overhead. “Nothing untoward,” he assured him again. “Just business logic.” He held up one hand defensively. “You wouldn’t expect us to throw free supplies at every turn, would you? We operate on principles—principles that prioritize quality over quantity, and value over volume. If you demand perfection without paying accordingly, we’ll be forced to reconsider our partnership entirely.”

Elias studied the man carefully, noting the way his eyes flickered toward the apprentices working near the wagon, then back to Elias’s face. There was a calculative quality to Silas’s demeanor now—a hint of unease beneath the practiced smile. “I suppose I can accept your explanation,” Elias said slowly, though he didn’t sound convinced. “But let me ask you this: if you truly believe in your products, why are you trying to negotiate terms instead of simply delivering what was promised?”

Silas’s expression softened slightly, though his grin remained fixed in place. “Because trust isn’t given freely,” he replied. “It’s earned over time—and sometimes, people forget that lesson until it’s too late.” He gestured broadly with both hands, sweeping them outward as if encompassing the entire market square. “But don’t worry about my motives here. Let’s focus on the matter at hand: securing fair payment for goods delivered and services rendered.”

Elias nodded slowly, though he still didn’t trust the man entirely. “Fair enough,” he said finally. “But before we sign anything, let me make one thing clear: we’re not here to play games. If you want our business, show us why we should trust you with our lives—and our cargo.”

Silas blinked once, caught off guard by Elias’s bluntness. Then he recovered quickly, smoothing down his robes with deliberate precision. “Very well,” he agreed gracefully. “Let’s get this done then. No more games, no more delays—just straight-to-the-point negotiation.” He reached into his coat and produced a second contract, identical to the first except for a few handwritten additions in neat script across the bottom margin. “Here are the revised terms as you requested. Sign them here,” he instructed, pointing to a blank line where Elias’s signature belonged.

Elias took the document with careful attention, scanning each clause before setting it down again on the table. There was no rush, no sense of urgency that might have led him to overlook something important hidden within those lines. Instead, he read every word aloud, muttering phrases like *deducted from final payment* and *extra charges incurred during transport* under his breath while Silas waited patiently beside him.

When Elias finished reading, he looked up at the merchant again, his expression unreadable. “Okay,” he said finally. “These terms seem reasonable enough. But there’s still one thing bothering me.” He gestured vaguely toward the contract without specifying what exactly troubled him. “Why does everything seem so complicated? Why can’t we just agree on something simple?”

Silas laughed softly, shaking his head with a mix of amusement and resignation. “Simple solutions rarely survive complex situations,” he noted mildly. “Life—and business—is full of complications that require thoughtful planning. That’s why we charge premium prices for quality goods: because they’re worth every penny.”

Elias considered this briefly before nodding again. “Maybe you’re right,” he admitted reluctantly. “But I still don’t fully understand why we can’t trust each other completely. Isn’t that how partnerships work? By building mutual respect and honesty between us?”

Silas leaned casually against the table once more, crossing one arm over his chest while holding his flagon in the other hand. “Mutual respect is essential,” he agreed without hesitation. “Honesty is ideal—but realism often requires compromise. Compromise means finding common ground where both parties feel satisfied with the outcome.” He smiled again, though there was less genuine warmth in it this time. “Now, shall we finalize this agreement and move on? The caravan won’t wait forever for us to settle these details.”

Elias picked up his pen, uncapping it with practiced ease before signing his name firmly across the bottom of the page. “Done,” he declared once he finished writing. He handed the contract back to Silas, who accepted it with a nod of approval. “Consider this our agreement from now on.”

The barrier hummed—a steady, low note that vibrated in the marrow of Elias’s bones rather than shaking them apart. Elias moved to where Kaelen leaned against Torin’s leg. The young man looked up as Elias approached, his eyes clearer now, the hazy panic from earlier replaced by a focused calm. A valve that could open and close without needing a key. “That’s progress,” Elias said. “But we can’t hold this with your body. You’ll burn out before you’ve drained the reservoir.”

Maren followed suit, stepping away from the edge and watching the flow with a mixture of relief and lingering caution.

It was absorbing the Blight not just as a wall would block wind, but as a lung would inhale air. The green light within it swirled, darkening as it digested the corruption, then fading to a soft violet that seemed almost peaceful in its regularity.

“Elara,” Elias said, keeping his voice low so as not to disturb the delicate balance. “Keep your ear to the stone. If the pitch spikes, warn me immediately.”

Elara nodded, pressing her palm flat against the cold stone beside the fissure. She closed her eyes, letting the vibration travel up her arm and settle in her bones. “It’s stable,” she said after a moment. “The shards are creating drag, but Kaelen is regulating the intake. We’re feeding it at a rate the system can handle.”

The gauntlet remained cold, but he could feel the Blight flowing through him like water through a pipe—no longer a burden, but a tool. The barrier grew stronger with every passing second. The violet light intensified, casting long shadows that seemed to dance in time with the hum. Elias watched the edges of the barrier where they met the shrine. There was no darkening there anymore; the shadow that had been eating the stone earlier had vanished, consumed along with the rest of the Blight. The mechanism was working as intended, but they hadn’t yet reached their goal. The reservoir beneath the city was vast, and what they were draining now was only a fraction of it.

“We’re making progress,” Elias said, his voice steady despite the intensity of the situation. “But we need to keep this up for another hour or so. Until the core is fully drained.”

Kaelen nodded, his expression serious. “I can do that. But I’ll need breaks. Every ten minutes or so, I have to close the valve and let my mind rest.”

“Then we take those breaks,” Elias agreed. “Torin, Maren—keep watch on the flow. If Kaelen slips, you intervene.”

Torin stepped forward, positioning himself between Kaelen and the fissure again, though this time his sword was sheathed. He wasn’t there to fight; he was there to support, to catch Kaelen if the pull ever returned. “Understood,” he said. “If he falters, I’ll be ready.”

Maren nodded, crossing her arms over her chest. “Same here. We won’t let it slip past us again.”

Elara stood up from the fissure, brushing dust from her knees. She looked at Kaelen, then at Elias. “The resonance is holding,” she said. “But I’m seeing something in the harmonic feedback. The Blight’s core is denser than we thought. It’s resisting digestion more than the outer layers did.”

But there were no cracks forming, no stress lines appearing. The system was stable, even as it processed something far greater than they had anticipated.

“Then we adjust,” Elias said. “Kaelen, slow the intake further. Let the barrier work harder on its own.”

The stream of Blight thinned, becoming a slender ribbon that wove through the air toward the barrier without overwhelming it.

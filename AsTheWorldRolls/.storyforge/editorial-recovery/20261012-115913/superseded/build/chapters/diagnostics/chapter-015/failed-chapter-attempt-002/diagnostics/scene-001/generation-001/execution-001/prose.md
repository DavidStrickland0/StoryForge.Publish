Elias did not move immediately after speaking the word. He held his hand up for a heartbeat longer, letting the silence stretch until it felt heavy enough to choke on. Then he lowered it, watching the barrier with a tension in his jaw that made his teeth ache.

The stream of Blight had changed.

Where it had been a thin, controlled ribbon of smoke before, it now surged forward like a sudden gale. It was no longer just flowing; it was being pulled, drawn out from the fissure with a violent urgency that rattled the stones of the shrine. The violet light of the barrier flared in response, brightening to a searing lavender, and the ground beneath them pulsed faster, *thump-thump-thump*, rising from a slow rhythm to a frantic thrumming.

Kaelen was still standing ten paces back, but he had stopped breathing normally. His eyes remained closed, his posture rigid as a statue carved from ice, but Elias saw the sweat darkening the hairline on Kaelen’s forehead. The young man’s skin was losing its pallor, turning a sickly, translucent gray where the veins beneath showed through like blue roots.

Kaelen had promised to pull. He had promised to open the valve wider. And he was doing exactly that.

But there was a cost.

Elias watched the brass gauntlet on Kaelen’s left wrist. The surface, previously cold and inert, began to glow from within. It wasn’t the green of the Blight, nor the pale gold of the barrier. It was a deep, burning orange, spreading rapidly up Kaelen’s arm like a fever breaking skin. The metal didn’t look heavy anymore; it looked like it might crack, as if the heat generated by the resonance was cooking the metal itself.

“Kaelen,” Elias said. His voice came out rough, unused to the effort of speaking aloud in this silence.

Kaelen did not open his eyes. “The channel is opening,” he murmured. His voice sounded thin, stretched across a great distance. “It’s... hungry.”

“It’s burning you,” Torin corrected sharply, stepping forward. He didn’t raise his sword; he knew that would only provoke a defensive reaction and waste energy. Instead, he placed a hand on Kaelen’s shoulder, not to push, but to feel the heat radiating through the fabric of the young man’s tunic. Torin’s own face was pale, but his grip was firm. “Elias, look at him.”

Elias looked. He saw the way Kaelen’s muscles were twitching beneath his clothes, not from fear, but from overexertion. The veins on his neck were black with heat. The air around them smelled of ozone and burnt copper, a sharp tang that made Elias’s nose itch.

“The pull is too much,” Elara said softly, though she didn’t look away from the barrier. Her chisel still rested against the quartz stone, the vibration having intensified to a high-pitched whine that vibrated in her teeth. “The resonance is feeding the beast faster than our seal can digest it. If we push further, the frequency will slip. The barrier will tear.”

“The barrier isn’t tearing,” Elias said, though he couldn’t deny the flicker of light at its edge. The violet hue was deepening to a bruised purple, and faint cracks were appearing in the solidified membrane, hairline fractures that seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it. “We’re making progress.”

“Progress that kills Kaelen is not progress,” Torin snapped. He looked at Elias, his eyes wide with a mixture of anger and fear. “He’s a conduit, Elias. Not a battery. If we drain him dry, he won’t just pass out. He’ll die right there in the clearing.”

Elias felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. He had known the risks. He had calculated the margin of error. But he had assumed that Kaelen’s will alone could regulate the flow. He had forgotten that the Blight was not just energy; it was entropy, and entropy demanded sacrifice.

“Elara,” Elias said, his voice dropping to a whisper that cut through the roar of the ground. “Adjust the angle. Reduce the strike force by half.”

Elara hesitated. Her eyes were locked on the quartz stone, watching the intensity of the glow. “If I reduce it, the flow will drop. We won’t consume enough to break the seal before the entity arrives.”

“Then we find a new balance,” Elias said. He turned his gaze back to Kaelen. The young man was still standing, but his legs were trembling slightly now, barely held together by sheer force of will. “Maren, brace him. If he slips, catch him. Torin, stay ready, but don’t intervene unless he loses control.”

Torin nodded, stepping closer to Kaelen’s other side. His hand moved to the hilt of his sword again, not in threat, but as an insurance policy.

Elara stepped forward and made a small adjustment to her grip on the chisel. She didn’t change the angle; instead, she altered the rhythm of her stance, letting the vibration build naturally rather than forcing it with a sharp strike. The quartz stone dimmed slightly, its glow shifting from a bright white to a soft amber.

The stream of Blight slowed. The surge receded into a steady, manageable current. The ground stopped shaking violently, settling back into a deep, rhythmic pulse that felt less like an earthquake and more like a heartbeat.

Kaelen’s gauntlet cooled visibly. The orange glow faded to a dull red, then to the cold, inert brass of before. But his face was worse. Sweat soaked through his hair, dripping down his temple in rivulets of water that didn’t fall—they evaporated before they hit the ground, leaving behind a fine dust of ash.

He opened his eyes. They were bloodshot, filled with tears that had no moisture to them. “It’s holding,” he rasped. His voice sounded like stones grinding together. “The barrier is... accepting it.”

Elias nodded, though he felt sick. The plan was working, but the price was becoming clear. They could not sustain this level of output without burning Kaelen alive. And they couldn’t afford to stop now. Not until the seal was broken.

“Keep the rhythm steady,” Elias said. “Don’t push harder. Just hold.”

Kaelen nodded again, a jerky motion that sent a shiver through his frame. He closed his eyes once more, and the fog around him began to swirl inward, drawn into the new, stabilized channel. The Blight flowed smoothly now, no longer a violent gale but a steady stream, feeding the barrier without overwhelming it.

The barrier pulsed in response, the violet light deepening to a rich, saturated hue that seemed to glow from within the stone itself. It was strong. It was stable. And yet, every second Kaelen held this position felt like stealing time from his own life.

Elias watched him, guilt twisting in his gut. He had asked for this. He had pushed them into this situation because there was no other way. But he couldn’t keep asking Kaelen to give more without offering something back. Something real.

“Torin,” Elias said quietly. “Check his vitals. See if he’s fading.”

Torin frowned, moving closer to Kaelen. He placed a hand on the young man’s forehead, feeling the heat radiating from him. “He’s burning up,” Torin said. “But he’s not losing focus. Not yet.”

“Not for long,” Elara added. She watched the barrier with intense concentration, her brow furrowed as she tried to find a way to stabilize the flow without sacrificing Kaelen. “The seal has a limit. Once we push past it, the frequency will destabilize. The barrier will tear, and the Blight will flood the valley.”

Elias looked at the horizon. The sun was beginning to rise, casting long shadows across the clearing. The fog was thinning, revealing more of the ruined city below. But the temple loomed in the distance, a dark silhouette against the brightening sky.

They had bought themselves time. But how much time? And what would they give for it?

Elias looked at Kaelen one last time, then at the barrier, and finally at the rising sun that promised a new day—and perhaps, an end to their struggle.

“Hold,” Elias said again. “Just hold.”

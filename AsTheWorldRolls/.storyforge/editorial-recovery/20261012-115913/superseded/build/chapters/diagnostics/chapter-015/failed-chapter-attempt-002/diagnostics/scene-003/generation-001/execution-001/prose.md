Elias did not move from his spot at the edge of the clearing. He kept his eyes on the barrier, watching the way the violet light seemed to grow brighter with every passing second. The world felt different now. The fog was still there, but it no longer threatened to swallow them whole. The path ahead was clearer. They had bought time, and they had used it well.

The hum of the quartz stone had settled into a rhythm that matched the pulse in Elias’s own ears. It wasn’t a violent thrumming anymore; it was a steady heartbeat, a living thing that acknowledged their presence without demanding a sacrifice. The ground beneath them vibrated with a gentle, rhythmic certainty rather than the chaotic stutter of earlier moments.

“Stable,” Elara whispered, her voice barely rising above the hum. She did not look at Elias, but her gaze remained fixed on the chisel in her hands. Her knuckles were white, gripping the steel with a precision that belied the exhaustion etched into her face. “The pitch is holding. The barrier’s intake rate matches our output.”

Elias nodded, though he made no sound. He watched the fissure in the shrine’s base. The green fluid that had once gushed out in chaotic waves was now reduced to a thin, controlled stream. It rose from the dark earth like steam from hot pavement, curling upward before being caught by the barrier. The light didn’t just block it; it digested it. The violet hue deepened, pulsing with a strength that felt less like a desperate emergency patch and more like a natural function.

Maren stepped back from Elara’s side, releasing her grip on the young scholar’s shoulder. Her hands were now free, resting lightly at her hips, but her posture remained coiled, ready to intervene if the frequency wavered even an inch. She looked at Torin, who stood beside Kaelen with a hand resting on the young man’s back. Torin’s grip was light, not restraining, but grounding—a silent reassurance that Kaelen was not alone in this control room of flesh and brass.

Kaelen had stopped shivering entirely. The gauntlet on his wrist was warm now, the cold dead weight replaced by a comforting heat that felt less like a brand and more like a hearth fire. He could feel the pull of the Blight, but it was no longer a siren song; it was a current he could ride, a river he could navigate without drowning. His eyes were open now, fixed on the barrier with a calm intensity that surprised Elias.

“Torin,” Kaelen said, his voice low and steady. “The noise in my head… it’s not screaming anymore. It’s just… talking.”

Torin didn’t look away from the shrine. “Talking is better than screaming, lad.”

“Exactly,” Kaelen added, a faint smile touching his lips. “It’s telling me where the pressure is building, where the flow is thin. It’s not trying to take me over; it’s asking me to direct it.”

Elias turned fully toward them now, meeting their gazes one by one. He saw the shift in Maren’s stance, the relaxation in Torin’s shoulders, the way Elara’s breathing had synchronized with the resonance. They were no longer a group of survivors clinging to a lifeline; they were a crew operating a machine they had built together.

“The barrier is stable,” Elias said, his voice carrying easily over the hum. “But we don’t stay here forever. The reservoir beneath us is vast. If we hold this flow for too long without increasing the pitch, the intake will slow again. We need to push higher.”

He looked at Elara. “How much longer can we maintain this angle?”

“Five minutes,” she said quickly. “Maybe six. After that, the stone’s resonance will begin to fade unless we add more force.”

Maren cracked her knuckles. “Then we prepare for the next surge. I’ll tighten my stance on Elara’s shoulders. Torin, keep Kaelen anchored. If the pull spikes, you break the focus immediately.”

Torin nodded, his hand tightening slightly on Kaelen’s back. “Understood.”

Kaelen looked down at the gauntlet. The runes etched into the brass were glowing faintly again, not with a sickly green, but with a soft, violet light that matched the barrier. He flexed his fingers, feeling the warmth spread through his arm. It wasn’t fear anymore; it was power, controlled and precise.

“Ready,” Kaelen said.

“Ready,” Torin echoed.

Elara adjusted her grip on the chisel, her eyes narrowing as she focused on the angle of contact. “I’m ready.”

Maren took a deep breath, her chest rising and falling in a rhythm that matched the hum. She was no longer trembling; she was braced.

Elias looked at the barrier one last time. The violet light seemed to pulse outward, touching the edges of the clearing, pushing back the lingering fog until it stood like a wall of gray smoke behind them. It was a victory, but not the final one. They had stabilized the flow, they had proven the system could work, and now they needed to finish what they started.

“The next phase,” Elias said, turning his attention back to the shrine. “We increase the pitch. We open the channel wider. But only as much as the barrier can handle without shattering.”

He looked at each of them, seeing the resolve in their eyes. They were tired, yes. They were afraid, perhaps. But they were together, and they had a plan that worked.

“Elara,” he said softly. “Keep the angle steady. Maren, hold her firm. Torin, watch Kaelen. I’ll monitor the barrier.”

They moved as one, a single organism tuned to a single frequency. The hum grew louder, filling the clearing, vibrating in their bones, in their teeth, in their hearts. It was the sound of a machine waking up, and they were its operators.

The barrier pulsed brighter, stronger, consuming the Blight with a hunger that felt like salvation rather than destruction. They were feeding it. And soon, it would be full.

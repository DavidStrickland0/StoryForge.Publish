The first light of dawn did not break over the valley; it leaked through the cracks in the world, filtering down from a sky that had already decided whether the Blight would be with them or against them. The air was thin and cold, carrying the scent of damp moss and woodsmoke from the distant campfires of the night before.

Elias Thorne stood at the front of the caravan line, his hands resting on the pommel of his sword as he scanned the horizon. Beside him, Torin adjusted the reins of a sturdy draft horse that pulled a wagon loaded with barrels and crates. Maren walked slightly ahead, her eyes scanning the treeline where the road dipped into shadowed ravines. She moved with a predator's economy of motion, her boot heels finding purchase on the slick cobblestones without slipping.

The caravan itself was a mixed fleet of supplies. Behind Elias’s lead wagon came Silas Vane’s merchant wagons, heavy and bricked up with canvas awnings that flapped softly in the morning breeze. The air smelled of fresh hay, unwashed bodies, and the distinct, metallic tang of the Blight residue still clinging to Elias’s bandages. In the center of the convoy sat the sealed crate from Temple Springs. It was a simple wooden box, reinforced with iron bands and wrapped in layers of wax-sealed parchment that promised stability. To the uninitiated, it was merely cargo. To Elias, it was the anchor of their mission, the very thing that drew the monsters into the open.

“Formation established,” Torin called out, his voice cutting through the quiet hum of the horses’ breathing. He wasn’t shouting; he had learned that shouting wasted breath and alerted prey. “Elias at point. Maren sweep rear and flanks. Silas, keep your wagons tight. No one strays from the perimeter.”

Silas Vane emerged from the caboose of his lead wagon, wiping condensation from a brass telescope that he had clearly not bothered to clean since the night before. He looked less like a merchant and more like a captain surveying a fleet. “The road ahead is clear for another mile,” Silas reported, lowering the glass. “But the terrain changes after the ridge. We’ll need to switch to narrow-gauge wagons once we hit the gravel.”

Elias nodded, his gaze fixed on the tree line where the light was still struggling to penetrate the canopy. “Keep your eyes open,” he said quietly. “The water is calling something out there.”

Maren stopped dead in her tracks, her hand tightening on the hilt of a short spear that she carried tucked under her arm. She didn’t speak; she simply pointed toward a patch of ferns near the road’s edge where the grass was trampled and broken. The disturbance was recent, but not from horse hooves.

“Torin,” Elias said, his voice dropping an octave as he felt the shift in pressure. “Check the rear.”

Torin spun around, his eyes darting to the wagons behind them. He pulled a small lantern from his belt and held it high, scanning the shadows under the merchant carts. Maren moved first, stepping silently to the left flank, her spear poised like a dagger ready to be thrown. Elias followed, moving with a deliberate caution that betrayed the fatigue in his legs but not the steel in his spine.

The crate sat on its trailer, wrapped in layers of waxed cloth and sealed with symbols that Elara had drawn from memory during the night. The iron bands were cold to the touch, radiating a faint hum that Elias could feel in his teeth. It was not magic; it was physics, but a kind of physics that obeyed laws older than mathematics. The water inside was pure, yes, but it was also a beacon.

A low growl echoed through the valley, vibrating in their chests like a second heartbeat. It came from the trees to the left, where the ferns had been crushed not by weight, but by something that dragged itself forward on all fours. The creature that emerged was small, barely two feet tall, its skin mottled with patches of green and black that shifted when looked at directly. Its limbs were spindly, ending in claws that dug into the earth as it skittered toward the caravan.

“Blight-Touched,” Elias muttered, raising his shield. “First contact.”

The creature lunged, its mouth opening to reveal rows of needle-sharp teeth that dripped with a viscous fluid. Maren was faster than anyone had expected, her spear darting forward to impale the beast’s shoulder before it could reach the caravan. The impact sent a spray of green ichor across the road, and the creature shrieked—a sound that was half-organic, half-mechanical, like a child screaming through a broken wind-up toy.

“Torin, flank right!” Elias shouted, parrying a second blow from another Blight-Touched that had emerged from the brush on the opposite side. His sword met the creature’s claw with a clang of steel on bone, and he stepped back, using the momentum to pivot and deliver a clean thrust into its chest cavity. The creature collapsed, writhing before dissolving into a cloud of spores that drifted upward like smoke.

“Hold!” Torin yelled, swinging his greatsword in a wide arc that cleaved through the leg of a third attacker. “Maren, cover the rear!”

Maren obeyed without hesitation, her spear finding purchase in the throat of a fourth creature that had tried to slip past Silas’s wagons. She didn’t waste time on follow-up strikes; she moved with the efficiency of a woman who knew that every second counted. The Blight-Touched were fast, yes, but they were also predictable. They moved toward the source of the water, drawn by an instinct that overrode their fear.

“Elara’s math,” Elias thought, seeing the pattern emerge as he parried another blow. “They’re coming in waves, timed to the surge.”

The caravan was a tight formation, designed to maximize defense and minimize exposure. Elias led at the front, acting as a shield against the creatures’ initial charge. Torin covered the right flank, his broadsword creating a wall of steel that kept the monsters at bay. Maren guarded the rear, her spear darting forward like a needle stitching up wounds. Silas Vane stood behind his wagons, his own weapon—a heavy mace that he had claimed from a previous adventure—crushing skulls with every swing.

The first wave lasted only minutes, but it was enough to test their resolve. The creatures were not mindless animals; they were drawn by the water, and their aggression was fueled by hunger. Elias felt the weight of the crate beneath his feet, the vibration of the Blight’s presence pulsing through the ground. He could almost feel the surge coming, a pressure building in the air like a storm front approaching.

“Hold steady,” Elias said, his voice steady despite the chaos around him. “They’re coming again.”

The second wave was worse than the first. More creatures poured from the trees, their movements synchronized as if they were following a single command. Elias ducked under a blow that would have cleaved his head in half, rolling to the side and slashing at the creature’s knee joint. Torin roared as he swung his sword again, this time catching a second attacker on the edge of his blade. Maren moved with practiced grace, her spear finding purchase in the throat of yet another beast before it could reach the caravan.

“Torin!” Elias shouted, seeing the first sign of fatigue in the other man’s stance. “Switch to the iron-bound rope!”

Torin nodded, pulling a coil of thick cable from his pack and lashing it around the legs of one of the wagons. The creature that had been trying to climb over the wagon was caught on the rope, its claws tearing at the hemp as Elias stepped forward and drove his sword into its chest.

“Silas!” Elias called out. “Get your men to secure the wagons!”

Silas Vane nodded, shouting orders in a voice that carried easily over the din of battle. His men were already moving, securing the canvas awnings and reinforcing the wagon wheels with iron shims. The caravan was not just a group of people; it was a machine designed to move through danger without breaking apart.

The third wave was coming faster now, driven by the surge that Elara had predicted. The Blight-Touched were no longer skittering; they were sprinting, their limbs elongated and twisted as if they were being pulled by an invisible force. Elias could feel the pressure building in the air, a static charge that made his hair stand on end. He knew what was coming next: a siphon effect that would pull the creatures toward them like a magnet.

“Torin,” Elias said, his voice calm despite the chaos around him. “We need to move the crate away from the wagon.”

Torin nodded, pulling the iron-bound rope taut and lashing it around the crate itself. The water inside was humming now, a sound that was almost audible if one listened closely enough. It was the sound of power, of energy stored in the form of liquid.

“Maren,” Elias said, turning to her. “Cover the rear.”

Maren nodded, stepping back and positioning herself behind the wagons, her spear ready to strike any creature that tried to slip past Silas’s men. She didn’t need to say anything; they all knew what was coming next. The siphon effect would pull the creatures toward them, but if they held their ground, they could fight back.

“Elias,” Torin said, his voice low and urgent. “The crate is unstable.”

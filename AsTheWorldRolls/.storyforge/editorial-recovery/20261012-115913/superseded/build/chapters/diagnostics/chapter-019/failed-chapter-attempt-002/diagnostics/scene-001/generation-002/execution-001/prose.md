The heavy oak doors of the Gilded Tankard groaned shut behind them, severing the warm, noisy hum of the tavern from the crisp, biting air of the valley road. The transition was immediate: the scent of stale ale and roasted meat vanished, replaced by the sharp tang of dry dust, unwashed horses, and the metallic promise of rain that always seemed to hover just out of reach in this part of the world.

Elias stepped onto the cobbles with a careful gait, his side throb quietly but steadily beneath his leather coat. He didn’t limp; he merely adjusted his weight, a habit born of long days on the trail. Beside him, Torin moved with practiced efficiency, checking the straps on Maren’s shoulder harness and ensuring the iron-bound rope was coiled securely against the wagon wheel. Maren herself walked ahead, her eyes scanning the road for loose stones or signs of fresh travel, her hands tucked into the pockets of a coat that had seen better decades but remained undeniably functional.

Silas Vane brought up the rear, his merchant’s smile replaced by a tight-lipped grimace that didn’t quite reach his eyes. He clutched his ledger to his chest as if it were both a shield and a burden. The caravan behind them was already moving, the wheels of four heavy wagons rutturing over the uneven stones with the rhythmic clatter of commerce in motion. In the center of the formation, suspended between two guard wagons by an intricate system of pulleys and chains, hung the crate from Temple Springs. It was a simple, unassuming box of dark ironwood, sealed with wax that shimmered with an odd, internal luminescence even in the gloom of the overcast morning. No one had dared open it since loading; no one knew what lay within but the guards, and they treated its silence like the weight of a crown.

“Keep your eyes on the road,” Silas said, his voice carrying just above the din of the wagons’ wheels. “And keep your hands off the crate unless I signal.”

Elias nodded once, then turned to Torin. “Maren’s right about the rope,” Elias said quietly, gesturing toward the thick, dark cables reinforced with wire that now wrapped the crate’s suspension lines like a second skin. “But if something gets too close, we need to be ready to move fast.”

Torin adjusted his grip on the reins of the lead horse. “I’ve mapped the route in three different ways,” he replied, glancing back over his shoulder at Elias. “There’s a narrow pass ahead where the road splits into two. One is shorter but steeper; the other is longer but wider. If we hit a surge, we can shift to the wider one quickly.”

“Good,” Elias said, watching the horizon for any sign of movement that didn’t belong to the caravan itself. “But if the Blight pulls us off course, we don’t argue about which way to go. We trust Maren’s gut and Elara’s math.”

Maren glanced over her shoulder without breaking stride. “I’ll mark the path as I see it,” she said simply. “If the road changes, the map changes too.”

Elias didn’t respond immediately. He watched Silas fall into step beside him, the merchant’s posture still rigid with unease. “You don’t have to carry that ledger like a bombshell,” Elias said after a moment. “We’re not going to steal your supplies or sell you out.”

Silas let out a short laugh that sounded more like a cough. “I know that,” he admitted, though his grip on the book didn’t loosen. “It’s just… the water’s never been delivered before under these conditions. And now it’s worth more than gold.”

“Worth what?” Elias asked, keeping his voice level.

Silas hesitated, then lowered his voice. “Enough to buy a small estate in the capital. Enough to fund an expedition against the Blight-Touched themselves. But also enough to make someone very angry if it’s lost.”

Elias nodded slowly. “Then we’ll keep it intact.” He turned slightly toward the wagon driver ahead, signaling for a brief pause as they approached a fork in the road where two paths diverged into shadowed brush. Torin reined in the horses, letting the wagons settle with a soft rumble before dismounting to inspect the ground.

“Both paths look stable,” Torin reported after a few moments. “But the left one has loose gravel near the edge. If we take that, we might lose traction if the road gets muddy.”

“Take the right,” Maren said decisively, stepping forward to examine the surface herself. Her boots crunched over stones with a precision that suggested she’d walked this exact spot a dozen times in her mind’s eye. “The left path is safer for wagons, but the right one gives us better visibility. If something comes from the woods, we’ll see it coming.”

Elias watched her work, noting how she didn’t just look at the road but read the way light caught on twigs and moss, checking for signs of recent disturbance or animal tracks that might indicate a predator’s presence. “Agreed,” he said finally. “We’ll stick to Maren’s call on the path.”

As they moved forward again, the caravan fell into a steady rhythm: wheels grinding over stones, horses snorting steam into the cold air, and the occasional shout from a guard checking the perimeter. Elias rode alongside Silas, who had decided against joining the front line but insisted on staying close to the crate’s protective detail.

“You know,” Silas said after a while, breaking the silence with a nervous laugh, “I’ve heard stories about this water. They say it heals wounds that refuse to scar. They say it keeps men young enough to ride forever. But I’ve never believed in miracles.”

“Belief doesn’t matter,” Elias replied, keeping his eyes on the road ahead. “What matters is whether we can deliver it without losing it.”

Silas chuckled again, though there was no humor in the sound. “That’s true. That’s very true. But still…” He trailed off, shaking his head as if trying to dismiss a thought he couldn’t quite name. “It’s just strange, isn’t it? Something so simple—just water—can be worth so much.”

Elias didn’t answer directly. Instead, he glanced toward the crate hanging between the wagons, its dark surface gleaming faintly in the weak light. “Simple things have power,” he said softly. “We just don’t always see it until it’s too late to ignore.”

The road stretched ahead of them, winding deeper into the valley where shadows lengthened and the air grew heavier with the scent of damp earth and distant storms. Somewhere beyond the tree line, something moved in the brush—a twig snapped, a low growl echoed through the trees—but neither Elias nor Torin reacted immediately. They waited, watching the edges of the road for any sign of movement that didn’t belong to the caravan itself.

Maren remained ahead, her boots crunching over stones with a precision that suggested she’d walked this exact spot a dozen times in her mind’s eye. “Keep moving,” she called back without turning around. “If we stop now, we give them time to circle us.”

Elias nodded once, then turned Silas toward the rear of the caravan where Elara had taken up position with Kaelen, monitoring the crate’s seal and preparing purification reagents in case the Blight breached their defenses. “Elara’s ready,” Elias said quietly. “Torin’s got the road covered. We’re not alone out here.”

Silas relaxed slightly, though his grip on the ledger didn’t loosen. “Good. Good. Then let’s keep moving. Let’s get this over with before something happens that we can’t fix.”

They rode forward together, a unit bound by shared purpose and wary competence. The road ahead was still dangerous, the Blight-Touched still hunting them, but they had the plan, the supplies, and the trust of each other. They were ready to face whatever came next.

The torrent of green light did not stop abruptly; it simply ceased to be necessary. As if a dam had finally collapsed upstream, the flow swelled one last time, a final rush of pressure that made the valley floor tremble under their boots. Then, with a sudden, silent snap, the stream vanished.

There was no explosion, no shrieking wind, only the quiet *thwack* of a bubble bursting in water. The fissure in the shrine's base darkened, the green glow retracting into the stone like a wound closing. The air, heavy with the scent of ozone and rot for what felt like hours, cleared instantly. It smelled now of damp earth, crushed pine needles, and the cold, clean scent of rain on hot asphalt—the smell of a world that had not just been saved, but restored.

Elara was the first to move. She didn't cheer; she didn't look at her companions with wide, relieved eyes. She dropped her chisel into her satchel and knelt before the fissure, peering into the darkened hole with a magnifying lens she’d brought for inspecting structural integrity rather than magical resonance. Her brow was furrowed, not in fear, but in calculation.

"The harmonic is gone," she said, her voice tight. She held up the small glass prism she used to analyze light frequency. "No longer singing. No longer resonating. The conduit has collapsed."

Kaelen exhaled, a long, shuddering sound that seemed to deflate his entire frame. He lowered his arms from their tense posture at his sides. The brass gauntlet on his left wrist, which had remained cold and inert throughout the operation, now sat heavy and silent against his skin. The pull—the agonizing, magnetic drag that had kept him anchored to the shrine floor for nearly an hour—was gone. He felt light-headed, his knees knocking together as the adrenaline drained from his system.

"It’s done," Elias said softly. He walked over, careful not to touch the fissure, and placed a hand on Kaelen's shoulder. The young man leaned into the touch, his eyes fluttering open for a moment before sliding shut again. "The core is consumed. The reservoir beneath us... the source feeding the Blight... it’s empty."

Elias looked up at the barrier that had stretched across the valley floor like a taut membrane. It was still there, shimmering faintly in the twilight, but it had changed. The violet hue had faded to a pale, indigo mist, almost transparent now. Where once it had been a wall of living light, pulsing and breathing with the hunger of a predator, it was now merely a shadow against the sky—a scar that was already knitting itself shut.

"It’s retracted," Maren said, stepping forward from her position near Torin. She looked at the barrier with a mixture of awe and disbelief. Her hammer was still slung over her shoulder, but she hadn’t touched it since they’d removed the physical blockage an hour ago. "It’s... it’s dormant."

"Exactly," Elias said. He turned to look at the shrine itself. The stone had cooled, the heat radiating from the fissure dissipated into the cooling air. The black basalt looked less like a wound in reality and more like a grave marker. "The machine is off."

Kaelen nodded slowly, his face pale but steady. He looked down at his wrist, tracing the line of the gauntlet with a trembling finger. "It didn’t hurt anymore," he murmured. "Not really. Just... pressure. Like holding your breath underwater too long. And now I can breathe again."

"Good," Elara said, standing up and brushing dust from her knees. She was already checking the quartz shards she’d collected earlier, sorting through them with a clinical eye. Most were shattered beyond use, but a few larger fragments still glowed faintly, their internal structure intact enough to be cataloged or repurposed later. "We’ve confirmed the local threat is neutralized. The core has been drained. Without that source, the Blight cannot sustain itself here."

Elias walked to the edge of the clearing, looking out over the valley floor. The fog was gone, replaced by a crisp, cool air that made his lungs feel clean for the first time in weeks. Below them, the city sprawled like a sleeping beast, its towers and spires visible in the dim light. They could see the streetlamps flickering on, one by one, as if waking from a nightmare. The drainage ditches were dark and still, no longer glowing with the sickly green of corruption.

"They’ll think we’re heroes," Kaelen said quietly. He shifted his weight, wincing as a dull ache settled in his chest—the residual cost of holding back a hurricane for an hour. "Or... they’ll ask questions."

"Let them ask," Elias said. "We have answers, and we have work to do before the sun rises. But first, rest. Maren, Torin—check the perimeter again. Make sure nothing’s stirred in the dark while we were busy feeding the beast."

Maren nodded, already moving toward the ridge where they’d been positioned earlier. Torin followed close behind, his sword still drawn but relaxed now that the immediate danger had passed. They didn’t speak; their silence was a language of its own—a shared understanding that the hardest part was over, but the work wasn’t done.

Elara packed away her tools, leaving only the larger quartz fragments in her satchel for later study. "I’ll document the resonance patterns," she said, her voice practical despite the relief washing over them all. "For the guild’s records, if anything else comes of this."

Elias glanced at Kaelen. The young man was still leaning against Torin, who had offered him a steadying hand without comment. Elias knew what that ache meant—it wasn’t just fatigue; it was the price of controlling something that shouldn’t be controlled. But Kaelen hadn’t flinched, hadn’t complained. He’d held the line when they needed him to, and now he was ready to move on.

"You did well," Elias said. It wasn’t a grand declaration; it was simple, honest praise. "You regulated the flow better than I expected."

Kaelen smiled faintly, a tired expression that reached his eyes. "I listened," he said. "And I learned when to stop pushing."

"Good enough," Elara muttered, though there was no bite in her words. She looked at the fissure one last time, then at the barrier retreating into the shadows of the shrine. "We’re done here. Let’s get back to camp before the night gets colder."

They began to move, stepping carefully over the cooling stone and the darkened fissure. The air was quiet now, save for the distant hum of the city waking up around them. No more screams, no more hissing vents, no more desperate attempts to hold back a tide that had finally receded.

As they walked, Elias felt a strange sense of peace settle over him. They hadn’t destroyed the Blight—they had fed it until it ran out of food, until it could no longer sustain itself. It wasn’t victory in the traditional sense; it was containment through understanding. And for all its strangeness, that felt like progress.

Behind them, the barrier faded completely, leaving only the cold stone of the shrine and the memory of what they had done. The valley was safe again.

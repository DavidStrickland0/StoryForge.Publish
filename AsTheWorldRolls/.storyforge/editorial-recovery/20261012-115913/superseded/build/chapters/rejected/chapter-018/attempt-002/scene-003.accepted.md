Torin was already moving before the last echo of their agreement had faded into the tavern’s ambient roar. He didn’t announce his departure; he simply stood, his joints cracking softly, and walked toward the door. The heavy oak swung open, letting in a gust of cool evening air that smelled of dust and pine, and for a moment he paused on the threshold, listening. The street outside was quiet, save for the distant clatter of a cart wheel and the occasional bark of a dog.

He stepped out, and the cold hit him like a splash of water. It was good. It was necessary. The warmth of the booth was safe, but safety was a luxury they couldn’t afford if they wanted to be ready by dawn. He needed to think with his hands, not just his head. Strategy was Elara’s domain; timing was Kaelen’s. Logistics were his.

Torin walked briskly toward the market square, his boots striking the cobblestones with a rhythmic cadence. The shops were closing, their shutters being drawn down with heavy, final thuds. The day’s commerce had ended, but the night market was just beginning to stir. Stalls were being set up, canvas awnings being unfurled, lanterns being lit. It was a different kind of market than the one they had seen during the day—less about volume, more about necessity.

He found what he needed within the first few minutes. A general goods merchant, a man with arms like hams and a face that suggested he had never smiled in his life, was unloading a crate of rope and cordage. Torin didn’t haggle. He simply pointed to a coil of thick, braided hemp, strong enough to hold a man but light enough for a pack. He took two lengths. Next, a bundle of flint and steel, the flint dark and smooth, the steel sharp and ready. He needed fire. Always fire.

He moved on, his mind compiling a list as he walked. Food that wouldn’t spoil. Water purification salts, if they were available. A spare set of undergarments for each of them, because wet wool was a killer in the mountains. A small kit of bandages, clean and sterile. He bought them all, paying without blinking. The coin left his purse, a small loss for a large gain.

As he turned to leave the stall, he almost collided with Maren. She had followed him out, moving with the quiet efficiency of a predator, her eyes scanning the crowd. She didn’t say anything at first, just watched him finish his purchase. When he turned, she was there, leaning against the wooden frame of the stall, her arms crossed.

“You didn’t wait,” she said. It wasn’t an accusation. It was an observation.

“I didn’t think you’d want to watch me buy rope,” Torin replied, tossing a length onto his back. “It’s not exactly a negotiation.”

Maren pushed off the frame and fell into step beside him. “It’s about trust,” she said. “Elias trusts Elara with the map. Kaelen trusts the gauntlet. You trust your list. I need to know what’s in it before we commit to it.”

Torin looked at her, surprised by the directness. He had expected friction, perhaps, or a demand to lead. Instead, he got a partner. He smiled, a rare, genuine expression that softened the lines around his mouth.

“Fair enough,” he said. “What do you need?”

Maren didn’t answer immediately. She stopped at a weapons merchant’s stall, the owner a thin man with nervous hands who was polishing a dagger with a rag. Maren didn’t look at the dagger. She looked at the rack of whetstones behind the counter.

“Those,” she said, pointing. “The medium grit. And the leather strips for the handles. My grip is slipping in the humidity.”

The merchant blinked, then pulled the stones from the rack. “For your gauntlet?”

“For my hands,” Maren corrected. “The metal doesn’t help if the leather rots.”

Torin watched the transaction, noting the way Maren tested the edge of the whetstone against her thumb, feeling for nicks, checking the density. It was a practical, grounded assessment. She wasn’t buying a weapon; she was maintaining a tool. It was a small thing, but it told him everything he needed to know about her readiness.

When they moved on, Torin felt a shift in the air between them. The tension of the evening, the weight of the decision to leave, had settled into something more manageable. It was a shared burden, distributed evenly.

They walked in silence for a while, their footsteps syncing without either of them realizing it. The night market was bustling now, the air thick with the smell of roasting meat and spiced wine. Torin bought a loaf of dense bread, wrapped in oilcloth, and a jar of honey. Maren bought a small vial of salve for her aching knees.

At the edge of the market, near the city gates, they stopped. The gate was massive, iron-banded wood, guarded by two soldiers who looked more bored than vigilant. Beyond the gate, the road stretched out into the darkness, a ribbon of moonlight cutting through the trees.

Torin looked at the road, then at Maren. “We have what we need,” he said.

Maren nodded. “The bread is good. The rope is strong. The whetstone is sharp.” She paused. “It’s enough.”

Torin turned back to the street, the noise of the tavern already a distant memory. They had a plan. They had a schedule. They had each other. The rest was just walking.

He started back toward the Gilded Tankard, Maren falling into step beside him, the sound of their boots on the cobblestones providing a steady, reassuring rhythm. The night was cool, the air crisp, and for the first time in days, the future didn’t look like a threat. It looked like a destination.

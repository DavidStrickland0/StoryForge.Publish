The tavern's din faded into the background, yielding only to the scratch of a quill and the low, gravelly murmur of negotiation. Silas Vane had not actually vacated the table; instead, he had positioned himself with the casual ease of a man who owned the room. He sat on the edge of the adjacent booth, legs crossed, his silver-threaded robes catching the candlelight. Before him lay a sheaf of parchment, thick and heavy, bound with a strip of green leather.

Elias stared at the document. It was a contract. Specifically, it was a *very* specific contract.

"Let's look at this line by line," Elias said, his voice flat. He tapped the parchment with a fingernail, not quite touching it. "You call this a 'comprehensive escort and intelligence package.' That's a lot of adjectives for what amounts to us walking behind a wagon."

Silas smiled, an expression that suggested he was enjoying the friction. "And yet, the wagon contains goods that could heal a battalion, Elias. The 'intelligence' portion is where the value lies. You have the sword; I have the map. Together, we are a force of nature."

Maren, who had been leaning back in her chair with her arms folded, uncrossed them. She leaned forward, resting her elbows on the table, her eyes fixed on Silas with the intensity of a hawk spotting a field mouse. "You're trying to add a second merchant group," she stated. It wasn't a question.

Silas raised an eyebrow. "I'm attempting to optimize the route, Maren. If we can secure the protection of a second caravan traveling the same corridor, we can split the cost of the 'premium' escort services. It's good business."

"It's bad business if we get killed," Maren countered, her voice devoid of inflection. "Two caravans means double the targets. Double the chances of a Blight-Touched entity deciding our little procession is a buffet. And you want to put that in the contract? As a 'liability mitigation clause'?"

Elias looked at the clause in question. It was buried in the fine print, written in a script so small it looked like a swarm of angry ants. *Party agrees to maintain escort integrity for all associated commercial interests within a fifty-yard radius...*

"Fifty yards," Elias repeated, feeling a headache beginning to throb behind his eyes. "Vane, if we are escorting *your* caravan, we are not also babysitting some other merchant's oxen. That's not an escort; that's a circus."

Silas waved a hand dismissively. "It's a standard industry practice for high-value goods. You're being overly literal. It's a 'zone of influence.'"

"It's a trap," Maren said.

Elias turned to Silas, his expression hardening. "Here is what happens. We sign for the primary caravan escort only. The payment structure is as we discussed: fifty percent upfront, fifty percent upon safe delivery. The 'zone of influence' clause is struck. If another merchant wants protection, they can hire their own guards. They can even hire you, if you feel so inclined, but you don't get to attach their liability to our neck."

Silas's smile faltered, just for a second, before he smoothed it back into place. He took a sip of his ale, though the liquid remained untouched in the glass. "You're difficult, Thorne. I respect it. But I'm a man of contracts. I can't just strike a clause. It sets a precedent."

"Then let's set a different precedent," Elias said. He reached into his coat and withdrew a small pouch of coins. He didn't place them on the table; he simply held them up, letting the metal clink softly in the silence that had fallen around their booth. "This is a deposit. Not for the contract, but for the map. You said you had detailed route intelligence. That intelligence is valuable. I'm buying it. Separately. If you want to sell us the escort, we'll negotiate that based on the primary caravan only. If you want to sell the map, you take the coin."

Silas's eyes flickered to the pouch, then back to Elias's face. The calculation was visible, rapid and precise, behind his merchant's mask. He was weighing the immediate liquidity of the coin against the long-term potential of a difficult client.

"And if I refuse?" Silas asked, his voice dropping an octave.

"Then you keep your map, and I keep my coin. And you'll have to find another way to sell that 'pure' water if you don't have a reliable escort. Word travels fast in this city, Vane. Especially about merchants who try to tie adventurers down with fine print while they're still bleeding from the last job."

Maren gave a low chuckle, the sound dry and sharp. "He's bluffing," she said to Silas, though her hand rested casually on the hilt of her hammer. "But he's not wrong about the fine print. It's ugly. And I hate ugly things."

Silas stared at Elias for a long moment. The tavern noise seemed to swell again, the clatter of plates and the roar of conversation pressing in, but the air between the three of them remained suspended, brittle and sharp.

Finally, Silas reached out and tapped the parchment with a single finger. "The map," he said. "You want the map."

"I want the route," Elias corrected. "And I want it to be accurate. No 'zone of influence' nonsense. Just the path, the dangers, and the landmarks."

Silas picked up the sheaf of parchment. He didn't strike the clause; he didn't need to. He simply tore the page containing the 'associated commercial interests' section from the rest of the document. The sound of the tearing paper was loud in the quiet booth. He slid the remaining pages, the ones detailing the primary escort and the map's location, across the table.

"Take the map," Silas said, his smile returning, though it was thinner now, more cautious. "I'll have the primary escort contract ready for review tomorrow morning. But Elias?"

Elias took the map, his fingers brushing against the parchment. It was heavy, high-quality vellum. "Yes?"

"If that map leads us into a Blight-Touched nest, I'm billing you for the cleanup."

Elias looked at Maren, who raised an eyebrow, then back at Silas. "Deal," Elias said.

He took the map. The transaction was complete, clean, and devoid of the messy entanglements Silas had tried to weave in. The party had secured the intelligence they needed without surrendering their autonomy or their sanity. It was a small victory, but in the world of merchants and adventurers, small victories were the only ones that lasted.

Silas stood up, brushing a speck of dust from his sleeve. He looked at the group, his gaze lingering on Kaelen, who had been silent throughout, studying the table's grain as if it held secrets.

"You're an interesting group," Silas said, his tone light, almost friendly. "Don't let the Blight get you before I can collect my other half."

With a nod to Maren and a sharp glance at Elias, he turned and disappeared into the crowd, his silver robes swirling around him like a phantom.

Elias sat back, exhaling a breath he didn't realize he had been holding. He unfolded the map on the table. It was detailed, marked with red lines and symbols that Elara, sitting quietly beside him, was already leaning over to inspect.

"Good work," Maren said, her voice soft. She didn't smile, but the tension in her shoulders had eased. "He wanted to bleed us. You stopped him."

Elias looked at the map, then at his companions. The threat was still out there, the Blight still prowling the edges of the city, but for now, they had a path. And they had the time to study it.

The vellum was heavier than expected, its surface textured with a grain that felt almost organic under Elias's fingertips. He smoothed it flat across the scarred wood of the table, the edges curling slightly despite the weight of the coins he'd placed on the corners. The ink was dark, precise, and smelled faintly of iron and dried rosemary—a scent that suggested Silas Vane's cartographers were either very old or very meticulous, possibly both.

Elara leaned over the map, her chin resting on her folded hands. She didn't speak immediately. Instead, her eyes tracked the red lines that denoted the primary caravan routes, moving from the Temple Springs in the east to the market square in the city center. Her gaze was quiet, analytical, dissecting the geography before she began to interpret it.

"The scale is accurate," she said finally, her voice dropping low. "But the annotations are... aggressive."

Elias looked up. "Aggressive?"

Elara tapped a cluster of symbols near the eastern ridge. They weren't simple warning marks; they were intricate, spiraling shapes that seemed to shift in the candlelight. "These are Blight resonance markers. Not just sightings, but measured intensity. Silas didn't just map where the creatures are; he mapped how they move."

Kaelen, who had been resting his head against the booth's backrest, opened one eye. "How do they move?"

Elara traced a line with a slender finger, following the flow of the river that bisected the valley. "The Blight isn't random. It's tidal. Look here." She pointed to a series of concentric circles drawn around a dried-up lake bed. "The activity peaks at high tide, which in this case corresponds to the moon's position relative to the local ley lines. It's cyclical. If we travel during the low tide windows, the creatures are dormant, or at least sluggish. But if we cross the ridge at peak resonance, we won't just be fighting monsters. We'll be fighting the environment itself."

Elias frowned, studying the markings. "So the danger isn't just the distance. It's the timing."

"Exactly," Elara said. She extracted a small notebook from her satchel and flipped to a blank page. "I need to calculate the specific windows. The moon is in its fourth quarter tonight. If I'm reading these resonance charts correctly, we have a six-hour window of relative calm before the next surge. After that, the ridge becomes impassable without heavy magical shielding, which we don't possess."

Kaelen sat up, the gauntlet on his wrist pulsing with a faint, rhythmic light. He didn't look at the map; he looked at Elara. "You're sure about this? The gauntlet reacts to Blight energy. If you're right, I should be feeling a pull right now."

Elara glanced at his wrist. "The gauntlet is a sensor, Kaelen. It tells you the strength of the field, not the direction of the flow. I'm reading the flow." She turned back to the map, her expression focused. "The symbols here indicate a convergence point. If we stay east of the river during the peak, we're safe. If we cross, we're dead."

Elias looked at the map, then at Elara. There was a new quality to her presence in the room. Earlier, she had been the observer, the one who recorded. Now, she was the architect. The map was no longer just a piece of paper; it was a puzzle, and she was solving it in real-time.

"Can you give us a schedule?" Elias asked. "Specific times, specific stops."

Elara nodded, already writing. "We leave at dawn. We have to be at the first waypoint by mid-morning. That's where the resonance drops off. We rest there, let the gauntlet cool, and wait for the next low tide. Then we push through the narrow pass before the surge hits." She paused, looking up at Elias. "It's tight. There's no room for error. If we're late, or if we get stuck, the Blight will catch us."

Maren, who had been silent, stirring her ale with a spoon, spoke up. "Tight is better than dead. I can handle tight."

Elias looked at Kaelen. "Can you sustain the shielding if we need it?"

Kaelen flexed his fingers, the brass plates of the gauntlet clicking softly. "I can hold a shield for a short time. But if the resonance is as high as she says, it'll drain me fast. I'd need to recharge between surges." He looked at Elara. "Your calculations are good? I don't like relying on guesswork."

Elara met his gaze. "I don't guess. I measure. And right now, the measurements say we have a path."

Elias nodded slowly. He looked at the map, tracing the route with his eyes. It was a complex dance, a choreography of movement and rest, of danger and safety. It required precision, trust, and a level of coordination that they hadn't yet fully achieved. But they were getting there.

"Elara," he said, "you're in charge of the timing. Kaelen, you're in charge of the shielding. Maren, you're in charge of the logistics. We move when I give the order, but we follow your lead."

Maren raised an eyebrow. "Logistics? I thought I was the muscle."

"You're the muscle," Elias said. "But muscle needs direction. And right now, Elara is the one who knows where the ground is solid."

Elara didn't smile, but the tension in her shoulders eased. She continued writing, her stylus scratching softly against the paper. The sound was a steady rhythm, a heartbeat in the quiet tavern.

Kaelen watched her for a moment, then looked at Elias. "She's good," he said quietly. "Maybe better than we thought."

Elias nodded. "We're learning. That's what we're supposed to do."

The map lay flat on the table, the red lines glowing faintly in the dim light. The Blight was out there, prowling the edges of the valley, but for now, they had a plan. And they had the time to execute it.

Elias rolled the map carefully, securing it with a leather strap. He tucked it into his pack, feeling the weight of the knowledge it held. It wasn't just a map; it was a promise. A promise that they could survive, that they could move, that they could win.

"Let's rest," he said. "We need to be fresh when we leave."

Maren nodded, finishing her ale. "Good idea. I'm tired of sitting."

Kaelen stood up, stretching his arms above his head. The gauntlet hummed softly, a low, reassuring sound. "I'll keep watch for a while. You two sleep."

Elara packed her notebook, her movements precise and deliberate. "I'll stay with the map," she said. "Just in case."

Elias smiled, a small, tired thing. "Of course."

They settled into their seats, the silence of the tavern wrapping around them like a blanket. The noise of the other patrons faded into the background, a distant hum that seemed to belong to another world. For a moment, they were just four people, sitting in a booth, with a map and a plan.

And for the first time in a long time, they felt like they knew where they were going.

Torin was already moving before the last echo of their agreement had faded into the tavern's ambient roar. He didn't announce his departure; he simply stood, his joints cracking softly, and walked toward the door. The heavy oak swung open, letting in a gust of cool evening air that smelled of dust and pine, and for a moment he paused on the threshold, listening. The street outside was quiet, save for the distant clatter of a cart wheel and the occasional bark of a dog.

He stepped out, and the cold hit him like a splash of water. It was good. It was necessary. The warmth of the booth was safe, but safety was a luxury they couldn't afford if they wanted to be ready by dawn. He needed to think with his hands, not just his head. Strategy was Elara's domain; timing was Kaelen's. Logistics were his.

Torin walked briskly toward the market square, his boots striking the cobblestones with a rhythmic cadence. The shops were closing, their shutters being drawn down with heavy, final thuds. The day's commerce had ended, but the night market was just beginning to stir. Stalls were being set up, canvas awnings being unfurled, lanterns being lit. It was a different kind of market than the one they had seen during the day—less about volume, more about necessity.

He found what he needed within the first few minutes. A general goods merchant, a man with arms like hams and a face that suggested he had never smiled in his life, was unloading a crate of rope and cordage. Torin didn't haggle. He simply pointed to a coil of thick, braided hemp, strong enough to hold a man but light enough for a pack. He took two lengths. Next, a bundle of flint and steel, the flint dark and smooth, the steel sharp and ready. He needed fire. Always fire.

He moved on, his mind compiling a list as he walked. Food that wouldn't spoil. Water purification salts, if they were available. A spare set of undergarments for each of them, because wet wool was a killer in the mountains. A small kit of bandages, clean and sterile. He bought them all, paying without blinking. The coin left his purse, a small loss for a large gain.

As he turned to leave the stall, he almost collided with Maren. She had followed him out, moving with the quiet efficiency of a predator, her eyes scanning the crowd. She didn't say anything at first, just watched him finish his purchase. When he turned, she was there, leaning against the wooden frame of the stall, her arms crossed.

"You didn't wait," she said. It wasn't an accusation. It was an observation.

"I didn't think you'd want to watch me buy rope," Torin replied, tossing a length onto his back. "It's not exactly a negotiation."

Maren pushed off the frame and fell into step beside him. "It's about trust," she said. "Elias trusts Elara with the map. Kaelen trusts the gauntlet. You trust your list. I need to know what's in it before we commit to it."

Torin looked at her, surprised by the directness. He had expected friction, perhaps, or a demand to lead. Instead, he got a partner. He smiled, a rare, genuine expression that softened the lines around his mouth.

"Fair enough," he said. "What do you need?"

Maren did not answer immediately. She halted before a weapons merchant's stall, where a thin man with nervous hands polished a dagger with a rag. Maren did not look at the dagger. Her gaze fixed on the rack of whetstones behind the counter.

"Those," she said, pointing. "The medium grit. And the leather strips for the handles. My grip is slipping in the humidity."

The merchant blinked, then pulled the stones from the rack. "For your gauntlet?"

"For my hands," Maren corrected. "The metal doesn't help if the leather rots."

Torin watched the transaction, noting how Maren tested the edge of the whetstone against her thumb, feeling for nicks and checking the density. It was a practical, grounded assessment. She wasn't buying a weapon; she was maintaining a tool. It was a small thing, but it told him everything he needed to know about her readiness.

When they moved on, Torin felt a shift in the air between them. The tension of the evening, the weight of the decision to leave, had settled into something more manageable. It was a shared burden, distributed evenly.

They walked in silence for a while, their footsteps syncing without either of them realizing it. The night market was bustling now, the air thick with the smell of roasting meat and spiced wine. Torin bought a loaf of dense bread, wrapped in oilcloth, and a jar of honey. Maren bought a small vial of salve for her aching knees.

At the edge of the market, near the city gates, they stopped. The gate was massive, iron-banded wood, guarded by two soldiers who looked more bored than vigilant. Beyond the gate, the road stretched out into the darkness, a ribbon of moonlight cutting through the trees.

Torin looked at the road, then at Maren. "We have what we need," he said.

Maren nodded. "The bread is good. The rope is strong. The whetstone is sharp." She paused. "It's enough."

Torin turned back to the street, the noise of the tavern already a distant memory. They had a plan. They had a schedule. They had each other. The rest was just walking.

He turned back toward the Gilded Tankard, Maren falling into step beside him; the rhythmic clatter of their boots on cobblestones offered a steady, reassuring cadence. The night air was cool and crisp, and for the first time in days, the future ceased to look like a looming threat and instead appeared as a clear destination.

The tavern’s interior was a different world from the sun-baked streets outside. The air here was thick with the scent of stale ale, roasting meat, and the faint, metallic tang of the Blight residue that still clung to Elias’s bandages. He found Elara in the corner booth, where she had claimed their table earlier in the day. She was buried in a stack of parchment sheets, her stylus moving with the precise, rhythmic scratching of a clock’s tick.

Elias slid into the seat opposite her, the wood creaking under his weight. He didn’t sit fully; he remained half-standing, leaning against the back of the chair, keeping his hand near his sword hilt. The noise of the tavern was a dull roar behind him, a constant backdrop that made private conversation difficult but necessary.

“You’re late,” Elara said, not looking up. Her voice was dry, clipped, but there was no real heat in it. She was counting on him, and she was counting the seconds.

“Market delays,” Elias replied, finally lowering himself into the chair. He winced slightly as his side throbbed, a dull ache that had become a familiar companion over the last few weeks. “Torin and Maren are finishing up outside. They’ve secured the rope and the antiseptic. It’s more than we had an hour ago.”

Elara’s stylus paused. She looked up, her dark eyes narrowing slightly. “Antiseptic? Maren found a use for sulfur paste?”

“Cheap, effective, and it smells like a dragon’s armpit,” Elias said, a ghost of a smile touching his lips. “Maren says it drives the Blight-Touched away. Or at least, it makes them hesitate long enough for us to act.”

Elara made a note in the margin of her journal, her handwriting small and neat. “Hesitation is a tactical advantage. I’ll factor it into the engagement timing.” She turned a page, revealing a complex diagram of intersecting lines and shaded areas. “But the rope and the paste are only tools. They don’t solve the core problem.”

Elias leaned forward, resting his elbows on the table. “What core problem?”

“The Blight isn’t random,” Elara said, her tone shifting to the formal cadence she used when explaining complex theories. It was a defense mechanism, Elias had learned. When she was uncertain, she recited facts. When she was certain, she dissected them. “Look at this.” She tapped the diagram. “I’ve cross-referenced the sightings from the last three months with the lunar cycles and the tidal patterns of the river below the city. There’s a correlation.”

Elias frowned, studying the lines. “The Blight surges when the moon is in the third quarter and the river is at its lowest ebb.”

“Precisely,” Elara said. “It’s not just a creature or a curse. It’s a reaction. The Blight is drawn to the instability in the seal. When the seal is weakest—during those specific celestial alignments—the pressure builds. The creatures don’t just wander in; they’re pulled in. It’s a siphon effect.”

Elias stared at the diagram, the pieces clicking into place with a satisfying click in his mind. He had known the Blight was dangerous, had known it was unpredictable, but he had been treating it as a wild animal. Elara was showing him it was a machine.

“So, if we time the escort to avoid those windows…”

“We don’t avoid them,” Elara corrected him, her voice sharp. “We exploit them. If the Blight is drawn to the seal’s weakness, then the seal itself is the anchor. If we can reinforce the seal locally, even temporarily, we can create a dead zone. A pocket of stability where the Blight cannot manifest.”

Elias looked at her, really looked at her, seeing the fatigue under her eyes and the intensity in her gaze. She wasn’t just a scholar hiding behind her books. She was a strategist, using logic as a weapon.

“You’re saying we need to move the caravan not around the danger, but through a neutral zone we create ourselves,” Elias said.

“Correct,” Elara said. “But it requires precise timing. We need to be in position at least an hour before the surge peaks. If we are late, the siphon will pull the creatures toward us, not away from us. If we are too early, the seal might not be stable enough to hold.”

Elias nodded slowly, absorbing the implication. “We have the supplies. We have the route. We just need to trust the math.”

“Math is reliable,” Elara said, closing her journal with a firm snap. “People are not. But Torin’s logistics and Maren’s… practicality… are reliable in their own way. I have confidence in the team.”

It was the closest thing to a compliment Elara had ever given him. Elias felt a small, warm swell of pride in his chest, quickly suppressed by the weight of responsibility.

“Then we finalize the schedule tonight,” Elias said, standing up. He stretched, his joints popping softly. “I’ll go check on Torin and Maren. We need to make sure everyone is on the same page. No more guessing. No more improvising.”

Elara watched him stand, her expression softening just slightly. “Be careful, Elias. The Blight is patient. And it is very, very hungry.”

Elias gave her a small nod, then turned and walked toward the back of the tavern, where the light from the windows was fading into the deepening twilight. He could hear Torin’s low voice arguing with Maren over something, likely the weight of the iron-bound rope. He smiled faintly. They were ready. Not because they were brave, but because they were prepared. And in a world that ran on magic and mystery, preparation was the only true shield.

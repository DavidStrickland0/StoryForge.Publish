The market district was a chaotic sprawl of canvas awnings, stacked crates, and the sharp tang of fresh meat mingling with the dusty heat of the cobbles. Torin moved through the crowd with the efficient, low-slung gait of a man who had spent more time in supply depots than in taverns. He was not here to haggle over the price of an apple or to marvel at a particularly shiny coin; he was here to secure the logistical skeleton of their upcoming escort.

Maren walked beside him, her hands tucked into the pockets of her leather coat, her eyes scanning the stalls with a predator’s casual indifference. She had already identified the three merchants who looked like they might try to short weight on grain and the one who was likely to overcharge for a standard-issue lantern.

“Torin,” she said, stopping abruptly in front of a stall laden with coils of rope and heavy canvas. “You’re buying the wrong rope.”

Torin frowned, looking at the thick, braided hemp he had been inspecting. “It’s rated for heavy loads. The caravan’s wagons are packed with sealed crates.”

“Crates,” Maren corrected, her tone dry. “Blight-Touched things don’t respect load ratings. They respect tension points. If one of those things latches onto the rigging, standard hemp will snap like a dry twig. You need the iron-bound cable. The stuff that smells like a blacksmith’s nightmare.”

Torin blinked, then looked again at the rope. He had been so focused on the weight of the cargo that he had neglected the nature of the threat. He shifted his gaze to the adjacent stall, where a merchant with a stained apron was uncoiling thick, dark cables reinforced with twisted metal wire.

“This is twice the price,” Torin muttered, calculating the cost in his head.

“It’s half the price of a dead horse and a full day of digging a new path,” Maren replied. She stepped up to the merchant, who immediately straightened up, sensing a customer who understood the difference between a commodity and a necessity. “I’ll take two hundred feet of the iron-bound. And the matching shackles. The ones with the double-lock mechanism.”

The merchant looked surprised, then greedy. “Aye, aye. For the lady, of course.”

Torin watched the transaction, noting how Maren didn’t raise her voice, didn’t threaten, and didn’t explain the tactical rationale. She simply stated the requirement with such absolute finality that the merchant complied without a single argument. It was a lesson in efficiency that he filed away for later.

They moved on to the apothecary’s stall, a cramped space filled with jars of dried herbs, powders, and pungent oils. Torin had a list of supplies for the water purification process, but Maren intercepted him before he could present the parchment.

“Elara is handling the chemistry,” Maren said, picking up a jar of clear, viscous liquid. “But we need something that works when the chemistry fails. We need antiseptics that don’t rely on a stable temperature. And we need something that smells bad enough to drive a Blight-Touched creature away from our skin if it gets too close.”

Torin looked at the jar. “That’s crude sulfur paste. It’s caustic.”

“It’s effective,” Maren said, dropping it into the basket. “And it’s cheap. I’ve seen creatures recoil from the smell before they even touch the source. It buys seconds. Seconds are all we need to kill them.”

As they moved through the market, a pattern emerged. Torin was concerned with the macro: the routes, the weights, the timing, the cost. Maren was concerned with the micro: the friction points, the failure modes, the specific, ugly details of how things broke under pressure.

At a stall selling oilskins and heavy boots, Torin began to negotiate the price for a dozen pairs. Maren leaned against the counter, watching the merchant try to upsell a pair of decorative boots with silver buckles.

“Tell him no,” she said to Torin, without looking up.

Torin blinked, then turned to the merchant. “I’m only interested in the standard-issue greaves and the heavy soles. No buckles. No stitching that isn’t triple-reinforced.”

The merchant, confused by the sudden shift in the buyer’s demeanor, quickly adjusted his pitch, dropping the price of the decorative items and focusing on the practical gear.

Torin realized he was learning a different kind of negotiation. It wasn’t about convincing the other person that his perspective was correct; it was about being so undeniably practical that any deviation from utility seemed absurd. Maren didn’t argue; she just removed the options that didn’t work.

As they finished their purchases, the sun had climbed higher, casting sharp shadows across the cobblestones. The market was still bustling, but the air felt heavier, charged with the anticipation of the day’s work.

Torin gathered the bundles, checking the weight of each one. He had secured the iron-bound rope, the sulfur paste, the heavy greaves, and a crate of high-grade flint. It was enough to keep them alive, if the plan held together.

Maren shouldered one of the heavier bundles, testing its balance. “You’re thinking too hard,” she said, glancing at his furrowed brow.

“I’m calculating the margin of error,” Torin said. “If the Blight acts unpredictably, the logistics could fail.”

“Logistics don’t fail,” Maren said, adjusting the strap on her shoulder. “People do. And people are easier to fix than broken ropes.”

Torin looked at her, really looked at her, and saw the quiet confidence in her eyes. It wasn’t arrogance; it was the settled assurance of someone who had seen the worst of what the world could throw at them and had found a way to stand in the middle of it.

He nodded slowly. “I’ll adjust the route plan. I’ll add more buffer time for the unpredictable variables.”

“Good,” Maren said. “And Torin?”

“Yes?”

“Next time, let me pick the rope. I know what holds.”

Torin smiled, a rare, genuine expression that softened the lines around his eyes. “Noted.”

They turned and walked back toward the tavern, the bundles heavy on their backs, the weight of their shared purpose settling comfortably between them. The market noise faded behind them, replaced by the quieter, more urgent rhythm of their own footsteps. They had the supplies. They had the plan. Now they just needed to make it work.

The corridor narrowed, the ceiling lowering until the rough-hewn stones above seemed to press down on the party’s heads. The air grew heavier here, thick with the scent of damp moss and rotting wood, a stagnant stillness that clung to their skin like a cold sweat. Torin moved with the fluid, practiced silence of a man who had spent his life moving through hostile spaces, his boots finding purchase on the uneven flagstones without a single scrape. He did not rush. Rushing was a luxury they could not afford, and more importantly, it was a mistake they could not afford to make twice.

Elias followed close behind, his hand resting lightly on the pommel of his sword, not in threat, but in readiness. His eyes scanned the shadows, tracking the subtle shifts in the light cast by Kaelen’s gauntlet. The mage’s brass plate, once a steady beacon of amber warmth, now pulsed with a faint, irregular rhythm. It was a visual heartbeat, slow and labored, a testament to the reserves he was hoarding. Every step Kaelen took, the light dimmed slightly, as if the magic itself was being siphoned away by the very act of movement through the keep’s oppressive atmosphere.

"Maren," Elias murmured, his voice barely a whisper, cutting through the heavy silence. He didn’t turn to look at her; he knew where she was. He could feel the shift in the group’s rhythm, the slight delay in her stride that had been growing with every yard they covered.

Maren grunted, a short, sharp sound that was less a reply and more an acknowledgment of pain. She was bringing up the rear, her body angled slightly to compensate for the weight of the broken cart axle strapped across her back. The leather straps bit into her shoulders, a constant, grinding reminder of her injury. With each step, a dull throb radiated down her arm, a physical anchor that dragged at her speed. She adjusted her grip on the haft of her hammer, her knuckles whitening, her jaw tight against the urge to wince.

"Keep moving," she said, her voice flat and controlled. "I’m not stopping."

"You’re drifting," Torin called back, his tone neutral, devoid of judgment but laced with urgency. "If we hit a sudden drop, you won’t have time to brace."

"I know," Maren replied. She took a breath, a deliberate, shuddering intake of air that she pushed deep into her lungs before releasing it slowly. She shifted her weight, trying to distribute the strain away from her injured shoulder, but the effort only seemed to sharpen the ache. The axle shifted, a creak of stressed wood that sounded impossibly loud in the quiet stone chamber.

Elias glanced back. Kaelen was walking beside Maren, his hand hovering inches from her elbow, not quite touching. He wasn’t offering to carry the weight; he knew better than to ask for such a burden when his own reserves were critically low. Instead, he was acting as a counterbalance, his presence a subtle physical support that allowed Maren to lean slightly without collapsing.

"Kaelen," Elias said, his eyes fixed on the mage’s face. The skin around Kaelen’s eyes was pale, the veins standing out against his complexion. He looked exhausted, the kind of tiredness that settled in the bones and refused to leave. "How much further?"

"Hard to say," Kaelen admitted, his voice raspy. He didn’t look at Elias; he was watching the floor, tracking the faint, shimmering residue of the wards they had bypassed earlier. "The magical field is thinner here. It’s like walking through water. It takes more effort to stay hidden." He paused, a flicker of irritation in his eyes. "And it’s getting harder to keep the light steady. One shield left. Maybe two, if I’m careful. After that, I’m just a man with a brass glove."

Elias nodded. He understood the cost. Kaelen was burning his own vitality to maintain their cover, a sacrifice that was slowly draining him down to a husk. It was a necessary evil, but it was a debt that would be collected.

They reached the junction. It was a low, arched doorway, barely tall enough for Torin to walk through without hunching. Beyond it, the passage branched into two distinct paths. The left-hand passage was narrow, choked with debris, but it offered cover. The right-hand passage was wider, clear, and led directly toward the faint, flickering light of a torch burning in the distance.

Torin stopped, raising a hand to signal the halt. He leaned against the archway, his shoulder pressing against the cold stone, and listened. The silence beyond the junction was absolute, a vacuum that sucked the sound from their breathing. There were no footsteps. No voices. Just the distant, rhythmic drumming of rain against the roof, a sound that seemed to come from miles away.

"Right side," Torin said, his voice low. "Direct route to the storage chamber. But it’s exposed. If there’s a guard on the gallery above, we’re visible for the whole stretch."

"We don’t have time for the long way," Maren said. She was leaning heavily on her hammer now, her other hand braced against the wall for support. Her face was grim, but her eyes were sharp, focused on the path ahead. "We’re already late. If they’re planning another raid, we need to be in position before they shift patrols."

Elias looked at Kaelen, then at Maren. He could see the strain on both their faces. Kaelen’s reserves were nearly spent; Maren’s body was reaching its limit. They could not afford a detour. They could not afford a fight. They had to move through that open corridor, into the light, and hope that their composure was enough to keep them hidden.

"Kaelen," Elias said. "Can you hold the light steady for twenty feet? Just enough to see what’s there, but not enough to draw attention?"

Kaelen nodded slowly. He lifted his hand, the brass gauntlet glowing brighter, the light spilling out in a controlled, narrow beam. It was a focused effort, a deliberate restriction of his power. The mage’s brow furrowed, a line of sweat forming between his eyebrows. "I can do it," he said, his voice strained. "But it’ll drain me. And if anything moves, I won’t have the reflexes to react."

Elias turned to Maren. "You’re on point. Torin, cover the rear. If anything moves, we freeze. If it attacks, we move. We don’t engage unless we have to."

Maren straightened, her hand tightening on her hammer. "Understood."

They moved into the junction. The air here was colder, drier, and carried a faint, metallic scent of old blood and rusted iron. The path ahead was clear, but the darkness beyond the beam of Kaelen’s light was thick, almost solid. They stepped forward, one by one, their boots finding purchase on the damp flagstones.

The journey was slow, deliberate, a test of endurance and will. Maren’s steps were heavy, each one a small victory against the pain. Kaelen’s light flickered, the beam wavering slightly as his concentration wavered. Elias watched them both, his mind calculating the distance, the time, the risk. They were moving into the heart of the goblin’s territory, armed with nothing but their wits and the fading light of a mage’s magic.

As they reached the midpoint of the corridor, Maren stopped. Her breath was ragged, a sharp intake of air that she quickly suppressed. She looked at Elias, her eyes wide with a mixture of pain and determination.

"I can keep going," she said, her voice barely a whisper. "But I’m running out of steam."

Elias nodded. He didn’t offer a solution; there was no time to find one. He simply gave the signal to move, and they continued on, into the darkness, toward the storage chamber where Nella and Hobb waited.

The midpoint of the corridor was a trap of silence. The stone here was older, the mortar crumbled into dust that coated their boots, and the air hung heavy with the scent of damp iron. It was not the silence of empty space, but the held breath of a mechanism waiting to snap.

Elias raised a hand, his fingers spread. The party froze. Maren leaned against the cold wall, her hammer head resting on the flagstones, her breath coming in shallow, controlled pulls. She did not slump; she anchored herself. Behind them, Kaelen’s gauntlet flickered, the amber light stuttering like a candle in a draft, then steadying into a dim, pulsing glow. It was not enough to light the room, only enough to outline the immediate floor.

"Elara," Elias whispered, his voice low and steady, cutting through the oppressive quiet. "The vibration."

Elara was already crouching, her face inches from the stone floor. She held a vial of quartz dust, a fine, pale powder that caught the meager light from Kaelen’s hand. She didn’t speak. She listened. To the untrained ear, the keep was silent. To Elara, it was a chorus of ticking gears, shifting weights, and strained wire.

"There," she murmured, pointing to a section of floor three feet ahead of Maren’s boot. "It’s not a pressure plate this time. It’s a tripwire. But it’s not for a man."

Elias knelt beside her, his eyes narrowing. "For what?"

"A goblin," Elara said, her voice tight with concentration. "The tension is low. It’s set to trigger on a light footstep, or a small weight. If Maren steps there, it snaps. If I step there, it snaps. But if we bypass it..."

"And if we don't?" Kaelen asked, his voice rough, strained from the effort of holding the light steady. His forehead was beaded with sweat, despite the chill in the air.

"Then we’re in the open with a bell ringing in Skrit’s ear," Torin said from the rear. His hand was on his sword, but he hadn’t drawn it. He was watching the shadows at the end of the corridor, his posture loose, ready to move. "And I’d rather not spend the night explaining to the goblins why five friends are visiting their pantry."

Elias looked at Maren. The hammer-wielder was watching the floor, her jaw tight. She knew the weight of her own body. She knew the limits of her injury. She did not need to be told that she was the danger.

"Maren," Elias said softly. "Can you jump?"

Maren let out a short, dry breath that might have been a laugh. "I can jump, Elias. I just can’t land. And if I miss, I break a hip. Or the wire. Or both."

"Then we don't jump," Elias decided. He turned to Elara. "The wire. Can you cut it?"

Elara shook her head slowly. "It’s braided iron. Hard as steel. My knife would snap it, but the noise? A pin drop sounds like a cannon in this place. And the vibration..."

"Then we remove the trigger," Maren said.

Elias looked at her. "The stone?"

"The floor is loose," Maren said, her eyes scanning the masonry. "Here." She tapped a flagstone to the left of the tripwire. "See the gap? It’s not anchored. If we pull it out, the floor drops. The wire loses its anchor point. It goes slack. No snap. No sound."

It was a simple solution. Brutal, in its directness. And it required the person with the least mobility to do the most delicate work.

Elias hesitated for a fraction of a second, the conflict between caution and action warring in his chest. Then he looked at Kaelen. "Kaelen, light. Hold it. Just enough to see the gap."

Kaelen nodded, his face pale, the effort of the magic writing lines of exhaustion around his eyes. He focused his will, and the gauntlet brightened, just slightly, casting a cone of pale gold onto the floor.

Maren moved.

She did not rush. She shifted her weight, bracing her good leg against the wall, her injured shoulder locked in place. She crouched, her hammer resting on her knee, and extended her good hand toward the loose stone. Her fingers found the edge, the rough, jagged lip of the masonry.

"Elara," she said, her voice barely audible. "Guide me."

Elara leaned in, her eyes locked on the wire, her finger hovering in the air. "Inch by inch, Maren. Feel the tension. If it pulls, stop."

Maren’s fingers gripped the stone. She felt the faintest resistance, the tension of the iron wire buried in the dust. She pulled.

The stone did not move.

She pulled again, her knuckles white, her breath held in her lungs. The stone shifted, a fraction of an inch, grinding against the mortar.

*The click echoed through the room.*

The sound was tiny, a dry snap of stone on stone. But in the silence, it was a thunderclap.

Maren froze. Elara’s eyes widened. Torin’s hand tightened on his sword hilt.

For a long, agonizing second, nothing happened. No bell. No shout. No movement from the shadows.

Then, a soft *thud* as the stone settled into the gap, dropping below the level of the flagstones.

Maren slumped back against the wall, her shoulder trembling with the effort. She did not look at Elias. She looked at the floor.

"It’s dead," she whispered. "The wire is slack. It’s just hanging there now. Useless."

Elias exhaled, the tension in his shoulders releasing. He looked at Torin, who gave a single, sharp nod. The soldier’s eyes were bright with a mix of relief and respect.

"Good work, Maren," Torin said, his voice low.

Maren met his gaze, her expression unreadable. "Don't thank me yet. I haven't landed."

Elara stood up, brushing the dust from her knees. She looked at the loose stone, then at Maren, her eyes soft with a quiet admiration. "You felt the tension?" she asked.

"Every bit of it," Maren said. "It’s a lesson, Elara. You don't have to break the wire. You just have to remove the reason it exists."

Kaelen’s light flickered again, dimmer this time. "We should move," he said, his voice strained. "Before I forget how to see."

Elias stood, brushing the dust from his hands. He looked at the path ahead, now clear of the immediate hazard. The corridor stretched on, dark and waiting.

"Stay close," Elias said. "And keep your feet light. The goblins are not the only ones who know how to listen."

They moved forward, the rhythm of their steps careful, deliberate, a dance of stone and shadow. The alarm was silent. The path was clear. And for the first time since they had entered the keep, the air felt less like a trap and more like a passage.

They reached the next junction, a wider space where the stone walls gave way to rough-hewn timber beams. Here, the air was warmer, carrying the faint scent of smoke and wood.

"Wait," Elias said, holding up a hand.

He pointed to the beams above. There, hanging from the rafters, was a series of small, leather pouches. They were strung together with thin wire, each one containing a handful of dried herbs.

"Smoke bombs," Elara said, her eyes widening. "Not for fire. For confusion. If they open the door, the smoke fills the room. They can’t see us. We can’t see them."

Elias looked at Maren. "Can you disable them?"

Maren looked up at the pouches, her face tight with concentration. "If I can reach them. But the wire is taut. If I cut it, they fall. If I pull them, they might trigger a secondary mechanism."

"Then we don't touch them," Elias said. "We go around."

He pointed to a narrow gap in the wall to the left, barely wide enough for Maren to squeeze through.

"That way," he said. "It’s tighter. Slower. But it’s quiet."

Maren nodded, her eyes hard. "I can do it."

And so they moved, one by one, into the dark, carrying their burdens and their breaths, leaving the silent alarms behind them. The keep was vast, and they were small, but they were together. And in the shadows, the goblins waited, unaware that the silence had already been broken, not by a shout, but by a stone.

The gap was a jagged tear in the masonry, no wider than a man’s shoulders and barely high enough for a crouching figure to pass without scraping his spine against the rough-hewn stone. It smelled of wet clay and old dust, a sharp contrast to the stale, metallic air of the main corridor they had just left.

Maren went first. She didn’t hesitate. With a grunt of effort that she clearly tried to suppress, she twisted her body sideways, her armor grinding against the stone with a sound that was far too loud for the silence they needed. She froze, her eyes wide, looking back at Elias.

"Sorry," she mouthed.

Elias shook his head, a subtle gesture of dismissal. *Don’t apologize. Elias told him to move.*

She turned back to the gap and forced herself through, her muscles straining against the tight space. The broken cart axle strapped to her back made the maneuver nearly impossible, the wood digging into her shoulder blades. She groaned low in her throat, a sound of pure physical exertion, and then was through, disappearing into the darkness beyond.

Torin was next. He moved with the fluid grace of a man who had spent more years in tight spaces than most had spent in open fields. He slipped through the gap with barely a sound, his sword sheathed, his body a blur of dark leather and steel.

Kaelen followed. His brass gauntlet was barely a flicker now, a dying ember in the gloom. He winced as the stone scraped against his robes, the fabric tearing slightly. He didn’t stop to fix it. He pushed himself through, his face pale, his breathing shallow. He was exhausted, but he was still there.

Elara came next, her small frame fitting through the gap with relative ease. She paused on the other side, her lantern casting a weak, trembling light onto the stone floor. She looked back at Elias, her eyes wide with concern.

"Elias," she whispered. "Are you coming?"

Elias looked at the gap. He looked at his companions, huddled in the dark, waiting for him. He thought of the goblins, unaware and confident in their defenses. He thought of the captives, waiting in the shadows. He thought of the stone he had removed, the silence he had preserved, the respect he had earned.

He stepped forward and pushed himself through the gap.

The moment he was on the other side, the air changed. It was colder here, drier, and it smelled of earth and stone. The passage was narrow, winding deeper into the keep. The light from Elara’s lantern was the only source of illumination, casting long, dancing shadows that seemed to move independently of their source.

They stood in the narrow passage, breathing hard, their hearts beating in a synchronized rhythm. They had bypassed the first defense. They had preserved their strength. They had proven that they were not the prey.

Elias allowed himself a small exhale, a sound barely audible over the rain. He glanced back once, just to be sure. The passage behind them was empty. The goblins were gone, lost in the shadows, confused and defeated without ever knowing how.

They moved on, deeper into the keep, toward the storage chamber where Nella and Hobb waited. The path was clear. The next step was theirs to take. And for the first time since they had entered the keep, the silence felt less like a threat and more like a promise.

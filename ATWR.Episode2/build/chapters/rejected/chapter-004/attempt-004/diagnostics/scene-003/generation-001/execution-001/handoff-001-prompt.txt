The goblins’ argument had shifted from the spilled pot to the relative merits of a stolen chicken leg, a debate that consumed their attention with the single-minded intensity of children disputing over a broken toy. Their voices rose and fell in a rhythmic barking cadence, the sound of wet wood and sputtering fire filling the damp air. In the corner, where the light from Kaelen’s fading gauntlet barely reached, Nella and Hobb sat bound to a rough-hewn wooden post. Their clothes were torn, their faces smudged with dirt and dried blood, but their eyes were open, wide and terrified, tracking the movement of the intruders with a desperate, animal alertness.

Elias did not move. He stood in the threshold, his hand resting lightly on the pommel of his sword, his body a stillness that contrasted sharply with the chaotic energy of the room. He watched the goblins, counting their numbers, noting the crude sharpened sticks they held and the way they clustered around the fire for warmth. There were four of them, plus the one who had been sleeping near the door, now waking with a groggy, confused squint. Five goblins. Five of them, distracted by a spilled pot and a missing chicken leg, while five humans stood in their midst, armed and ready.

Maren shifted her weight, the broken cart axle strapped to her shoulder creaking softly. She looked at Elias, her expression a mixture of impatience and grim calculation. She pointed a thick finger at the goblins, then at the captives, then back at Elias. The message was clear: they had the element of surprise, but they were wasting it.

Elias nodded, a subtle dip of his chin. He signaled to Torin, who was holding the door open with one hand, his other resting on his sword hilt. Torin gave a thumbs-up, his face a mask of quiet readiness. He had already identified the exit route, the narrow passage that led back to the junction, and he knew exactly where to stand to cover their retreat if things went wrong.

Kaelen, standing near the center of the room, was the only one who looked visibly strained. His brass gauntlet, which had been providing the dim, steady light that illuminated the chamber, was flickering. The amber glow was weaker than it had been a minute ago, the light pulsing with a faint, irregular rhythm that matched the tremor in his hands. He was expending the last of his reserves to maintain the illumination, knowing that if he let the light fade completely, the goblins would become more suspicious, more aggressive. But every second the light burned, it drained him further, leaving him weaker, slower, and more vulnerable.

Elara, crouched near the base of the wooden post, was studying the captives. She did not touch them, did not speak to them, but her eyes were sharp, analytical. She was looking at the ropes binding their wrists, the way the knots were tied, the condition of their skin. She was gathering data, building a picture of the goblins’ methods, their limitations, their vulnerabilities. And as she worked, she noticed something that made her pause.

The ropes were not just crude leather. They were woven with a thin, metallic thread, a subtle shimmer that caught the dim light from Kaelen’s gauntlet. It was a simple ward, a minor binding spell that would make it difficult for the captives to break free, even if they had the strength to do so. But it was also a flaw. The ward was tied to the goblins’ presence, their intent, their belief in their own power. If the goblins were removed from the equation, the ward would weaken, eventually failing entirely.

Elara looked up, her eyes meeting Elias’s. She didn’t speak, but her nod was clear. She had found a way to free the captives without triggering any alarms, without using any magic, without risking Kaelen’s already fragile reserves. It was a simple, elegant solution, born of observation and understanding, not brute force or spellcraft.

Elias understood. He looked at Maren, then at Torin, then at Kaelen. He gave the signal.

The plan was simple. Maren would move to the goblins’ left, her hammer raised, ready to strike the first one who turned. Torin would cover the right, his sword drawn, his body a shield between the goblins and the exit. Kaelen would remain in the center, his light steady, his eyes on the goblins, his mind on the exit. And Elias would step forward, not to fight, but to speak.

He walked into the room, his boots silent on the stone floor. The goblins turned, their eyes widening, their weapons raised. For a moment, the room was still, the air thick with tension, the fire crackling, the goblins’ breath visible in the cold air.

Then Elias spoke.

"Stand down," he said, his voice calm, steady, carrying the weight of authority. "You have made a mistake. You have taken people who are not yours. You have stolen goods that do not belong to you. And now, you are going to let them go."

The goblins hesitated, their weapons wavering. They were not used to being spoken to, let alone commanded. They looked at each other, confused, uncertain. One of them, the largest, began to raise his spear, his eyes narrowing in anger.

But before he could move, Maren was there.

Her hammer struck the ground with a resonant crack, sending a wave of shock through the stone floor. The goblins flinched, their formation breaking. In that moment of confusion, in that fraction of a second of hesitation, Elias moved.

He did not attack. He did not threaten. He simply stood there, his hand on his sword, his eyes on the goblins, his voice steady. And in that silence, in that stillness, the goblins realized that they were outmatched.

One by one, they lowered their weapons. They backed away, their eyes wide with fear, their bodies trembling. They did not run. They did not scream. They simply stood there, frozen, defeated by the weight of their own mistakes.

And in the corner, bound to the post, Nella and Hobb watched, their eyes wide with disbelief, their hearts pounding in their chests. They had been waiting for this moment, for the rescue, for the end of their suffering. And now, as the goblins surrendered, as the light from Kaelen’s gauntlet faded to a faint, barely visible glow, they knew that it had finally come.

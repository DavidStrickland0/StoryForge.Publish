Torin stopped.

He didn't signal with a hand or a word. He simply ceased moving, his boots settling into the damp grit of the floor with a finality that silenced the group. The air in the corridor shifted; the damp chill seemed to thicken, carrying the scent of wet stone and something sharper, like rusted iron.

Elias moved up beside him, keeping his voice below a whisper. "What is it?"

Torin pointed forward. The passage they had been following widened slightly, opening into a low-ceilinged antechamber. The light from Kaelen's gauntlet, dimmed to a faint amber glow, caught the edges of objects piled against the far wall: sacks, crates, and the distinct, curved shape of a bow. It wasn't a defensive line. It was a storage dump.

"Traps," Torin said, his eyes narrowing. "Look at the floor."

Elias looked. The flagstones were uneven, cracked, and covered in a thin layer of mud. To the untrained eye, it was just debris. To Torin, it was a map. He crouched, his hand hovering over a loose stone near the center of the path.

"Pressure plate," Torin murmured. "Under that slab. It's heavy, but the support is thin. One wrong step, and the mechanism drops."

Elara stepped forward, her satchel swinging gently as she moved. She didn't ask Torin to move. Instead, she pulled a small vial of quartz dust from her pocket, uncorked it, and let a pinch fall onto the stone. The dust didn't glow. It just sat there, inert.

"No magic," Elara said, her voice clear and steady. "It's mechanical. Crude. A counterweight, probably. If we step on it, it trips a lever. If we don't, it's just a rock."

Kaelen, who had been watching the far wall, shifted his weight. "Can we go around?"

Torin shook his head. "The passage narrows here. If we go left, we hit a dead end behind a stack of crates. Right, it's a drop-off into the cellar proper. This is the only path."

Elias looked at the trap, then at his companions. The solution was obvious, and it was the one they had hoped to avoid. "Maren?"

Maren didn't ask what he wanted. She was already moving, her motions tight and controlled despite the ache throbbing in her shoulder. She reached into her pack and withdrew her hammer. The wooden haft was worn smooth by years of use, the head heavy and bound in iron. She didn't need to inspect the trap. She had seen enough of these mechanisms to know that if it was built from stone and lever, it could be broken.

She knelt, her knees sinking into the cold mud. With a grunt that was mostly effort and little else, she wedged the claw of her hammer under the edge of the loose stone. "Hold this," she said, not looking up.

Torin moved to her side, placing a hand on the stone to stabilize it. Elara stayed back, watching the dust on the floor, ready to verify the mechanism's state. Kaelen held the light steady, ensuring the shadows didn't swallow them. Elias stood ready, his hand on his sword, listening to the darkness beyond the antechamber.

Maren pulled.

The stone shuddered. For a second, it resisted, the weight of the counterweight pulling it down. Then, with a sharp crack, the support beneath it snapped. The heavy slab tilted, grinding against the floor, and slid into the gap Torin had marked. The mechanism, deprived of its trigger, hung uselessly in the air.

"Clear," Torin said.

They moved.

Torin led, stepping over the now-inactive trap with a fluid grace that spoke of years spent navigating hostile ground. Elias followed, checking the shadows. Maren brought up the rear, her breathing steady, the pain in her shoulder a constant companion she refused to acknowledge. Kaelen and Elara moved between them, a tight cluster of competence and caution.

As they rounded the corner, the antechamber opened into a larger space. The air here was heavier, smelling of woodsmoke and unwashed bodies. The goblins' living quarters.

And there, in the center of the room, stood a goblin.

He was not looking for them. He was hunched over a small fire, tending a pot that bubbled with something dark and pungent. His back was to the party, his shoulders hunched in the way of someone who was tired of being afraid.

Elias stopped.

The goblin did not turn. He stirred the pot with a wooden stick, his movements slow, deliberate. He was cooking. Or rather, he was trying to cook. The fire was small, barely more than an ember, and the pot was likely to be burnt or undercooked. It was a mundane act, devoid of threat, yet it carried a weight that cut deeper than any spear.

"He's not guarding anything," Elara observed, her voice low. "He's just... living."

Torin's hand rested on his sword hilt, but he didn't draw it. He watched the goblin, his expression unreadable. "He's alone."

"Not alone," Kaelen corrected quietly. "Just not paying attention. That's how we get in. And out."

Elias looked at the goblin, then at the narrow passage that led deeper into the keep, where the storage chamber—and the captives—waited. The goblin was a hazard, yes. But he was also a distraction. If they moved now, they would be silent. If they waited, they might be seen.

The choice was not between combat and stealth. It was between speed and silence.

Torin made the call. "We go around. Left. The wall is thin here. I can hear the draft from the cellar. If we squeeze through that gap, we bypass him entirely."

Elias nodded. It was a risk. The gap was narrow, barely wide enough for a man to turn. Maren, with her broad shoulders and the weight of her injury, would have difficulty. But it was the only way to remain unseen.

They moved.

Torin went first, his body pressing against the rough stone, his movements precise. He found the gap, a fissure in the masonry no wider than a doorframe, and slipped through. Elara followed, her satchel catching on a protruding rock, but she shook it free and continued. Kaelen moved next, his light dimmed to a near-invisible glow, his body moving with the economy of a man who knew his limits.

Maren was last.

She paused at the gap, her hand on the stone. She looked back at the goblin, who was still hunched over his pot, humming a tuneless melody. She looked at Elias, her eyes hard, her jaw set. She didn't ask for help. She didn't need it. She simply pushed through the gap, her body forcing a path through the cold stone, her breath coming in short, controlled bursts.

When she emerged on the other side, she was breathing hard, but her voice was steady. "Clear."

They were in the cellar.

The air here was colder, darker, and smelled of fear. The passage was narrow, the walls close, the ceiling low. It was a place designed for storage, not for people. And yet, ahead of them, through a heavy iron-bound door, they could hear it.

A cough. A whisper. A sound so faint it might have been the wind, but which Elias recognized as the sound of someone trying to stay alive.

They had bypassed the trap. They had bypassed the guard. They had navigated the maze of the keep's defenses without alerting a single goblin.

Now, they stood before the door to the storage chamber.

The rescue was no longer a question of whether they could find the captives. It was a question of how they would bring them out.

Elias placed a hand on the door, feeling the cold metal through his glove. He looked at his companions, their faces illuminated by the faint, dying light of Kaelen's gauntlet. They were tired. They were hurt. They were ready.

"Let's go," he said.

The door did not open.

It was not locked, exactly, but the mechanism was seized by years of neglect and the recent, clumsy handling of the goblins. The iron handle, cold and pitted, resisted Elias's grip. He could feel the weight of the bar on the other side, a rough-hewn piece of oak wedged into a slot in the frame. It was a crude barrier, the kind that could be kicked in with a single, well-placed boot, but the sound of such a force would carry through the thin stone walls and down the corridor like a bell toll.

Elias released the handle and stepped back, his breathing controlled. He turned to the group, his eyes moving from Torin's tense posture to Maren's grimace, which she masked by adjusting the strap of her hammer.

"We can't force it," he said, his voice low, barely a whisper above the damp silence of the cellar. "We need to open it from the inside, or wait for the bar to be removed. We don't know which."

Torin shifted his weight, the leather of his armor creaking softly. He looked at the door, then at the narrow gap between the doorframe and the wall where the bar was likely wedged. "I can try to lift it," he offered, his voice steady but strained. "If I get my weight behind it, I might be able to pry the bar loose enough for someone to slip through. But it will make noise."

"Too much noise," Elara said. She was crouched near the base of the wall, her fingers tracing the rough stone. "Listen."

They all went still. From somewhere deeper in the keep, beyond the immediate chamber they were in, came the sound of a pot clattering against a wooden table. It was a sharp, metallic ring, followed by the dull thud of something heavy being set down. Then, a low, guttural murmur, the sound of a goblin speaking to another, their voices slurred and lazy.

"They're close," Maren said. Her eyes were sharp, scanning the shadows. "That's the guard we passed. He's not alone. There's at least one other. Maybe more."

Elias nodded. "That changes things. We can't risk a noise that draws them to this door. If they come here, they'll find us, or they'll find the captives and panic. Either way, we lose the element of surprise."

Torin frowned, his jaw tight. "So we wait? While they're in there with Nella and Hobb?"

"We wait for a gap," Elias said. "Or we find another way in."

He looked at Kaelen, who was standing slightly apart, his face pale in the dim light of his gauntlet. The mage's hand rested on the brass device, his knuckles white. He hadn't spoken in a long while, but his presence was a constant, draining weight in the air.

"Kaelen," Elias said. "Can you do anything? A small light? A distraction?"

Kaelen shook his head slowly. "My reserves are empty, Elias. I can keep the light steady, but that's it. If I try to do anything more, I'll pass out. And I don't think I can take the pain of a sudden blackout in a place like this."

Elara stood up, brushing the dust from her knees. "We don't need magic. We need observation. Look at the door."

She pointed to the bottom of the door, where the wood met the stone floor. A thin line of light cut through the gap, the faint, orange glow of a fire. It was flickering, casting long, dancing shadows that stretched out into the darkness of the corridor.

"Firelight," Elara said. "They have a fire in the room. That means they are gathered around it. That means they are not patrolling. They are resting."

Maren nodded slowly. "Resting is dangerous. Goblins don't rest well. They get bored. They get loud."

"And they get distracted," Torin said. A small, grim smile touched his lips. "I remember something about that."

He stepped forward, moving with a fluid grace that belied the strain in his shoulders. He reached into his belt and pulled out a small, smooth stone, no larger than a walnut. He looked at Elias, his eyes asking for permission.

Elias hesitated for a fraction of a second, then nodded.

Torin moved to the side of the door, where the gap was widest. He crouched, his back to the wall, his body a solid block of muscle and intent. He aimed the stone with the precision of a man who had thrown a thousand such objects in a thousand different situations.

He threw.

The stone skipped once on the stone floor, a sound like a soft click, and then sailed through the gap at the bottom of the door. It landed on the wooden table with a sharp *tock*, near the edge of the pot.

For a moment, nothing happened. The murmuring continued, low and rhythmic.

Then, a sharp yelp. A crash of wood. The sound of a pot being knocked over, the liquid inside spilling with a hiss and a sizzle.

"Damn it!" a goblin voice shouted, angry and confused. "Who did that, Skrit? You clumsy idiot!"

A second voice, lower and sharper with irritation, cut in. "I didn't do it, you fool. It was the wind. The door is broken."

"The door is not broken, it is *open*!" the first voice snapped back. "And the wind is not coming through a closed door!"

Elias watched the sliver of light beneath the door. The shadows flickered and shifted in erratic bursts. The goblins were arguing, their voices climbing in pitch and volume. They were distracted, their attention turned inward to their own failure.

"Now," Elias whispered.

Torin was already moving. He did not wait for the argument to end. He did not wait for the goblins to look up. He stepped up to the door, his shoulder bracing against the wood. He did not kick it. He did not slam it. He simply pushed, using his core strength, his legs, his entire body weight.

The wood groaned. The bar, wedged in its slot, shifted. It was not enough to break, but it was enough to loosen.

Maren moved to Elias's side. She didn't have a key, but she had a hammer. She didn't swing it. She used the flat head of the hammer to pry at the edge of the door, her muscles straining, her face tight with pain and focus. She used the leverage, the small gap created by Torin's push, to widen it just an inch.

Elara slipped through.

She moved with the silence of a cat, her body pressing against the rough stone, her satchel swinging gently at her hip. She was gone into the room before the others could even see her.

Kaelen followed, his gauntlet dim, his face pale, but his movements steady. He was not a fighter, but he was a survivor. He moved through the gap, his eyes fixed on the floor, on the shadows, on the goblins who were still arguing, their backs turned to the door, their attention fixed on the spilled pot and the angry goblin who had tried to clean it up.

Maren pushed through next, her hammer resting against her shoulder, her body a shield between the door and the room. She was the barrier, the anchor, the one who held the line.

Torin was last. He held the door open with his shoulder, his face grim, his eyes scanning the room for any sign of movement. He saw the goblins, their backs to him, their voices raised in a heated dispute. He saw the fire, the table, the chairs. And he saw the captives.

Nella and Hobb were lashed to a rough-hewn wooden post in the room's corner, their wrists bound with frayed, coarse rope. Bruises darkened their cheeks, and their faces were swollen from blows. They were alive. They were breathing. They were afraid.

But they were there.

Elias stepped through the gap, his hand resting on his sword hilt, his gaze locked on the goblins. He did not speak. He did not move. He waited.

The goblins were still arguing. They were still distracted. They were still unaware of the five people standing in their room, watching them, waiting for the right moment.

The rescue was no longer a question of whether they could find the captives. It was a question of how they would bring them out. And for the first time in hours, Elias had the answer.

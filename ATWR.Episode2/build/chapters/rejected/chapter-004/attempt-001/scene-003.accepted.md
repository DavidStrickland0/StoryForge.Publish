Elias did not answer immediately. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, his eyes fixed on the map for a long moment, weighing the red ink against the reality of the five people standing in the cold. The plan was sound. It was even elegant, in a grim sort of way. But elegance required resources they no longer possessed. Kaelen’s gauntlet was a dying ember, and Maren’s body was a ledger of debts she was currently paying in pain.

"Stick with the plan," Elias said finally, his voice low and flat. "But we modify the approach. We don't rush. We don't rely on speed. We rely on silence and the element of surprise. Torin, you take point. Elara, you’re with him. Watch the walls, watch the floor. If you see anything that doesn’t belong, we stop."

Maren nodded, her jaw tightening. "And if we get stuck?"

"Then we wait," Elias said. "We wait for the patrol to shift. We wait for the goblins to make a mistake. We do not make one ourselves."

Torin checked the edge of his sword, a habitual motion that served no tactical purpose but anchored his own nerves. "Understood. Let’s move."

They stepped through the heavy wooden door into the next chamber. The air here was different—thicker, heavier, carrying a distinct metallic tang that coated the back of the tongue. It smelled of old blood and wet stone. The torchlight from the previous room did not follow them; instead, they were left in a deeper gloom, illuminated only by the faint, pulsing amber of Kaelen’s gauntlet.

The room was small, circular, and lined with rough-hewn stone. There were no maps here, no stolen grain, no trophies. There were only bars.

Three heavy iron doors stood along the curved wall, each reinforced with thick bands of rusted metal. The hinges were oiled, a trick of maintenance that spoke of frequent use. Behind the first door, a faint sound drifted through the cracks—a low, rhythmic tapping. A fingernail against stone.

The name was Nella.

Elias moved toward the door, his hand resting on the hilt of his sword. He paused, listening. The tapping continued, slow and deliberate. It was not a frantic scratching; it was a marker of time. Someone in there was counting their own survival in small, measurable beats.

"Elara," Elias whispered. "Can you hear anything else?"

Elara shook her head, her eyes wide in the dim light. "No. Just the tapping. And... breathing. Shallow breathing."

Maren stepped forward, placing a gloved hand on the cold iron of the door. She closed her eyes for a second, listening not with her ears, but with the bones of her shoulder, feeling the vibration of the metal. "The lock is simple," she murmured. "A bar. But it’s thick. If we force it, the noise will carry through the whole cellar."

"Then we don't force it," Elias said. "Kaelen, can you open it?"

Kaelen shook his head, his face pale in the amber glow. "I can’t even light a candle, Elias. If I try to dispel a lock or pick a mechanism, I’ll lose consciousness. I’m sorry."

The failure hung in the air, heavier than the stone walls. They had come here with a plan, with skills, with preparation. Now, faced with the simplest of obstacles, they were out of resources.

Torin looked at the door, then at Maren. "Can you break it?"

Maren’s hands tightened on the hammer. "I can break it. But if I swing this hammer, the noise will bring every goblin in this keep running. And I’m tired, Torin. My shoulder is screaming. If I miss the first swing, or if the bar holds, I’m done."

Elias looked at his companions. They were the best he had. They were strong, clever, and brave. But they were also human, and they were exhausted. The goblins were waiting beyond those doors, unaware that their captors were about to be freed, but also unaware of how close the party was to breaking.

"We can’t do this yet," Elias said, the words tasting like ash. "We need to be ready. We need to be certain."

"Then what do we do?" Torin asked. His voice was tight, frustrated. "Wait for them to come to us?"

"We reposition," Elias said. "We go back to the junction. We take the other route. The longer one. We find a better angle. We find a way to get in without making a sound. Or we find a way to make a sound that they can’t ignore."

Maren let out a short, sharp breath, a sound that was almost a laugh, but held no humor. "You’re going to make us walk back up the stairs?"

"I’m going to save your life," Elias said quietly. "And theirs."

He turned to Elara. "How long until the patrol shifts?"

Elara frowned, her mind working through the patterns she had seen on the maps. "Based on the schedules... twenty minutes. Maybe thirty."

"Then we have twenty minutes," Elias said. "We move to the junction. We wait. We watch. And when they move, we strike."

The group stood in the silence of the cell block, the tapping of Nella’s fingernail the only sound in the world. It was a small, stubborn sound, a refusal to be erased. Elias listened to it, letting it settle into his bones.

"Let’s go," he said.

They turned and walked back out of the chamber, leaving the doors closed, the captives still waiting in the dark. The path back was the same, but it felt different now. The silence was no longer a weapon; it was a countdown.

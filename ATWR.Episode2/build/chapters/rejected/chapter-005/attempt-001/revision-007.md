The corridor narrowed, the ceiling dropping until Torin had to tilt his head back to avoid the rough-hewn flagstones. The air grew heavier, thick with the scent of damp rot and something sharper, like rusted iron. Elias kept his hand on Maren's shoulder, not to push, but to maintain the spacing Torin had established. They were a single, breathing unit, moving through the dark with the slow, deliberate grace of water finding its level.

Torin paused at a junction where the stone floor split into three paths. To the left, the passage descended steeply, the air rushing out in a cold, steady draft. Straight ahead, the corridor continued into a gloom so complete that even Kaelen's dimmed light seemed to die a few feet out. To the right, a narrow fissure in the wall, barely wide enough for a man to slip through sideways.

Torin gave a sharp nod. He stepped into the fissure, his body twisting to navigate the tight space. He emerged on the other side, where the passage opened into a wider, dimly lit chamber. The light here was different—yellow, flickering, casting long, dancing shadows against the stone walls.

Torin nodded. He moved to the left, positioning himself to cover the exit if the goblins panicked. Maren moved to the right, her hammer resting against her leg, ready to swing. Elara and Kaelen stayed close to Elias, a wall of quiet competence behind him.

Elias signaled to the group. Torin moved first, his sword drawn now, the steel singing softly as it left the scabbard. He didn't run; he flowed, covering the angle between the goblins and the door. Maren followed, her eyes scanning the shadows behind the crates, looking for a third goblin, a hidden blade, a trap. She found nothing. The room was small, cramped, and smelled of wet dog and ash.

It was in the narrow, lightless throat of the corridor, with the taste of wet stone and old blood still clinging to the back of Elias's throat, that the weight of the moment finally settled. The adrenaline of the initial confrontation had burned off, leaving behind a colder, sharper clarity. They were moving, but they were not yet free. The keep was a beast with many jaws, and they were currently in the belly, carrying the prize.

Torin, carrying Hobb across his broad back, moved with a heavy, deliberate grace that spoke of years spent hauling gear and bodies. Each step was a negotiation with the uneven flagstones, a silent test of balance and endurance. He did not complain of the weight. He did not complain of the dark. He simply moved, his breathing a low, steady rhythm that seemed to anchor the erratic breathing of the man on his back.

Elias watched him, and for a second, a familiar, irksome tension tightened in his own chest. It was the old, professional friction: the desire to lead versus the reality of the physical burden. Elias could map the route, could deduce the goblin patrol patterns, could assign roles with the precision of a surgeon. But he could not carry Hobb. He could not lift the beam that might fall, or break the lock that might jam. He looked at Torin's shoulders, the way the muscle bunched and released with each careful step, and saw not a subordinate, but a pillar.

Maren looked up. Her face was grim, her jaw set in that way that meant she was calculating angles and escape routes even while comforting a terrified civilian. "She's stable," Maren said. "Weak, scared, but stable. Hobb's leg is bad. A break. He's got a bruise on his cheek that looks like it's going to swell up into a purple bruise the size of a fist, but the rest of him is just tired. Elara's water did its job. He's not bleeding out. He's not dying. He's just... very, very hurt."

"Confused," Elara said from the shadows ahead. She had paused, her head tilted, listening to the silence beyond the immediate corridor. "They're shouting. Not at us. At each other. I hear the clatter of metal on metal. They're probably moving their loot, or changing shifts. One of them is crying. A high, thin sound. It's a child, or a young one. They're panicking."

Elias exchanged a glance with Kaelen. The mage was leaning against the wall, his gauntlet dark now, his eyes closed. He looked like he had aged ten years in the last hour.

"Kaelen," Elias said. "How are you?"

"Empty," Kaelen said, his voice a rough rasp. "I spent the last of my light to find the door. I burned the last of my smoke to hide our scent as we entered. Now I am just a man with a sword and a bad back. If you need me to throw a fireball, I can try, but it will be more of a spark. A very angry spark."

"We don't need fire," Maren said, rising to her feet. She moved to Torin's side, her hand resting briefly on his shoulder in a gesture of pure, practical solidarity. "We need speed. And we need to know where the goblins are not."

Elara stepped forward, her satchel clinking softly as she produced her charcoal and a scrap of parchment. The faint glow of Kaelen's earlier magic was gone, but the memory of the light remained in her eyes. "The layout is simple," she said. "We are in the cellar. Above us is the living quarters. To our left, the exit. To our right, the chief's room. The goblins are in the center. If we move left, we pass through the open space. If we move right, we go through the door."

"Left is faster," Torin said.

"Left is risky," Maren countered. "Open space means no cover. If they see us, we're sitting ducks in the dark. Right is slower, but it's narrow. A corridor. We can move through it in a line, and if they see us, we can fight one at a time."

Elias looked at the two of them, and then at Kaelen, who was opening his eyes. The mage looked at the parchment Elara was sketching, and then at the dark hallway ahead.

"I have an idea," Kaelen said. His voice was quiet, yet it sliced through the heavy tension. "I can't make light. But I can make noise."

Everyone turned to him.

"I can't cast a spell," Kaelen continued, a dry humor returning to his face despite the pain. "But I can drop a stone. Or a coin. Or a piece of that broken axle, Maren, if you can spare a splinter. I can make a sound that sounds like a person falling. Or a goblin being dropped. It will draw their attention to one specific point."

"Which point?" Elias asked.

"The far end of the corridor," Kaelen said. "If they run to investigate, the corridor will be empty. We pass through. It's not magic. It's just... physics. And a little bit of theater."

Maren looked at Torin. Torin looked at Elias.

"It's a gamble," Torin said.

"It's a plan," Elias corrected. "And it's the one we have. Kaelen, find a stone. Maren, check your pockets. Elara, mark the route. Torin, ready Hobb for movement. Maren, get Nella on her feet."

They moved with the synchronized efficiency of a machine that had been oiled and calibrated. There was no shouting, no frantic gesturing. There was only the soft scrape of boots, the rustle of cloth, and the quiet, focused intensity of five people who understood that their survival depended on each other's competence.

Elias watched them work, and he felt the tension in his chest ease. It was not a release of danger. The goblins were still there. The keep was still hostile. But for the first time, they were not just reacting. They were acting. And that was enough.

Kaelen did not have a coin. He had a handful of loose gravel, dislodged from the flagstones by Torin's recent struggle, and a scrap of dried meat rind that he had been saving for a snack that now felt like a distant, theoretical luxury.

He crouched near the mouth of the corridor, his fingers trembling slightly as he selected a pebble the size of a walnut. It was grey, rough, and perfectly ordinary. It was also the only thing he had left to offer the darkness.

"Ready?" he whispered.

Elias nodded. His eyes were fixed on the far end of the passage, where the shouts of the panicking goblins were growing louder, a chaotic chorus of fear and confusion. The sound was a shield, a blanket of noise that would cover their own movements if they timed it correctly.

"Go," Elias said.

Kaelen stood, his legs stiff from the cold, and walked to the edge of the visible light. He looked up. The ceiling was high, lost in the gloom. He tossed the pebble.

It arced through the air, a small, silent comet. It struck the stone floor at the far end of the corridor with a sharp, crisp *clack*.

Then, he followed it with the dried meat rind. The dried meat rind landed with a thump.

The sound was distinct. A person dropping a heavy object. A goblin falling.

For a heartbeat, the shouting in the center of the keep ceased. The silence was heavy, expectant. Then, the sound of shuffling feet, the clatter of dropped weapons, and the high-pitched yelp of goblins rushing toward the disturbance.

"Now," Elias breathed.

The group moved.

They did not run. Running was a mistake that invited injury and noise. Instead, they flowed. Torin, with Hobb slung over his back, moved first, his body a study in controlled effort. Each step was a negotiation with the pain in his shoulder, a silent plea for endurance that he answered with sheer will. Hobb groaned, a low, rhythmic sound that matched the cadence of Torin's gait.

Maren followed, her hand on Nella's elbow. The blacksmith was a bundle of nerves, her eyes wide and unseeing, but she was walking. She was *here*. That was enough for now.

Elias and Elara moved in the center, their boots silent on the wet stone. Kaelen brought up the rear, his heart hammering against his ribs, his mind already calculating the distance to the next turn, the next obstacle, the next breath.

The corridor narrowed again. The air grew thicker, heavier with the scent of old damp and something organic, something that had been here for a long time. The goblins' shouts faded behind them, replaced by the echo of their own breathing.

They reached the junction.

To the left, the passage opened into a wider space, the upper level of the keep. The air here was different. It was colder, drier, and carried a faint, metallic tang. To the right, the corridor continued downward, deeper into the keep's bowels.

Elias stopped.

"Which way?" he asked.

Elara stepped forward, charcoal poised in her grip. She had been sketching their route as they moved, a map of progress drawn in the dark. She pointed to the left.

"Up," she said. "The goblins are moving away from us. They are investigating the noise. If we go down, we risk running into them. If we go up, we are moving toward the exit, and toward the light."

"Light," Maren said, her voice dry. "We don't have any."

"No," Elias agreed. "But we have eyes. And we have each other."

He looked at Torin. The soldier's face was pale, his jaw clenched, but his eyes were steady. He had carried Hobb for a hundred yards, and he was still standing. He was still *here*.

"Can you keep going?" Elias asked.

Torin shifted Hobb's weight, a small, painful adjustment. "I can keep going," he said.

They turned left.

The passage led upward, a steep incline of uneven stone steps. It was a climb that would have been easy for a fresh man. For Torin, it was a test. For Maren, with the axle strapped to her shoulder and the weight of Nella in her arms, it was a struggle.

They climbed.

One step at a time.

The silence was absolute. The only sounds were their breathing, the creak of leather, the soft scrape of stone on stone. The world outside was rain and dark and chaos. The world inside was stone and breath and determination.

They reached the top.

The upper level was a large, circular chamber. It was empty, save for a single, broken table and a few scattered crates. The walls were lined with hooks, most of them empty, but a few still held rusted tools, broken shields, and tattered cloaks.

In the center of the room, a door stood slightly ajar.

It was a heavy, iron-banded door, similar to the one they had passed in the cellar. But this one was different. It was locked. And it was guarded.

A single goblin stood before it. He was larger than the others, his skin a mottled grey-green, his eyes hard and sharp. He held a spear in one hand and a crude club in the other. He was not panicking. He was waiting.

Elias raised a hand, signaling for the group to stop.

They froze.

The goblin looked at them. His eyes were not wide with fear. They were cold, calculating, and utterly devoid of mercy.

He knew they were there.

He had always known.

And he was waiting for them to make a mistake.

The goblin did not flinch. He did not raise his spear to a combat stance. He simply stood there, his weight balanced on the balls of his feet, his eyes tracking the movement of Torin's burden and Maren's trembling grip on Nella. He was a professional. He knew what he was looking at, and he knew it was a mistake for him to be standing in the open.

Elias held his hand steady. The silence in the chamber pressed against his eardrums, heavy and suffocating, broken only by the ragged breathing of the captives and the distant, rhythmic drumming of rain on the stone roof far above. He watched the tension knot in Kaelen's shoulders, the way the mage's fingers twitched at his side, seeking a magic that wasn't there.

"Let him go," Elias said.

His voice was calm, stripped of any threat, but it carried clearly across the empty space. The goblin's head tilted. He looked from Elias to the door behind him, then back again.

"Open the door," Elias continued, taking a single, deliberate step forward. "Or we take the others. And we leave. And you are alone."

The goblin's grip tightened on the spear, the wood creaking under the strain. He was not stupid. He could hear the groan of Torin's strained muscles, the weak, frightened whimper of Nella. He could see the sword at Elias's hip, the hammer resting on Maren's shoulder, the cold, steady gaze of Torin.

He knew they were there. He had always known. And he was waiting for them to make a mistake.

But Elias wasn't making a mistake. He was making a point.

"The chief is behind that door," Elias said. "I know. The layout is simple. You keep your treasures in the cellar, your slaves in the middle, and your leader in the back. It's a standard arrangement. It's predictable."

The goblin's eyes narrowed. He glanced over his shoulder, toward the iron-banded door. For a second, his posture shifted, a subtle tightening of the muscles in his back, a slight turn of the hips. He was listening.

"He's waiting for you to panic," Elias said, his voice dropping an octave, becoming a low rumble that seemed to vibrate in the stone floor. "He's waiting for you to shout, to charge, to break the door down. And then he'll let you in, and he'll kill you. One by one. It's a game he plays. It's how he keeps the fear alive."

The goblin's hand moved to the hilt of his club. His eyes were fixed on Elias, but his head was turned, just slightly, toward the door.

"Open it," Elias said. "Or I do."

The goblin made a sound, a low, guttural growl, and raised his spear. He was going to attack. He was going to test them. He wanted to see if they were as confident as they looked.

Elias did not draw his sword. He did not shout. He simply watched the goblin move, his eyes tracking the tip of the spear, calculating the angle, the distance, the time it would take to close the gap.

Torin moved.

It was not a charge. It was a step. A single, precise step that brought his sword into the light. The steel caught the faint, dying glow of the torch in the corner, flashing with a cold, deadly promise.

The goblin froze.

He looked at Torin. He looked at the sword. He looked at the way Torin's body coiled, his weight shifting forward, his eyes locked on the goblin's throat.

He understood.

He was not a warrior. He was a guard. And he was outmatched.

The goblin lowered his spear. It was a slow, deliberate movement, a surrender that was more about survival than honor. He turned his back to Elias, his eyes fixed on the door.

He did not open it.

He moved to the side of the door, his hand finding the heavy iron latch. His fingers trembled as he fumbled with the mechanism, the metal clinking softly in the silence. Then, with a sharp, decisive pull, the latch gave way.

The door swung inward.

The goblin did not step aside. He stood rigid in the doorway, his body blocking the entrance, his eyes wide and terrified. He was not letting them in. He was letting them see.

Elias saw the room beyond.

It was small, cramped, and dark. A single torch burned in a sconce on the wall, casting long, dancing shadows. In the center of the room, a figure sat on a crude wooden chair.

It was Skrit Ironjaw.

He was larger than the guard, his skin a deep, mottled grey, his eyes hard and sharp. He held a dagger in one hand, the blade glinting in the firelight. He did not look surprised. He did not look afraid.

He looked bored.

"Ah," Skrit said, his voice a low, rasping growl. "The heroes."

Skrit did not move. He sat back in his chair, the wood creaking under his weight, and watched the five figures in the doorway with the detached curiosity of a man observing a weather system he had no intention of fighting. The torchlight caught the jagged edge of his teeth, yellowed and broken, and the faint, mottled scars that mapped his face like a topographic chart of petty violence.

"Heroes," Skrit repeated, the word rolling off his tongue with a sneer that seemed to taste of ash and old blood. He leaned forward, the dagger resting against his knee, the tip pointing at the floor. "You have a lot of confidence for people who are carrying around broken furniture."

Elias stepped into the light. The flame caught the edge of his leather armor, casting a sharp shadow behind him, but he kept his hands visible, palms open. He did not draw his sword. He did not need to. The weight of the moment was already hanging in the air, heavy and suffocating.

"And you have a lot of nerve for a scavenger who's holding up two people from Pinehollow," Elias said, his voice low and steady. "We know who you are, Skrit. We know what you've taken. And we know that the door to this room is the only thing standing between you and a very long, very uncomfortable walk back to the edge of the woods."

Skrit's eyes narrowed. He glanced past Elias, his gaze sweeping over Torin, who stood like a statue with Hobb slung over his shoulder, and Maren, whose face was a mask of strained patience, the broken axle digging into her shoulder. He looked at the captives. Nella was leaning heavily against Maren, her eyes wide and wary, but she was breathing. Hobb was unconscious, his head lolling against Torin's chest, but his chest was rising and falling in a steady rhythm.

"They are not dead," Skrit said, his voice dropping an octave. It was not a question. It was an assessment. He looked at the dagger in his hand, then at the party. "My people are... disorganized. I will grant you that. But you are five. I am one. And I have the high ground."

"You have a chair," Maren said, her voice flat and dry. She shifted her weight, a wince crossing her face as the axle pressed into the bruised meat of her shoulder. "And a dagger. I have a hammer that is currently being used as a walking stick because my shoulder is on fire. If you come out of that room, Skrit, I will not miss. And I don't miss often. It's a flaw in my character, really. I find it tedious to have to aim carefully."

Skrit's mouth twitched. It was not a smile, but the tension in his shoulders seemed to ease, just slightly. He was calculating. Elias could see it in the way the goblin's eyes moved, scanning the room, the doorway, the weapons of the party. He was weighing the odds.

"The captives," Elias said, his voice steady. "You need them. They are your labor. Your strength. Without them, you are just a gang of frightened animals huddled in a ruin. With them, you are a business. A profitable one. But right now, that business is about to be shut down. We can do this the hard way. We can break down that door, and we can let Maren handle the 'tedious aiming.' Or we can do this the easy way. You put down the dagger. You step back. And you tell your people to stand down. Right now."

Skrit looked at his guard, who was still standing in the threshold, his spear lowered but his eyes fixed on the party. The guard looked back at Skrit, his face a mixture of terror and confusion. He was waiting for a command. He was waiting for permission to survive.

Skrit looked at the guard, then at the party. He looked at the dagger in his hand, then at the door. He looked at the captives, and for a moment, his expression softened. It was not pity, exactly. It was the recognition of a resource that had lost its value.

“You are very confident,” Skrit said.

“We are very bored,” Elias corrected. “And we have a job to do. So, Skrit. What is it going to be?”

The silence stretched, taut as a bowstring. The torch crackled, sending a small spark drifting up into the darkness. The air in the room hung thick with the smell of damp stone and the metallic tang of fear.

Skrit slowly raised his hands. The dagger slipped from his fingers and clattered onto the stone floor, the sound echoing sharply in the small room. He stepped back from the chair, his hands raised, his palms open.

“I stand down,” he said. His voice was low, but it carried. “My people... they are not warriors. They are survivors. If you want them, you can have them. But leave the keep. Leave the loot. And do not come back.”

Elias nodded. “That seems reasonable.”

Skrit turned to his guard. "Tell them," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. "Tell them that the hero is coming. Tell them to put down their weapons. Tell them to sit."

The guard nodded, a jerky, frantic motion. He turned and ran out of the room, his footsteps a blur of panic in the corridor.

Elias watched him go, then turned back to Skrit. "Stay there," he said. "And keep your hands where I can see them."

Skrit did not move. He stood in the center of the room, his hands raised, his eyes fixed on Elias. He looked smaller now, less imposing. He was just a goblin, standing in a dark room, with a dagger on the floor and a party of five men and women standing at the door.

Maren stepped into the room, her hammer resting on her shoulder. She looked at Skrit, then at the dagger on the floor. She kicked it aside, sending it skittering across the stone.

"Next time," she said, "try to negotiate before you start holding people up. It saves everyone so much trouble."

Elias looked at Torin, who was still carrying Hobb. The soldier's face was pale, but his eyes were clear. He looked at Maren, who was supporting Nella. The blacksmith was trembling, but she was standing. She was alive.

Elias took a deep breath, the tension in his chest finally releasing. The threat was gone. The command structure was broken. The goblins were frightened, but they were alive. And the captives were safe.

"Torin," Elias said, his voice soft. "Put Hobb down. Let's get him to a bed. Or at least a pile of straw."

Torin nodded, easing Hobb down onto the floor. The cart driver groaned, his eyes fluttering open, but he did not wake. He was out of it, at least for now.

"Maren," Elias said. "How is Nella?"

"She's scared," Maren said, her voice gentle. "But she's here. That's what matters."

Elias fixed his gaze on Skrit, who remained rooted in the center of the room. The goblin's eyes flickered between the party and the open door, then dropped to the dagger lying on the floor before returning to his own trembling hands.

"Is it over?" Skrit asked, his voice thin and small.

"For you," Elias said, "yes. For us, we're just getting started."

He turned to the others. "Let's move. We have a job to finish."

The silence in Skrit's chamber was not empty; it was heavy, pressurized, and thick with the smell of wet fur and fear. Elias kept his hands visible, palms open, a deliberate counter to the tension that still coiled in the air. He watched the goblin chief's eyes dart from the open doorway to the dagger lying useless on the stone floor, then back to the party. Skrit was small, hunched under the weight of his own defeat, and for the first time since they had entered the keep, the room felt larger.

"Move," Elias said, his voice flat and final. He did not look at Skrit. He looked at Torin.

Torin was already shifting his weight, a grimace tightening his jaw as he adjusted his grip on Hobb. The cart driver was limp against his back, a dead weight that pressed painfully into Torin's right shoulder. The old injury, dormant for weeks, was now a sharp, hot spike that radiated down his arm with every micro-movement. He did not complain. He simply breathed through his teeth, a low, controlled hiss, and began to walk toward the door.

Maren followed, her arm locked firmly around Nella's waist. The blacksmith's apprentice was trembling so violently that her knees seemed to buckle with each step, but she kept her chin up, her eyes fixed on the door as if it were the only thing in the world keeping her tethered to reality. Maren's own posture was rigid, the broken axle strapped to her shoulder acting as a counterweight to the strain of supporting another human being. She moved with the careful, grinding precision of a machine running on frayed gears.

Kaelen brought up the rear, his steps light but hurried. He was not carrying anyone, but the air around him felt thinner, drained of the ambient magic that usually helped him balance. He was exhausted, his eyes hollow, but he kept his gaze on the corridor behind them, listening for the clatter of boots that had not yet arrived.

Elara walked beside Elias, her satchel clinking softly against her hip. She was watching the door with the intensity of a hawk, her head tilted, listening to the silence beyond the threshold.

They emerged into the corridor, a narrow throat of stone that stretched away into the dark. The air here was colder, carrying the scent of damp moss and old iron. Elias signaled for them to stop, not because of a threat, but because they needed a moment to assess the situation without the immediate pressure of the room behind them.

"Torin," Elias said, keeping his voice low. "How long can you carry him?"

Torin stopped, his boots scuffing against the flagstones. He shifted Hobb slightly, the movement causing the cart driver to groan in his sleep. "A hundred yards, maybe more," Torin said, his voice strained. "But if I have to climb, or if the ground gets slick, I'm dropping him. And if I drop him, I'm done."

Maren looked up from where she was checking Nella's footing. "I can take the weight for a bit," she said, her tone practical, devoid of sentiment. "But I'm not climbing. And I'm not running. If we have to go vertical, we need a rope. Or a ladder. Or a miracle, and I don't see one of those in my satchel."

Elias nodded. He looked at the corridor ahead. It was straight, but the ceiling was low, and the walls were rough-hewn, offering little cover. To their left, a passage branched off, leading down. To their right, the path continued toward the main exit, but it passed through an open area where the goblins had been storing their loot.

"We can't go right," Kaelen said quietly. He was leaning against the wall, his eyes closed, but his voice was sharp with alertness. "The goblin who fled... he's going to lead them back here. If we take the open route, we'll be seen. If we take the left, we're going down. Into the dark. Into the unknown."

"Into the exit," Elara corrected. She had been tracing the floor with her finger, her eyes narrowed. "There's a draft. It's coming from below. And there's a vibration. Faint, but steady. Like something is moving below us. Or above us."

"Or the structure settling," Maren said. "Old stone cracks. It's not magic. It's just a ruin."

"Maybe," Elara said. "But we don't know. And we can't afford to guess."

Elias looked at the group. They were tired. They were hurt. They were carrying two people who were not going to make it easy. But they were alive. And they had the job to finish.

"We take the left," Elias said. "It's slower. It's darker. But it's quieter. And if the goblins are moving, they're moving toward the exit, not toward the cellar. We go down, we wait, and we listen. If they pass, we move. If they don't, we wait."

Torin nodded. He shifted Hobb again, the pain in his shoulder flaring up, sharp and bright. "I can do that," he said. "But I need a break. Every twenty steps. Or I'm going to pass out."

"Agreed," Maren said. She adjusted her grip on Nella, who had stopped trembling enough to look around, her eyes wide and unseeing. "I need a break too. My shoulder is screaming at me, and I'm not sure if it's the axle or the fact that I'm carrying a woman who weighs less than my hammer."

A small, dry laugh escaped Kaelen. It was a fragile sound, barely audible, but it broke the tension. "I can carry the hammer," he said. "If you need to put it down."

Maren looked at him, her eyes sharp. "No. I'm good. Just... keep an eye on the dark. That's your job."

Kaelen nodded. He straightened up, his eyes opening, scanning the shadows. "I'll do it."

Elias turned to Elara. "You're with me. Keep listening. If you hear anything, you tell us. Immediately."

Elara nodded. She pulled a small vial of quartz dust from her satchel, holding it up to the faint light that seeped in from the cracks in the stone. The dust swirled, catching the gloom, revealing the faintest traces of movement in the air.

"Nothing yet," she said. "But the air is still. That's a good sign. Or a bad one. I can't tell."

Elias looked at the passage to the left. It was dark, cold, and inviting in a way that felt dangerous. But it was the only way forward.

"Let's go," he said.

They moved into the passage, one by one. The air grew colder, the darkness deeper. Elias kept his hand on Maren's shoulder, not to push, but to maintain the spacing Torin had established. They were a single, breathing unit, moving through the dark with the slow, deliberate grace of water finding its level.

And in the silence, they waited.

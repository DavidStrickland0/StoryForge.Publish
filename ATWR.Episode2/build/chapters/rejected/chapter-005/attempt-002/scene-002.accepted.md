The silence in the main hall was heavy, a physical weight that pressed against Elias’s eardrums. It was not the silence of peace; it was the held breath of a predator waiting for the trap to spring. He could hear the wet, shuffling footsteps below, the muffled shouts of goblins arguing over a fallen stone, and the occasional clatter of a dropped weapon. They were confused. They were looking for the source of the noise. They were not looking for the door.

Elara stood before the oak door, her hand resting on the iron latch. She gave a sharp, single nod. The lock was engaged from the outside, but the mechanism was old, the rust flaking off under her thumb. It would hold for a time. It was enough.

Kaelen leaned out of the broken window, his face pale in the moonlight. The night air was cold, biting at his skin, but he did not seem to notice. He scanned the yard below, his eyes sharp despite his exhaustion. The goblins were still clustered in the cellar entrance, their heads tilted as if listening to the stone itself.

Elias turned to Torin, who was sitting on the edge of the stone table, his sword resting across his knees. Torin’s face was grim, the lines around his mouth deepened by fatigue. He looked at Hobb, who was leaning against the wall, his breathing still shallow but steady.

"We need to move," Elias said, his voice low and steady. "Not yet. But soon. The goblins will realize the stone was a distraction. They will come up. When they do, they will find the hall empty. Or they will find us waiting."

Torin looked up, his eyes meeting Elias’s. "Waiting is better," he said. "It’s less... messy."

Elias nodded. He moved to the center of the room, his mind working through the possibilities. They had the advantage of position. They had the advantage of surprise. But they did not have the advantage of numbers. The goblins were many, and they were angry. If they came up in a surge, the narrow entrance to the shaft would become a bottleneck, a place where a single swing of a hammer or a stroke of a sword could turn the tide.

But they did not want a fight. Not yet. They wanted Skrit Ironjaw. They wanted the head of the snake cut off before the body could strike.

He looked at Elara, who was watching him with a quiet intensity. She was a scholar, a thinker. She saw patterns where others saw chaos.

"Elara," Elias said. "The goblins are below. They are looking for the stone. If we can make them think the stone is moving, they will follow it."

Elara raised an eyebrow. "You mean a second distraction?"

"I mean a controlled one," Elias corrected. "We don't need to hide. We need to lead. We need to draw them away from the main hall, into the side corridors, where the terrain is less favorable for them. Where we can control the pace."

Torin frowned. "You want to play tag with goblins?"

"I want to play chess," Elias said. "And I need a piece."

He looked at Kaelen. The mage was still at the window, his arms crossed over his chest. He was not looking at the goblins below. He was looking at Elias.

"I'm out," Kaelen said, his voice flat. "I have no magic. I have no light. I have a headache that feels like it's trying to split my skull in two. If I move, I might collapse. If I collapse, I might wake up screaming. Which would be very rude to the goblins, but also very inconvenient for us."

"We don't need magic," Elias said. "We need sound. We need a sound that sounds like something large and heavy is moving. Something that sounds like it's coming from the left corridor."

Kaelen looked at him, then at the table. On the table, there were several objects: a stack of stolen books, a pile of grain sacks, a broken chair, and a large, heavy iron pot.

"The pot," Kaelen said. He picked it up. It was heavier than it looked. "If I roll it, it will make a noise. But it will stop. It won't keep moving."

"Then we keep it moving," Torin said. He stood up, his shoulder stiffening, but his voice was firm. "I can push it. I can guide it. But I need someone to watch the entrance. If they come up while I'm focused on the pot, I'm finished."

"I'll watch the entrance," Maren said. She was standing by Nella, her hand resting on the hilt of her hammer. Her face was calm, but her eyes were sharp. "I can hold the line. Just don't let them get past me."

Elias nodded. He looked at the group, at the tension in the air, at the fear in Nella’s eyes, at the pain in Hobb’s face. They were tired. They were hurt. But they were together. And that was enough.

"Elara," Elias said. "You stay with the captives. If anything happens, if the goblins get past Maren, you take them to the window. Can you climb?"

Elara nodded. "I can climb. But I won't be fast."

"That's fine," Elias said. "We don't need you to be fast. We need you to be ready."

He looked at Kaelen. "You stay with me. We move the pot. We lead them into the left corridor. Then we wait."

Kaelen nodded. He set the pot on the floor. It made a dull, heavy thud.

Elias took a deep breath. He could feel his heart beating in his chest, a steady, strong rhythm. He was afraid. He was tired. But he was ready.

"Let's do this," he said.

He moved to the pot, his hand resting on the cold iron. He pushed. The pot rolled, making a low, rumbling sound that echoed in the stone walls. He guided it toward the left corridor, his movements slow and deliberate.

Kaelen followed, his eyes on the entrance to the cellar. He was watching for movement, for the flash of a spear, for the glint of a blade.

Below, the goblins stopped shouting. They were listening.

One of them let out a sharp, guttural cry.

They were coming up.

Elias rolled the pot further, his hand sweating on the iron. The sound was growing louder, filling the hall, filling the corridor. It was a lie, a small, heavy lie that promised safety and offered none.

But it was all they had.

He looked at Torin, who was watching the entrance with a grim focus. He looked at Maren, who was standing by the captives, her hammer resting on her shoulder. He looked at Elara, who was holding Nella’s hand, her face pale but steady.

They were ready.

The goblins were coming.

Elias pushed the pot into the shadows of the left corridor, and then he stepped back, his hand on his sword.

He was ready.

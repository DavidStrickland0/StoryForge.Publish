The corridor narrowed, the ceiling dropping until Torin had to tilt his head back to avoid the rough-hewn flagstones. The air grew heavier, thick with the scent of damp rot and something sharper, like rusted iron. Elias kept his hand on Maren's shoulder, not to push, but to maintain the spacing Torin had established. They were a single, breathing unit, moving through the dark with the slow, deliberate grace of water finding its level.

Torin paused at a junction where the stone floor split into three paths. To the left, the passage descended steeply, the air rushing out in a cold, steady draft. Straight ahead, the corridor continued into a gloom so complete that even Kaelen's dimmed light seemed to die a few feet out. To the right, a narrow fissure in the wall, barely wide enough for a man to slip through sideways.

Elara crouched beside the floor, her fingers tracing the dust. "The dust is disturbed here," she murmured. "But not by feet. By dragging." She looked up, her face pale in the faint glow of Kaelen's gauntlet. "Someone was dragged out of that passage. Recently. The drag marks are fresh. The mud is still damp."

Torin gave a sharp nod. He stepped into the fissure, his body twisting to navigate the tight space. He emerged on the other side, where the passage opened into a wider, dimly lit chamber. The light here was different—yellow, flickering, casting long, dancing shadows against the stone walls.

Elias moved past him, stepping carefully into the new space. The chamber was small, circular, and cluttered with the debris of goblin life. There were piles of stolen grain sacks, half-eaten food, and broken tools. In the center, a small fire burned in a ring of flat stones, its flames low and controlled.

Torin nodded. He moved to the left, positioning himself to cover the exit if the goblins panicked. Maren moved to the right, her hammer resting against her leg, ready to swing. Elara and Kaelen stayed close to Elias, a wall of quiet competence behind him.

The goblin holding the knife stared at Elias, then at the sword hilt visible at Elias's hip, then at Maren, who stood to the right with her hammer resting casually against her thigh. The hammer was larger than the goblin. The realization seemed to hit him with the force of a physical blow. His shoulders slumped, the tension draining out of his frame, leaving him looking small and incredibly tired.

Elias signaled to the group. Torin moved first, his sword drawn now, the steel singing softly as it left the scabbard. He didn't run; he flowed, covering the angle between the goblins and the door. Maren followed, her eyes scanning the shadows behind the crates, looking for a third goblin, a hidden blade, a trap. She found nothing. The room was small, cramped, and smelled of wet dog and ash.

Elara stepped forward, withdrawing a small vial from her satchel. The liquid inside was clear, pulsing with a faint, internal glow. "Water of life," she said, her voice tight with urgency. "It's not a cure. It's a stabilizer. It will slow the shock and keep his heart steady, but it won't fix the leg. We need to get him out. Now."

Maren helped Nella to her feet. The blacksmith was weak, her legs unsteady, but she kept her chin up. She looked at the goblins, who were huddled in the corner, their heads bowed, making small, appeasing gestures. She didn't look at them with anger, or with triumph. She looked at them with the cold, pragmatic assessment of a soldier who had seen enough to know that fear was a more reliable ally than loyalty.

Elias stood for a moment, looking at the room. The fire was dying down, the embers glowing red in the darkness. The air was still cold, still damp. But the feeling of dread had shifted. It was no longer the threat of the unknown; it was the weight of responsibility. They had found the captives. They had assessed the damage. Now, they had to carry them out.

It was in the narrow, lightless throat of the corridor, with the taste of wet stone and old blood still clinging to the back of Elias's throat, that the weight of the moment finally settled. The adrenaline of the initial confrontation had burned off, leaving behind a colder, sharper clarity. They were moving, but they were not yet free. The keep was a beast with many jaws, and they were currently in the belly, carrying the prize.

Torin, carrying Hobb across his broad back, moved with a heavy, deliberate grace that spoke of years spent hauling gear and bodies. Each step was a negotiation with the uneven flagstones, a silent test of balance and endurance. He did not complain of the weight. He did not complain of the dark. He simply moved, his breathing a low, steady rhythm that seemed to anchor the erratic breathing of the man on his back.

Elias watched him, and for a second, a familiar, irksome tension tightened in his own chest. It was the old, professional friction: the desire to lead versus the reality of the physical burden. Elias could map the route, could deduce the goblin patrol patterns, could assign roles with the precision of a surgeon. But he could not carry Hobb. He could not lift the beam that might fall, or break the lock that might jam. He looked at Torin's shoulders, the way the muscle bunched and released with each careful step, and saw not a subordinate, but a pillar.

"Your back," Elias said. He pointed a gloved finger at the air beside Torin's head. "You're compensating. I see it in the way you shift your weight to the left. You're protecting your right side, or the shoulder, or something else that doesn't show in the light. And Maren's axle is going to make the stairs a nightmare if we have to climb. I don't want us to reach the exit and have you collapse because you've been holding your pain in like it's a secret."

Maren looked up. Her face was grim, her jaw set in that way that meant she was calculating angles and escape routes even while comforting a terrified civilian. "She's stable," Maren said. "Weak, scared, but stable. Hobb's leg is bad. A break. He's got a bruise on his cheek that looks like it's going to swell up into a purple bruise the size of a fist, but the rest of him is just tired. Elara's water did its job. He's not bleeding out. He's not dying. He's just... very, very hurt."

"Confused," Elara said from the shadows ahead. She had paused, her head tilted, listening to the silence beyond the immediate corridor. "They're shouting. Not at us. At each other. I hear the clatter of metal on metal. They're probably moving their loot, or changing shifts. One of them is crying. A high, thin sound. It's a child, or a young one. They're panicking."

Elara stepped forward, her satchel clinking softly as she produced her charcoal and a scrap of parchment. The faint glow of Kaelen's earlier magic was gone, but the memory of the light remained in her eyes. "The layout is simple," she said. "We are in the cellar. Above us is the living quarters. To our left, the exit. To our right, the chief's room. The goblins are in the center. If we move left, we pass through the open space. If we move right, we go through the door."

Everyone turned to him.

"I can't cast a spell," Kaelen continued, a dry humor returning to his face despite the pain. "But I can drop a stone. Or a coin. Or a piece of that broken axle, Maren, if you can spare a splinter. I can make a sound that sounds like a person falling. Or a goblin being dropped. It will draw their attention to one specific point."

They moved with the synchronized efficiency of a machine that had been oiled and calibrated. There was no shouting, no frantic gesturing. There was only the soft scrape of boots, the rustle of cloth, and the quiet, focused intensity of five people who understood that their survival depended on each other's competence.

Elias watched them work, and he felt the tension in his chest ease. It was not a release of danger. The goblins were still there. The keep was still hostile. But for the first time, they were not just reacting. They were acting. And that was enough.

Kaelen found a loose flagstone near the base of the wall, prying it free with a grunt of effort that seemed to cost him more than the stone's weight warranted. He held it up, the rough edge catching the faint, dying glow of his gauntlet. "This one," he said, his voice tight. "It's heavy enough to sound like a body."

Elias watched the mage's hand tremble slightly. It was a small, human detail in a moment that felt like a mechanical calculation. He didn't offer words of encouragement; he simply nodded, signaling the rest of the group to move into position.

Torin had already shifted Hobb back onto his shoulders. The cart driver groaned, a low, guttural sound that vibrated against Torin's leather armor, but he didn't complain. He adjusted his grip, his jaw set in a hard line as he fought the familiar, burning flare in his right shoulder. The pain was a constant, dull throb now, a background noise he had learned to ignore in favor of the sharper, more immediate demands of the task.

Maren stood by Nella, her hand firm on the blacksmith's elbow. Nella was pale, her eyes wide and unseeing, but she was standing. Her legs shook, but they held. "Can you walk?" Maren asked, her voice low and devoid of sympathy. It was a practical question, and Nella, to her credit, nodded instead of answering.

"Kaelen," Elias said. "When you drop it, we move. No hesitation. We go through the narrow passage on the right. If we hear goblins, we freeze. If we see them, we wait. Do not engage."

Kaelen stepped to the edge of the corridor, where the view opened into the wider, central hall where the goblins were currently clustered. He could hear them, a cacophony of high-pitched shrieking and the clatter of dropped pots. They were in chaos, a hive with its queen suddenly removed from the equation.

Elias drew a breath, the air biting cold and carrying the metallic tang of rust. He watched Elara, who crouched low, her charcoal poised over the parchment. She gave a single, sharp nod.

Kaelen let the stone go.

The sound was distinct. A heavy, wet thud, followed by a series of smaller clatters as it bounced off the flagstones. It echoed in the stone chamber with unnatural clarity.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then, the shouting changed pitch. The shrieking stopped, replaced by a rapid, urgent babbling. Footsteps—many of them, shuffling and frantic—began to move away from the center of the hall, converging on the sound of the fallen stone.

"Now," Elias whispered.

The group moved. It was a slow, deliberate shamble, dictated by the weight on Torin's back and the weakness in Nella's legs. They entered the narrow passage on the right, the walls closing in around them, the darkness pressing against their eyes.

Torin led, his boots finding purchase on the uneven steps. The passage was vertical, a tight shaft that spiraled upward into the gloom of the keep's upper levels. It was a claustrophobic climb, the stone rough and slick with condensation.

Maren brought up the rear, her hammer resting on her shoulder, the broken axle strapped to her back creaking with every shift of her weight. She was breathing hard, the effort of the climb bringing a flush of color to her pale cheeks.

Elias moved through the center, keeping a hand on the wall for balance, his eyes fixed on the dark space above. He could hear the goblins below, their shouts growing fainter, muffled by the stone and the distance. They were chasing a phantom, drawn by a stone that had not killed anyone but had successfully stolen their attention.

The climb was long. Torin's breathing became a ragged, wet sound. Maren's steps faltered, and she had to lean against the wall for a moment, her hand braced on the cold stone, her knuckles white.

"Keep moving," Elias murmured, not looking back. He didn't need to look to know that Maren was listening. She was always listening.

They reached the top of the shaft. The air here was different—drier, less heavy with the scent of rot. It smelled of old dust and dry wood.

Elias signaled for them to stop. They crouched at the threshold of the upper level, a wide, open space that served as the keep's main hall. It was empty. The goblins were still below, drawn into the cellar by the distraction.

The main hall was a vast, circular room, its ceiling lost in the darkness above. The floor was covered in a thick layer of dust, undisturbed for what looked like years. In the center of the room, a large, stone table stood, its surface littered with the remnants of meals long since gone cold.

Elias looked around. The room was defensible. The entrance from the shaft was narrow, a chokepoint that could be held by a single person. There were two other exits, a heavy oak door on the far side and a broken window on the left wall, but both were accessible only if one crossed the open floor.

"We're here," Torin said, his voice hoarse. He set Hobb down on the dusty floor, the man slumping against the wall with a soft sigh of relief.

Maren helped Nella to her feet, supporting her as the blacksmith swayed. "We're out of the cellar," Maren said, her voice steady despite the exhaustion in her eyes. "We're in the main hall. The goblins are below. We have the high ground, or at least the upper ground."

Elias nodded, his gaze shifting from the heavy oak door to the shattered window. "We need to secure the exit," he said. "Torin, can you move? Hobb?"

Hobb looked up, his eyes glassy with pain yet sharp with clarity. "I can walk," he rasped. "If I have to."

"You don't have to," Elias said. "But you can, if you want. Maren, keep Nella with you. Elara, check the door. Kaelen, watch the window."

They moved with the careful precision of a well-oiled machine. Elara examined the oak door, finding it locked from the outside. Kaelen peered out the broken window, the night air cold against his face.

The hall was silent. The goblins were still below, trapped in their own confusion.

Elias looked at his companions. They were tired, bruised, and exhausted. But they were safe. And they were together.

"We have time," Elias said. "Let's make it count."

The silence in the main hall was heavy, a physical weight that pressed against Elias's eardrums. It was not the silence of peace; it was the held breath of a predator waiting for the trap to spring. He could hear the wet, shuffling footsteps below, the muffled shouts of goblins arguing over a fallen stone, and the occasional clatter of a dropped weapon. They were confused. They were looking for the source of the noise. They were not looking for the door.

Elara stood before the oak door, her hand resting on the iron latch. She gave a sharp, single nod. The lock was engaged from the outside, but the mechanism was old, the rust flaking off under her thumb. It would hold for a time. It was enough.

Kaelen leaned out of the broken window, his face pale in the moonlight. The night air was cold, biting at his skin, but he did not seem to notice. He scanned the yard below, his eyes sharp despite his exhaustion. The goblins were still clustered in the cellar entrance, their heads tilted as if listening to the stone itself.

Elias turned to Torin, who was sitting on the edge of the stone table, his sword resting across his knees. Torin's face was grim, the lines around his mouth deepened by fatigue. He looked at Hobb, who was leaning against the wall, his breathing still shallow but steady.

"We need to move," Elias said, his voice low and steady. "Not yet. But soon. The goblins will realize the stone was a distraction. They will come up. When they do, they will find the hall empty. Or they will find us waiting."

Torin looked up, his eyes meeting Elias's. "Waiting is better," he said. "It's less... messy."

Elias nodded. He moved to the center of the room, his mind working through the possibilities. They had the advantage of position. They had the advantage of surprise. But they did not have the advantage of numbers. The goblins were many, and they were angry. If they came up in a surge, the narrow entrance to the shaft would become a bottleneck, a place where a single swing of a hammer or a stroke of a sword could turn the tide.

But they did not want a fight. Not yet. They wanted Skrit Ironjaw. They wanted the head of the snake cut off before the body could strike.

He looked at Elara, who was watching him with a quiet intensity. She was a scholar, a thinker. She saw patterns where others saw chaos.

"Elara," Elias said. "The goblins are below. They are looking for the stone. If we can make them think the stone is moving, they will follow it."

Elara raised an eyebrow. "You mean a second distraction?"

"I mean a controlled one," Elias corrected. "We don't need to hide. We need to lead. We need to draw them away from the main hall, into the side corridors, where the terrain is less favorable for them. Where we can control the pace."

Torin frowned. "You want to play tag with goblins?"

"I want to play chess," Elias said. "And I need a piece."

He looked at Kaelen. The mage was still at the window, his arms crossed over his chest. He was not looking at the goblins below. He was looking at Elias.

"I'm out," Kaelen said, his voice flat. "I have no magic. I have no light. I have a headache that feels like it's trying to split my skull in two. If I move, I might collapse. If I collapse, I might wake up screaming. Which would be very rude to the goblins, but also very inconvenient for us."

"We don't need magic," Elias said. "We need sound. We need a sound that sounds like something large and heavy is moving. Something that sounds like it's coming from the left corridor."

Kaelen looked at him, then at the table. On the table, there were several objects: a stack of stolen books, a pile of grain sacks, a broken chair, and a large, heavy iron pot.

"The pot," Kaelen said. He picked it up. It was heavier than it looked. "If I roll it, it will make a noise. But it will stop. It won't keep moving."

"Then we keep it moving," Torin said. He stood up, his shoulder stiffening, but his voice was firm. "I can push it. I can guide it. But I need someone to watch the entrance. If they come up while I'm focused on the pot, I'm finished."

"I'll watch the entrance," Maren said. She was standing by Nella, her hand resting on the hilt of her hammer. Her face was calm, but her eyes were sharp. "I can hold the line. Just don't let them get past me."

Elias nodded. He looked at the group, at the tension in the air, at the fear in Nella's eyes, at the pain in Hobb's face. They were tired. They were hurt. But they were together. And that was enough.

"Elara," Elias said. "You stay with the captives. If anything happens, if the goblins get past Maren, you take them to the window. Can you climb?"

Elara nodded. "I can climb. But I won't be fast."

"That's fine," Elias said. "We don't need you to be fast. We need you to be ready."

He looked at Kaelen. "You stay with me. We move the pot. We lead them into the left corridor. Then we wait."

Kaelen nodded. He set the pot on the floor. It made a dull, heavy thud.

Elias took a deep breath. He could feel his heart beating in his chest, a steady, strong rhythm. He was afraid. He was tired. But he was ready.

"Let's do this," he said.

He moved to the pot, his hand resting on the cold iron. He pushed. The pot rolled, making a low, rumbling sound that echoed in the stone walls. He guided it toward the left corridor, his movements slow and deliberate.

Kaelen followed, his eyes on the entrance to the cellar. He was watching for movement, for the flash of a spear, for the glint of a blade.

Below, the goblins stopped shouting. They were listening.

One of them let out a sharp, guttural cry.

They were coming up.

Elias rolled the pot further, his hand sweating on the iron. The sound was growing louder, filling the hall, filling the corridor. It was a lie, a small, heavy lie that promised safety and offered none.

But it was all they had.

He looked at Torin, who was watching the entrance with a grim focus. He looked at Maren, who was standing by the captives, her hammer resting on her shoulder. He looked at Elara, who was holding Nella's hand, her face pale but steady.

They were ready.

The goblins were coming.

Elias pushed the pot into the shadows of the left corridor, and then he stepped back, his hand on his sword.

He was ready.

The iron pot did not roll forever. It was heavy, a solid chunk of blackened metal that had once held stew for a dozen hungry raiders, and gravity, combined with the slight incline of the flagstones, gave it a momentum that felt almost sentient. It clattered into the left corridor, a dull, rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* that echoed off the wet stone walls.

Elias did not wait for the echo to die. He spun on his heel, his boots scraping against the damp floor, and signaled the others with a sharp chop of his hand.

Torin was already moving. He had abandoned his post at the cellar entrance, the old pain in his shoulder flaring with a white-hot spike as he shifted his weight, but he ignored it. He moved toward the center of the main hall, his sword drawn, the steel catching the faint, ambient light from the high windows. He positioned himself between the left corridor and the main shaft, a broad wall of leather and steel.

Maren did not move toward the center. She stepped back, placing herself directly in front of Nella and Hobb. The blacksmith was trembling, her knees knocking together, but she did not collapse. Maren's hand rested on the haft of her hammer, her fingers tight, her knuckles white. She looked at the cellar entrance, then at the left corridor, her eyes calculating angles, distances, and the precise moment when fear would turn into action.

Elara had climbed to the edge of the stone table, her back against the wall, her eyes fixed on the window. The window was high, a narrow slit of glass that looked out onto the rain-slicked night. It was not an escape route for Hobb, whose leg was broken, but it was a possibility. A hope. She kept her hands on the window frame, ready to unlatch it if the goblins broke through Maren's line.

Kaelen stood beside Elias, his face gray, his breath coming in short, shallow gasps. He looked at the left corridor, where the sound of the pot had finally ceased, replaced by a silence that was heavier than the noise.

"They're coming," Kaelen whispered. His voice was thin, strained. "I can hear them. Multiple sets of feet. And... shouting."

Elias nodded. He could hear it too. The wet slap of bare feet on stone. The guttural, panicked yells of goblins who had been broken by the darkness and the silence, now driven by the promise of a noise that sounded like a threat.

They were coming to investigate. They were coming to see who had made the sound.

And they were coming to the center of the hall, where the light was brightest, and the exit was widest.

Elias took a step forward. Then another. He moved into the center of the main hall, his sword unsheathed, the blade held low, the tip pointing toward the left corridor. He stood in the open, in the light, a single figure of defiance in the middle of the goblins' domain.

He was not afraid. He was tired. He was hungry. He was ready to be done with this.

The goblins emerged from the left corridor, one by one, then in a cluster. They were hunched, their eyes wide and yellow, their hands clutching crude weapons. Spears, knives, a broken axe. They stopped at the edge of the light, their movements jerky, uncertain. They looked at Elias. They looked at Torin, who stood to his left, his sword drawn. They looked at Maren, who stood to his right, her hammer resting on her shoulder, her posture rigid, her eyes cold.

They looked at the captives.

Nella stood behind Maren, her face pale, her hands clasped tightly in front of her. Hobb leaned against the wall, his face a mask of pain, but his eyes were open, alert. They were alive. They were free. And they were being watched by a group of armed men who had the upper hand.

The goblins hesitated.

The silence stretched, thin and taut. The rain drummed against the roof, a steady, rhythmic sound that filled the void left by the goblins' hesitation.

Elias spoke. His voice was low, steady, and clear. It carried across the hall, bouncing off the stone walls, filling the space with a quiet authority.

"Skrit Ironjaw," he said. "I know you are here. I know you are the one who gave the orders. I know you are the one who held them."

He paused. He let the name hang in the air, a weight, a threat.

"I am not here to kill you. I am here to take you. And I am here to take your friends. And I am here to take your keep."

He looked at the goblins. He looked at their weapons, their fear, their uncertainty. He looked at their leader, who was not visible, but was surely listening, surely watching, surely calculating.

"You can come out and surrender," Elias said. "You can give up your weapons, your loot, and your claim to this keep. And you can walk out of here, into the rain, into the night, and into the hands of the village guard."

He paused again. He let the words settle.

"Or you can stay here. And I can come to you. And I can take you. And I can take your keep. And I can take your life."

The goblins looked at each other. They looked at their weapons. They looked at Elias.

The silence stretched again.

Then, a sound. A soft, shuffling sound. From the shadows, from the darkness, from the place where the leader should be.

A goblin stepped forward.

He was tall for a goblin, his skin a mottled gray-green, his eyes sharp and intelligent. He wore a leather tunic, patched and repaired, and a belt with a short sword. He held no weapon in his hands. His hands were open, his palms out, a gesture of surrender.

He looked at Elias. He looked at Torin. He looked at Maren.

He looked at the captives.

And then, he lowered his head.

"I am Skrit Ironjaw," he said. His voice was rough, tired, and resigned. "I am here. I am yours."

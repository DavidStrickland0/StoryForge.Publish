The corridor narrowed, the ceiling dropping until Torin had to tilt his head back to avoid the rough-hewn flagstones. The air grew heavier, thick with the scent of damp rot and something sharper, like rusted iron. Elias kept his hand on Maren's shoulder, not to push, but to maintain the spacing Torin had established. They were a single, breathing unit, moving through the dark with the slow, deliberate grace of water finding its level.

Torin paused at a junction where the stone floor split into three paths. To the left, the passage descended steeply, the air rushing out in a cold, steady draft. Straight ahead, the corridor continued into a gloom so complete that even Kaelen's dimmed light seemed to die a few feet out. To the right, a narrow fissure in the wall, barely wide enough for a man to slip through sideways.

Torin gave a sharp nod. He stepped into the fissure, his body twisting to navigate the tight space. He emerged on the other side, where the passage opened into a wider, dimly lit chamber. The light here was different—yellow, flickering, casting long, dancing shadows against the stone walls.

Elias moved past him, stepping carefully into the new space. The chamber was small, circular, and cluttered with the debris of goblin life. There were piles of stolen grain sacks, half-eaten food, and broken tools. In the center, a small fire burned in a ring of flat stones, its flames low and controlled.

He opened his eyes and fixed on Torin. The soldier's jaw was clenched, his hand resting on the pommel of his sword. He shifted his gaze to Elara, who was staring at the goblins, her face a mask of concentration. Then he looked at Maren, who was watching the door, her eyes sharp and bright with a fierce, protective anger.

Torin nodded. He moved to the left, positioning himself to cover the exit if the goblins panicked. Maren moved to the right, her hammer resting against her leg, ready to swing. Elara and Kaelen stayed close to Elias, a wall of quiet competence behind him.

The goblin holding the knife stared at Elias, then at the sword hilt visible at Elias's hip, then at Maren, who stood to the right with her hammer resting casually against her thigh. The hammer was larger than the goblin. The realization seemed to hit him with the force of a physical blow. His shoulders slumped, the tension draining out of his frame, leaving him looking small and incredibly tired.

Elias signaled to the group. Torin moved first, his sword drawn now, the steel singing softly as it left the scabbard. He didn't run; he flowed, covering the angle between the goblins and the door. Maren followed, her eyes scanning the shadows behind the crates, looking for a third goblin, a hidden blade, a trap. She found nothing. The room was small, cramped, and smelled of wet dog and ash.

Maren helped Nella to her feet. The blacksmith was weak, her legs unsteady, but she kept her chin up. She looked at the goblins, who were huddled in the corner, their heads bowed, making small, appeasing gestures. She didn't look at them with anger, or with triumph. She looked at them with the cold, pragmatic assessment of a soldier who had seen enough to know that fear was a more reliable ally than loyalty.

Elias stood for a moment, looking at the room. The fire was dying down, the embers glowing red in the darkness. The air was still cold, still damp. But the feeling of dread had shifted. It was no longer the threat of the unknown; it was the weight of responsibility. They had found the captives. They had assessed the damage. Now, they had to carry them out.

He turned to the group. "Torin, lead. Maren, rear. Kaelen, stay close to me. Elara, watch the exits."

It was in the narrow, lightless throat of the corridor, with the taste of wet stone and old blood still clinging to the back of Elias's throat, that the weight of the moment finally settled. The adrenaline of the initial confrontation had burned off, leaving behind a colder, sharper clarity. They were moving, but they were not yet free. The keep was a beast with many jaws, and they were currently in the belly, carrying the prize.

Torin, carrying Hobb across his broad back, moved with a heavy, deliberate grace that spoke of years spent hauling gear and bodies. Each step was a negotiation with the uneven flagstones, a silent test of balance and endurance. He did not complain of the weight. He did not complain of the dark. He simply moved, his breathing a low, steady rhythm that seemed to anchor the erratic breathing of the man on his back.

Elias watched him, and for a second, a familiar, irksome tension tightened in his own chest. It was the old, professional friction: the desire to lead versus the reality of the physical burden. Elias could map the route, could deduce the goblin patrol patterns, could assign roles with the precision of a surgeon. But he could not carry Hobb. He could not lift the beam that might fall, or break the lock that might jam. He looked at Torin's shoulders, the way the muscle bunched and released with each careful step, and saw not a subordinate, but a pillar.

"Your back," Elias said. He pointed a gloved finger at the air beside Torin's head. "You're compensating. I see it in the way you shift your weight to the left. You're protecting your right side, or the shoulder, or something else that doesn't show in the light. And Maren's axle is going to make the stairs a nightmare if we have to climb. I don't want us to reach the exit and have you collapse because you've been holding your pain in like it's a secret."

Maren looked up. Her face was grim, her jaw set in that way that meant she was calculating angles and escape routes even while comforting a terrified civilian. "She's stable," Maren said. "Weak, scared, but stable. Hobb's leg is bad. A break. He's got a bruise on his cheek that looks like it's going to swell up into a purple bruise the size of a fist, but the rest of him is just tired. Elara's water did its job. He's not bleeding out. He's not dying. He's just... very, very hurt."

"And the goblins?" Elias asked.

"Confused," Elara said from the shadows ahead. She had paused, her head tilted, listening to the silence beyond the immediate corridor. "They're shouting. Not at us. At each other. I hear the clatter of metal on metal. They're probably moving their loot, or changing shifts. One of them is crying. A high, thin sound. It's a child, or a young one. They're panicking."

Elias exchanged a glance with Kaelen. The mage was leaning against the wall, his gauntlet dark now, his eyes closed. He looked like he had aged ten years in the last hour.

"Kaelen," Elias said. "How are you?"

"Empty," Kaelen said. His voice was hoarse. "I used the last of my light to see the door. I used the last of my smoke to mask our scent when we entered. Now I am a man with a sword and a bad back. If you need me to throw a fireball, I can try, but I think it will be more of a spark. A very angry spark."

"We don't need fire," Maren said, rising to her feet. She moved to Torin's side, her hand resting briefly on his shoulder in a gesture of pure, practical solidarity. "We need speed. And we need to know where the goblins are not."

Elara stepped forward, her satchel clinking softly as she produced her charcoal and a scrap of parchment. The faint glow of Kaelen's earlier magic was gone, but the memory of the light remained in her eyes. "The layout is simple," she said. "We are in the cellar. Above us is the living quarters. To our left, the exit. To our right, the chief's room. The goblins are in the center. If we move left, we pass through the open space. If we move right, we go through the door."

"Left is faster," Torin said.

"Left is risky," Maren countered. "Open space means no cover. If they see us, we're sitting ducks in the dark. Right is slower, but it's narrow. A corridor. We can move through it in a line, and if they see us, we can fight one at a time."

Elias looked at the two of them, and then at Kaelen, who was opening his eyes. The mage looked at the parchment Elara was sketching, and then at the dark hallway ahead.

"I have an idea," Kaelen said. It was quiet, but it cut through the tension. "I can't make light. But I can make noise."

Everyone turned to him.

"I can't cast a spell," Kaelen continued, a dry humor returning to his face despite the pain. "But I can drop a stone. Or a coin. Or a piece of that broken axle, Maren, if you can spare a splinter. I can make a sound that sounds like a person falling. Or a goblin being dropped. It will draw their attention to one specific point."

"Which point?" Elias asked.

"The far end of the corridor," Kaelen said. "If they run to investigate, the corridor will be empty. We pass through. It's not magic. It's just... physics. And a little bit of theater."

Maren looked at Torin. Torin looked at Elias.

"It's a gamble," Torin said.

"It's a plan," Elias corrected. "And it's the one we have. Kaelen, find a stone. Maren, check your pockets. Elara, mark the route. Torin, ready Hobb for movement. Maren, get Nella on her feet."

They moved with the synchronized efficiency of a machine that had been oiled and calibrated. There was no shouting, no frantic gesturing. There was only the soft scrape of boots, the rustle of cloth, and the quiet, focused intensity of five people who understood that their survival depended on each other's competence.

Elias watched them work, and he felt the tension in his chest ease. It was not a release of danger. The goblins were still there. The keep was still hostile. But for the first time, they were not just reacting. They were acting. And that was enough.

Kaelen found what he needed in the loose gravel of the corridor floor. It was a chunk of flint, sharp-edged and heavy, no larger than a walnut. He held it in his palm, feeling the rough texture against his skin, and listened. Above them, the sound of the goblins' panic had shifted from a chaotic shrieking to a more organized, grumbling fury. A door slammed. Then another.

Elias watched Kaelen's hand. "Ready?" he whispered.

Kaelen nodded. He didn't look tired anymore, or at least he wasn't showing it. He looked like a man about to perform a minor magic trick for a skeptical audience. "One count," he said.

Elias looked at Torin, who was bracing himself against the wall, one hand gripping Hobb's collar, the other pressing firmly against his own shoulder. The old injury was a visible thing now, a wince that tightened the muscles of his neck. Maren stood beside Nella, her hand resting on the woman's back, her eyes fixed on the ceiling as if she could see through the stone to the living quarters above.

"Three," Elias said.

"Two."

"One."

Kaelen dropped the flint.

It struck the stone floor with a sharp, brittle *clack*, the sound ricocheting down the narrow passage. For a heartbeat, silence held. Then, from the upper level, a crash erupted, like a bucket of rocks hurled against a wall. A door swung open. Shouts burst forth, high and shrill, carrying the specific dialect of goblin urgency that Elias had learned to recognize. The sounds of movement were immediate and heavy, boots scrambling on wood, the clatter of weapons being drawn.

"Now," Elias said.

They moved.

Torin lifted Hobb again, a grunt escaping his teeth as the weight settled onto his back. The pain in his shoulder flared, a hot wire pulled tight, but he didn't stop. He moved with a grim, mechanical efficiency, his boots finding the uneven steps of the stone staircase that cut through the center of the keep's lower level. Maren followed, guiding Nella with a firm grip on her elbow. The blacksmith's legs were still weak, her steps a shuffle, but she walked. She was too stubborn to do otherwise.

Elias and Kaelen brought up the rear. Elias kept his sword drawn, the blade low and ready, his eyes scanning the upper landing. The staircase was a narrow throat, a single file of stone steps that opened into the main floor of the keep. The air here was different. It was warmer, drier, and smelled of woodsmoke, unwashed bodies, and the stale, sweet scent of rotting grain.

They emerged onto the main floor.

The space was larger than the cellar, a rectangular hall with a high ceiling supported by rough-hewn timber beams. To their left, a series of low, crude beds made of stacked crates and furs occupied the corner. To their right, a heavy oak table stood in the center of the room, cluttered with the spoils of their recent raids: sacks of flour, a pile of stolen silverware, and a collection of broken tools.

And there were goblins.

Five of them. They were gathered around the table, their attention fixed on the entrance to the room. They were not armed. Or rather, they were armed with the things they had on hand: a cleaver, a bent dagger, a thick stick. They were huddled together, their eyes wide and fearful, looking at the party with the same mixture of terror and confusion that Elias had seen in the cellar.

One of the goblins, larger than the others, with a scar running from his forehead to his chin, stepped forward. He held the cleaver loosely, his hands trembling.

"Who are you?" the goblin asked. His voice was a rasp, thick with fear. "What do you want?"

Elias stepped forward, his sword still sheathed. He kept his hands visible, his posture open but firm. "We are here to take our people home," he said. His voice was calm, steady, carrying the weight of authority without the need for volume. "And we are here to end the raids."

The goblin flinched. The word *raids* seemed to hit him harder than a physical blow. He looked at his companions, who were shrinking back, their eyes darting around the room as if looking for an escape route.

"The chief," the goblin stammered. "He is... he is in his room. He will not speak to you. He is angry."

"Angry is not a reason to keep holding people captive," Maren said. Her voice was flat, dangerous. She had stepped forward, placing herself between the goblins and the entrance to the room at the far end of the hall. "And it is not a reason to steal from farmers."

The scarred goblin looked at Maren, then at Torin, who was standing with Hobb securely on his back, his face a mask of cold determination. He looked at Kaelen, who stood with his hands in his pockets, looking bored. He looked at Elias, who was watching him with eyes that promised nothing good.

The goblin lowered the cleaver.

"He is in the room," the goblin said, pointing a shaking finger toward the heavy door at the far end of the hall. "The door is locked. But... he is not alone. There are others in there. Guards."

Elias looked at the door. It was solid oak, banded with iron, and it was closed. But it was not locked. He could see the gap at the bottom, a sliver of darkness that breathed out a scent of leather and old metal.

"How many guards?" Elias asked.

"Two," the goblin said. "And the chief."

Elias looked at Torin. The soldier was still holding Hobb, his breathing steady, his eyes sharp. He looked at Maren, who was watching the goblins with a predator's patience. He looked at Kaelen, who was picking at a piece of flint that had stuck to his shoe.

"We go in," Elias said.

"Alone?" Kaelen asked, raising an eyebrow.

"Together," Elias corrected. "But quietly. We don't want a fight. We want a conversation."

"A conversation," Maren repeated, her voice dripping with skepticism. "With a goblin who has been stealing our grain and breaking our people."

"Exactly," Elias said. "We have the advantage. We have their people. We have their fear. We use it."

He turned to the goblins. "You stay here," he said. "You do not move. You do not make a sound. And you do not run. If I see you move, I will not be as polite as I am now."

The goblins nodded, their heads bowed, their bodies trembling.

Elias turned to the door. He placed his hand on the iron handle and pushed. The door swung open on silent hinges, revealing a small, cluttered room.

Inside, two goblins stood guard. They were larger than the others, their muscles tensed, their weapons raised. And behind them, sitting on a rough-hewn chair, was a goblin who was different. He was older, his skin a deeper green, his eyes sharp and intelligent. He held a sword, not a crude knife, but a proper, well-kept blade.

He looked up at Elias, and a smile spread across his face.

"I was wondering when you would come," he said. His voice was smooth, cultured, and utterly devoid of fear. "Sit down, please. We have much to discuss."

Skrit did not move. He sat in the chair with the easy, sprawling posture of a man who had never been told to stop doing something. His hands rested on the pommel of his sword, fingers loose, relaxed. The blade was short, curved, and gleamed with a polish that stood out starkly against the grime of the room. It was a good weapon. Better than most of the steel Elias had seen in the village armory.

Elias kept his sword sheathed, though his hand rested lightly on the hilt. He felt the weight of his companions' attention pressing against his back. Torin was a statue of coiled tension, while Hobb's body hung across his shoulders like a dead, aching sack of meat. Maren's fingers were white-knuckled around Nella's elbow, the blacksmith shivering against her like a leaf in a gale. Kaelen stood behind them, a piece of flint resting in his palm, his face a mask of exhausted patience.

"We have no time for discussion," Elias said. His voice was flat, stripped of the courtesy Skrit seemed to expect. "We have your people. We have your goods. We are here to end this. Skrit Ironjaw, stand up. Now."

Skrit blinked. The smile did not leave his face, but it tightened at the edges. "You are being very rude," he said. "And very loud. Do you know how much work it is to keep a band together? I have to feed them. I have to keep them warm. And you walk in here, trampling the dust, demanding I stand up as if I were a dog."

"One," Kaelen said.

The word was soft, but it cut through the air with a sharpness that made the two goblins standing by the door flinch. Their spears, which had been raised in a clumsy threat, dropped to the floor with a clatter.

"Two," Kaelen said.

Skrit's eyes shifted. They moved past Elias, past Torin, to the mage. He looked at the flint in Kaelen's hand. He looked at the darkness of the corridor behind the party, where the shadows were deep and absolute. He looked at the two guards, who were now crouching low, their weapons abandoned.

Skrit sighed. It was a long, weary sound, the sigh of a man dealing with a bureaucratic inconvenience rather than a life-or-death threat. He stood up, slowly, deliberately. He did not draw his sword. He kept his hands where he could see them, palms open.

"Fine," he said. "I am standing. What do you want?"

Elias took a step forward, then stopped. He looked at Skrit, really looked at him, for the first time. The goblin was smaller than Elias had expected, but dense. His eyes were not the wild, yellow panic of the others; they were calculating, cold, and intelligent. He was not a mindless raider. He was a manager. And managers could be reasoned with, if the reasoning was built on the right kind of leverage.

"You want to know what I want?" Elias asked. He gestured to Torin, to Hobb. "I want to know why you are hurting them. They are not soldiers. They are not spies. They are farmers. And you are stealing their food, their tools, their lives. Why?"

Skrit looked at Hobb, then at Nella. His expression was one of mild confusion. "They are useful," he said. "Nella knows how to fix a plow. Hobb can drive a cart. We have grain. We have livestock. We need to move them. They are... assets."

"Assets," Elias repeated. The word tasted like ash in his mouth. "You are holding them captive to work for you. That is not an asset. That is slavery. And it is going to end tonight."

Skrit tilted his head. "Or it is going to end with you dead. I have two guards. I have a sword. I have the high ground. And you have... what? A broken stick and a tired man?"

He pointed a long, green finger at Torin. The soldier didn't flinch, but his jaw clenched. The pain in his shoulder was a hot, sharp line, but he didn't let it show. Not to Skrit. Not to anyone.

Elias looked at Kaelen. The mage gave a barely perceptible nod.

"Three," Kaelen whispered.

The flint left Kaelen's hand. It did not fly. It dropped, a simple, gravity-driven arc, and struck the stone floor with a sharp, ringing *clack*. The sound was small, but in the silence of the room, it was deafening.

Skrit's eyes widened. He looked at the flint on the floor. Then he looked at Kaelen. Then he looked at the door.

He understood.

He understood that Kaelen had not just dropped a rock. He had dropped a signal. And he understood that if the signal was followed by more noise, more light, more force, the two guards by the door would not be the only ones to react. The goblins in the main hall, the ones who were currently cowering in the corner, would panic. And panicked goblins were dangerous.

Skrit looked at Elias. The smile was gone. In its place was a cold, hard calculation. He was weighing his options. He could fight. He could run. He could negotiate.

He chose to negotiate.

"You have a point," Skrit said, his voice low. "You do have a point. I am... not a hero. I am a survivor. And survival requires resources. I am willing to talk. But I am not willing to be a prisoner. I have people who depend on me. If I am captured, they will scatter. And if they scatter, they will be more dangerous. They will go to other villages. They will steal more. They will kill more."

Elias stared at him. It was a classic gambit. The villain who argues that his defeat will cause more chaos than his survival. It was a lie, but it was a plausible one. And Elias knew that Skrit was not lying about the danger. If the band broke up, the goblins would indeed become more dangerous. They would be feral, desperate, and unaccountable.

But they would also be defeated.

"No," Elias said. "If you are captured, you will be taken to Pinehollow. You will stand trial. And the band will be disbanded. Not because you say so, but because there will be no one left to lead them. They will go home. To their caves. To their families. They will stop raiding. Not because they are good, but because they are tired. And because you will be gone."

Skrit was silent for a long moment. He looked at the flint on the floor. He looked at his guards, who were now on their knees, their heads bowed. He looked at his sword, and then at the captives.

He saw the truth. He saw that his power was not in his sword, or his guards, or his intelligence. His power was in his position. And his position was gone.

"Very well," Skrit said. He sheathed his sword. The sound of the blade sliding into the scabbard was a final, decisive click. "I will come with you. But I will not be chained. And I will not be killed. If you want me to stop the raids, you must let me stop them. My way."

Elias looked at Kaelen. The mage gave a sharp nod.

"Deal," Elias said.

The tension in the room broke, not with a bang, but with a sigh. Skrit Ironjaw, the leader of the goblin band, the terror of Pinehollow, the man who had stolen their lives and their livelihood, had surrendered. Not to violence. Not to magic. But to logic. To the simple, unyielding fact that he had lost.

Elias turned to his companions. "Let's go," he said. "We have a long way to walk."

"Let's go," Elias said, his voice flat and final. He turned his back on the chief, a deliberate dismissal that was louder than any shout. "Maren, keep her steady. Torin, if his shoulder buckles, we stop. We don't rush a man who's already broken."

They moved toward the door, a slow, heavy procession that seemed to stretch the seconds. The air in the room was thick with the scent of wet fur and old iron, a smell that clung to the back of Elias's throat. He could feel the weight of the moment pressing against his ribs, the solid, undeniable fact that the hardest part was over, and the boring, painful part was beginning.

Skrit Ironjaw did not follow immediately. He stood in the center of his room, looking small against the backdrop of stolen grain sacks and broken furniture. He looked at his two kneeling guards, then at the party's retreating forms. There was no anger in his eyes, only a profound, exhausted relief. He reached up and unclasped the heavy leather strap of his bow, letting it drop to the floor with a dull thud. The sound echoed in the silence, a final punctuation mark to the end of his reign.

Elias paused at the threshold, his hand resting on the doorframe. He didn't turn around. "Leave your weapons where they are," he called out, his voice carrying easily in the stillness. "We are not your friends, Skrit. But we are not going to kill you in the dark. If you want to live to see a trial, you walk with us. If you try to run, you will find that we are much faster than you think, and much harder to stop."

It was a bluff, mostly. They were exhausted, injured, and carrying a dead weight. But Skrit didn't know that. He only knew that the man who had just dismantled his entire power structure with a few words was now standing in the doorway, waiting.

The goblin chief picked up his sword. He sheathed it with a slow, deliberate clink, the sound of steel sliding into a worn leather scabbard. He straightened his spine, the movement stiff and painful, and then he began to walk.

The corridor outside was cold. The dampness seeped into Elias's bones, a physical reminder of the keep's decay. He kept his hand on his sword hilt, not because he expected an attack, but because the muscle memory was stronger than his better judgment. He watched Skrit's back, the greenish skin of his neck, the way his shoulders hunched under the weight of his own surrender.

Torin was struggling. Elias could hear the faint, rhythmic wheeze of the cart driver's breathing, muffled by the heavy wool blanket wrapped around him. Torin's steps were uneven, his weight shifting constantly to compensate for the strain on his right shoulder. He didn't complain. He didn't ask for help. He just kept moving, a silent, enduring pillar of strength that Elias felt he owed a debt he couldn't pay.

Maren was close to Elias, her hand gripping Nella's elbow with a firm, protective strength. The blacksmith was leaning heavily on her, her head lolling against Maren's shoulder. Maren's own face was pale, her jaw set in a tight line that spoke of the pain she was ignoring. She walked with a careful, measured gait, one foot in front of the other, conserving every ounce of energy.

Kaelen walked beside Elara, his steps light and quick. He was holding a piece of flint in his hand, turning it over and over, a small, nervous tic that betrayed his exhaustion. He looked at Elias, his eyes dark and tired. "We're almost there," he whispered. "Just a little further."

Elias nodded, though he couldn't hear him. He was too busy watching the shadows, too busy listening to the silence that followed them. The keep was quiet. The goblins were gone. The traps were dead. The only thing left was the long, cold walk to the surface, and the promise of a future that was finally, painfully, within reach.

They reached the stairs. The steps were rough and uneven, worn down by centuries of use. Torin took the first step, then the second, his face grimacing with the effort. Maren lifted Nella's leg, helping her over the edge. Elias watched them, his heart pounding in his chest, a strange, hollow feeling of triumph mixed with a deep, abiding fatigue.

They were out. The air was cold and fresh, carrying the scent of rain and wet earth. The stars were out, bright and clear, a vast canopy of light that seemed to stretch on forever. Elias took a deep breath, the cold air filling his lungs, and for a moment, he allowed himself to feel it. The victory. The end. The beginning.

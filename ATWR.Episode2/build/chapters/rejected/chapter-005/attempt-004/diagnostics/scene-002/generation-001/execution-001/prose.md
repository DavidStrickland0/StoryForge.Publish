The corridor stretched out before them, a long, narrow throat of cold stone that seemed to swallow the faint, residual glow of Kaelen’s gauntlet. The silence was heavy, not with the tension of ambush, but with the profound, ringing emptiness of a place that had just been emptied of its purpose. Footsteps echoed, soft and irregular, the rhythm of Hobb’s labored breathing on Torin’s back and the shuffling, uncertain steps of Nella, who walked with Maren’s hand firmly gripping her elbow.

Elias kept his eyes on the path, his mind already shifting gears from the immediate threat to the logistics of survival. They had the silver. They had the key. They had the people. But they did not have time. The rain was still falling outside, a constant, drumming reminder that the world beyond the keep was still turning, still wet, still cold.

"Kaelen," Elias said, his voice low enough to be swallowed by the stone but clear enough to be heard. "How are you holding up?"

The mage did not answer immediately. He was leaning slightly against the wall, his posture suggesting a man who had been running on fumes for the last hour and was only now noticing that the engine had sputtered. His face was pale, a sheen of sweat visible on his forehead despite the chill of the cellar air. But he did not collapse. He did not fade. He simply existed, with a quiet, stubborn dignity.

"Empty," Kaelen said, his voice hoarse but steady. "I used the last of my light to see the door. I used the last of my smoke to mask our scent when we entered. Now I am a man with a sword and a bad back. If you need me to throw a fireball, I can try, but I think it will be more of a spark. A very angry spark."

Elias allowed himself a small, dry smile. It was the first genuine expression of relief he had managed since they had entered the keep. "We don't need fire," he said. "We need to know where the goblins are not."

Kaelen straightened, his eyes tracking the darkness ahead. "I can't make light," he continued, a dry humor returning to his face despite the pain. "But I can make noise. I can drop a stone. Or a coin. Or a piece of that broken axle, Maren, if you can spare a splinter. I can make a sound that sounds like a person falling. Or a goblin being dropped. It will draw their attention to one specific point."

"Which point?" Elias asked.

"The far end of the corridor," Kaelen said. "If they run to investigate, the corridor will be empty. We pass through. It's not magic. It's just... physics. And a little bit of theater."

Elias looked at Maren, who was helping Nella navigate a slight unevenness in the floor. "Does that sound feasible?"

Maren looked up, her expression sharp. "If we're going to move through the open space, yes. But we have to be ready to move the moment the sound stops. If they don't come, we wait. If they do, we run."

"It's a gamble," Torin said, his voice strained as he adjusted Hobb’s weight.

"It's a plan," Elias corrected. "And it's the one we have. Kaelen, find a stone. Maren, check your pockets. Elara, mark the route. Torin, ready Hobb for movement. Maren, get Nella on her feet."

They moved with the synchronized efficiency of a machine that had been oiled and calibrated. There was no shouting, no frantic gesturing. There was only the soft scrape of boots, the rustle of cloth, and the quiet, focused intensity of five people who understood that their survival depended on each other's competence.

Elias watched them work, and he felt the tension in his chest ease. It was not a release of danger. The goblins were still there. The keep was still hostile. But for the first time, they were not just reacting. They were acting. And that was enough.

Kaelen crouched by the wall, his fingers brushing the rough-hewn stone until he found a loose fragment, a chunk of mortar no larger than a walnut. He held it up, examining it with the careful precision of a scholar assessing a rare text.

"One stone," Kaelen said. "One sound. One chance."

Elias nodded. "You have my trust, Kaelen. And my thanks."

The mage looked at him, and for a moment, the exhaustion in his eyes seemed to deepen, but it was a different kind of weight. It was the weight of being seen, of being valued not just for his power, but for his presence. He nodded back, a subtle, almost imperceptible motion.

They moved forward, the corridor stretching out before them, the silence broken only by the distant, rhythmic drumming of the rain against the roof. The keep was a beast with many jaws, and they were currently in the belly, carrying the prize. But for the first time, they were not just surviving. They were moving toward the light.

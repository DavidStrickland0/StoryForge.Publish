Elias let the silence stretch for a moment, the heavy rain drumming a relentless rhythm against the keep’s upper stones. The question hung in the damp air, waiting for an answer that none of them wanted to give yet because it implied a choice they hadn’t fully made. Maren’s gaze remained fixed on the iron-bound door at the far end of the hub, her jaw set tight. She was the one who had to push through whatever lay on the other side, and the weight of the cart axle on her back seemed to press down on her shoulders with every second they spent standing still.

“We can’t go through the wall,” Elias said finally, his voice low and steady. “Torin checked the perimeter. The goblins have a lookout post directly overlooking that gap. If we crawl out of the rubble, we’re visible from the guard tower. We’d be walking into a crossfire before we even cleared the courtyard.”

Maren turned her head sharply, her eyes narrowing. “Then we go through the front. The main entrance is guarded, yes, but it’s a single choke point. We can handle two or three goblins if we move fast.”

“Three is too many for a surprise attack in the dark,” Torin interjected, his voice quiet but firm. He was leaning against a stack of crates, his hand resting on the pommel of his sword. “And with your injury, Maren, you can’t hold a defensive line while the rest of us clear the room. If the alarm goes up, you’re stuck in that doorway.”

Maren’s expression hardened, but she didn’t argue the tactical point. She knew her limits, and she knew Torin was right. A stalemate in the center of the hub was not a strategy; it was a delay.

Elara, who had been crouching near the base of the iron door, looked up. She held a small piece of charcoal in one hand and a scrap of parchment in the other. She didn’t look at the map on the wall; she was looking at the floor.

“Neither,” Elara said.

Elias frowned. “Elara?”

“The passage behind the door,” she said, standing up slowly. She brushed a speck of dust from her sleeve. “Torin, you mentioned earlier that the goblins use a specific route to move supplies from the outer stores to their living quarters. I was looking at the wear patterns on the flagstones.”

She tapped the floor with her boot, indicating a narrow corridor that branched off to the left of the iron door, partially obscured by a fallen beam. “This isn’t a main thoroughfare. It’s a service passage. The stone is worn smooth in the center, but the edges are rough. That suggests heavy, repeated traffic, but not the kind of traffic that would require a full guard detail. It’s a back route.”

Maren stepped forward, her hammer shifting in her grip. “Back routes are where traps go. You know that.”

“I do,” Elara replied. She moved to the entrance of the narrow passage, kneeling again. She didn’t reach for her quartz dust this time. Instead, she pulled a small, flat stone from her pocket—something she had likely picked up during their trek through the woods—and held it over the threshold.

“Watch,” she whispered.

She dropped the stone. It clattered against the flagstones inside the passage, rolling a few feet before coming to rest against a pile of debris. The sound was sharp, echoing slightly in the confined space.

Nothing happened. No spring loaded mechanism, no swinging blade, no sudden release of gas. The air remained still.

Elara picked up another stone, slightly larger this time. She dropped it. It landed with a heavier thud. Still nothing.

“Weight distribution,” Elara said, looking up at Elias and Maren. “I’ve been studying the structure of these older keeps. The traps in the main corridors are usually pressure plates that trigger when a specific amount of weight is applied to a specific tile. But this passage… look at the wear.”

She pointed to the center of the floor, where the stone was polished to a shine. “This area has been walked on thousands of times. If there were a simple pressure plate here, it would have been triggered or broken long ago. The goblins don’t have the engineering skill to create a complex, delayed-action trap that resets itself. They use crude mechanics. Levers, weights, pits.”

Torin joined her, peering into the darkness of the passage. “So it’s safe?”

“It’s safe from immediate triggers,” Elara corrected. “But I suspect the danger isn’t in the floor. It’s in the ceiling.”

She gestured upward, her lantern light catching the jagged edges of the timber beams that supported the roof above. “Goblins are opportunistic, but they’re also practical. If they want to protect a supply route, they don’t build complex traps. They hang weighted bags of sand or stone from the ceiling, attached to tripwires. When someone walks through, the wire snaps, the bag drops, and the person is crushed or pinned. It’s simple. It’s effective. And it doesn’t require magic.”

Maren nodded slowly, her eyes scanning the ceiling. “I can see the knots,” she murmured. “There are frayed ropes hanging down about six feet off the ground. If they’re loaded, they’ll drop on anyone who walks the center line.”

“Exactly,” Elara said. “But if we stay to the sides, the tripwires are likely anchored to the walls, not the center. The center is the kill zone. The edges are the escape routes.”

Elias looked from Elara to Maren, then to Torin. He could see the plan forming in their minds, a practical, grounded alternative to the dangerous frontal assault or the risky wall crawl. It wasn’t elegant. It wasn’t heroic. It was just smart.

“We go through the service passage,” Elias said. “We avoid the main guard post and the lookout. We bypass the trapped main corridor. We enter the storage area from the side, where the goblins aren’t expecting us.”

Maren straightened up, the tension in her shoulders easing slightly. “I can manage that. The floor is uneven, but I can stay to the left wall. If I see a rope, I’ll cut it before it drops.”

“You’ll need a blade,” Torin said. He reached into his pack and pulled out a short, sturdy knife. He held it out to her. “Keep it sharp. If the rope is wet, it’ll be hard to cut with a dull edge.”

Maren took the knife, testing its weight in her hand. “Good enough.”

Elara rolled up her parchment and tucked it back into her satchel. She looked at Kaelen, who had been standing quietly in the corner, his brass gauntlet glowing with a faint, steady light.

“Kaelen,” she said. “We don’t need smoke for this part. We need light. Can you keep the gauntlet on a low burn? Just enough to see the tripwires, but not so bright that it draws attention from the main hall?”

Kaelen nodded, a small smile playing on his lips. “Low burn is easy. I’ve got enough energy for that for quite a while. Just don’t ask me to shield anything.”

“Deal,” Elias said.

He looked at the group, taking in their faces. They were tired, yes. Maren was in pain, and Kaelen was conserving his magic. But they were focused. They had found a way through the keep that accounted for their limitations and the goblins’ crude defenses. It wasn’t a perfect plan, but it was a workable one.

“We move when I give the signal,” Elias said. “Stay low, stay quiet, and watch those ropes.”

Maren gave a single, sharp nod. Torin checked his sword grip. Elara adjusted her lantern. Kaelen dimmed the glow of his gauntlet until it was barely visible in the gloom.

The rain continued to fall outside, a constant, rhythmic reminder of the world beyond the keep’s walls. But here, in the damp, silent service passage, they had a path. And for the first time since they had entered the ruins, the path seemed clear.

Elias took a deep breath, the cold air filling his lungs. He looked at the narrow entrance to the passage, the darkness waiting beyond the threshold. He didn’t hesitate. He stepped forward, his boots finding the worn stone, and began to move into the shadows.

The service passage was narrow, barely wide enough for two of them to walk side by side, and the air tasted of wet stone and old rust. Elias kept his sword drawn but low, the steel catching the faint, amber glow of Kaelen’s gauntlet. The light was dim, barely enough to see their own hands, but it was enough to keep the shadows from swallowing their feet.

Maren moved with a deliberate slowness that belied her usual explosive energy. She kept her left hand flat against the rough-hewn wall, using the stone as a guide, her eyes scanning the center of the path where the tripwires hung in silent, lethal suspension. The knife Torin had handed her was tucked into her belt, its hilt worn smooth from use, but she didn’t need to draw it yet. She just needed to know where the danger was, and the darkness told her that.

"Stay close to the walls," Elias murmured, his voice barely a breath in the damp silence. "Don’t look up unless you hear something move."

"Understood," Maren replied. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a soldier who had learned that panic was a luxury they could not afford.

They moved forward, step by careful step. The floor was uneven, the flagstones slick with moisture that had seeped through the cracks in the ceiling above. Elias felt the chill of it soak through his boots, a cold that settled deep in his bones. He was used to discomfort; it was part of the job. But he was also aware of the others. Kaelen’s magic was a resource, and he was spending it sparingly. Maren’s body was a vessel, and it was strained. Torin was watching their backs, his sword ready, his eyes sharp in the dim light.

And then there was Elara. She was slightly behind, her lantern held low, its light focused on the ground ahead of her. She was counting steps, or perhaps measuring distances. Elias didn’t know which, but he trusted her. She was the one who saw the patterns in the chaos, the logic in the madness. If there was a way through this passage that didn’t involve cutting a tripwire or triggering a weight, Elara would find it.

They reached the end of the passage, a small, open chamber that served as a junction. Three doors led out of it: one to the left, one to the right, and one straight ahead. The door straight ahead was heavy, iron-bound, and slightly ajar. A draft of cold air blew out from beneath it, carrying the faint scent of smoke and something else—something organic, something alive.

"The storage room," Elara said quietly. She was standing by the door, her eyes narrowed as she studied the gap. "I can see the grain sacks. And... ropes. Lots of ropes."

Elias stepped closer, pressing his ear to the door. He could hear nothing. No voices, no footsteps, no rustle of fabric. Just the steady drumming of the rain outside and the distant rumble of thunder.

"We need to be ready," Elias said, turning to face his companions. "Maren, you’re point. If anyone is in there, you’ll see them first. Kaelen, if you need to create a distraction or obscure our exit, you have my permission. Torin, watch the left door. If anyone comes out of there, you handle it. Elara, you’re with me. We’ll assess the situation inside."

Maren nodded, her hand moving to her hammer. The weight of it was familiar, comforting. She felt a pang of pain in her lower back as she shifted her stance, but she ignored it. This was what she was for. This was what she had trained for.

Kaelen raised his gauntlet, the light flaring briefly before dimming back down. "I can create a smoke screen if we need to retreat," he said. "It will last for a few minutes, no more. After that, I’ll be blind, and so will they, unless they have better eyes than us."

"Good," Elias said. "Torin?"

Torin was already moving to the left door, his sword drawn, his body coiled like a spring. "If anyone is in there, they won’t get out," he said. His voice was low, but there was a steel to it that matched the blade in his hand.

Elara moved to stand beside Elias, her lantern held high enough to illuminate the gap in the door. "I see movement," she said. "Inside. Someone is walking past the door."

Elias tensed, his hand tightening on his sword hilt. "How far away?"

"Close," Elara said. "Within ten feet."

"Wait," Elias said, holding up a hand. "Don’t move. Let them pass."

They stood in the darkness, breath held, their eyes fixed on the gap in the door. The footsteps faded, replaced by the distant sound of a goblin’s grunt, muffled by the stone walls. Then silence.

"They’re gone," Elara whispered.

Elias exchanged a glance with Maren. She nodded, her hammer raised, ready to strike.

"Now," Elias said.

He kicked the door open.

The door swung inward with a heavy thud, crashing against the stone wall and shaking dust from the ceiling. Maren stepped through first, her hammer raised low and ready. The room was larger than the junction chamber, a cavernous space cluttered with the spoils of their raids. Sacks of grain leaned against one another like tired giants, and coils of rope hung from iron hooks driven into the stone pillars.

For a heartbeat, there was nothing but the sound of their breathing and the distant drumming of rain on the roof above.

Then, from the shadows near the far wall, a voice grated out. "Who goes there?"

It was a goblin, hunched over a crate of stolen tools, its yellow eyes wide with panic. It didn't reach for a weapon; it reached for its legs.

Maren didn't wait. She moved with a speed that belied her earlier stiffness, crossing the distance in two strides. Her hammer came down not with a killing blow, but with a precise, concussive strike to the goblin’s knee. The creature folded, yelping in pain, and dropped to the floor.

"Stay down," Maren said, her voice flat.

Another goblin, thinner and older, emerged from behind a stack of barrels. He gripped a crude knife in one hand and held a torch in the other, his eyes darting nervously between Maren and the rest of the party as they streamed into the room.

"Easy, Skrit," Maren said, though she did not know his name. "Put the knife down."

The goblin hesitated. "You are... the farmers' friends," he squeaked. "We have your people. Nella. The driver. They are in the back. The dark room."

"Where?" Elias asked, stepping forward with his sword sheathed.

"The cellar," the goblin said, pointing a trembling finger toward a low archway in the far wall. "Down the stairs. But be careful. The chief is not there. He is in the tower. He is angry."

Elias exchanged a glance with Torin. The soldier nodded once, his hand resting on his sword hilt. "Show us the way," Torin said. "And if you run, Maren will break the other leg."

The goblin swallowed hard, then turned and scurried toward the archway. The party followed, moving cautiously. The archway led to a narrow staircase descending into the earth. The air grew colder and damper as they went down.

At the bottom, a heavy iron door stood closed. It was barred from the outside, but the lock was a simple mechanism that Elara picked in under a minute, her fingers working deftly in the dim light.

The door creaked open.

Inside, the room was small and cold. Two figures sat on a wooden bench, bound with ropes. Nella, the blacksmith's apprentice, looked battered but alive. Beside her, Hobb the cart driver slumped against the wall, his eyes half-closed.

"Nella?" Elias called out.

The woman’s head snapped up. Her eyes were wide with shock, then relief. "Is it over?" she asked, her voice raspy.

"It's over," Elias said. "We're getting you out."

Elara moved to their side, cutting the ropes with her knife. The bonds fell away, and Nella stretched her cramped limbs. Hobb coughed, then looked up at the party.

"Thanks," he mumbled. "Thought we were going to end up in a pot."

Maren let out a short breath. "Better you didn't. The broth here is terrible."

Elias turned to Torin. "How far is the tower?"

Torin pointed toward a separate staircase that wound upward. "That way. It’s the main access to the keep’s upper levels. Skrit will be in his room at the top."

Elias looked at Kaelen. The mage was leaning against the wall, his face pale. His gauntlet was dark, and he looked exhausted.

"Can you still fight?" Elias asked.

Kaelen shook his head slowly. "No. I have nothing left. If we need a distraction, I can make noise. But no magic."

Elias nodded. "Then we do it the old way. Steady and quiet."

He turned to the group. "Here is the plan. Torin, you and Maren take point. You know the layout better than I do. We go up the stairs, one floor at a time. Elara, you stay with the captives until we signal. Kaelen, you watch our backs. If anything moves, you yell."

Maren nodded. "I can do that. But if Skrit has more goblins, we might need to be faster than quiet."

"Then we’ll be faster," Elias said. "But we don’t want to alert the whole keep. Just the room we’re in."

They moved up the stairs. The keep was old, the wood creaking under their weight. They paused at each landing, listening. There was no sound from the floors above.

Finally, they reached the top floor. A single door stood at the end of the hallway. It was locked, but the lock was simple. Torin picked it with a thin wire he had pulled from his pack.

The door opened onto a small, cluttered room. In the center sat a goblin, larger than the others, with a thick beard and a sword resting on his lap. He looked up as the door opened.

"Ah," he said, his voice deep. "The rescue party. I was wondering when you’d show up."

Elias stepped into the room, his sword drawn. "Skrit Ironjaw," he said. "Your raids are over."

Skrit smiled. "Is that so? And what will you do about it?"

Elias didn't answer. He simply raised his sword and charged.

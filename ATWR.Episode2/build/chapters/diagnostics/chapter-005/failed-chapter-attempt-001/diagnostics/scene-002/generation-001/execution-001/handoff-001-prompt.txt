The goblin did not flinch. He did not raise his spear to a combat stance. He simply stood there, his weight balanced on the balls of his feet, his eyes tracking the movement of Torin’s burden and Maren’s trembling grip on Nella. He was a professional. He knew what he was looking at, and he knew it was a mistake for him to be standing in the open.

Elias kept his hand raised. The silence in the chamber was heavy, pressing against his eardrums, broken only by the ragged breathing of the captives and the distant, rhythmic drumming of rain on the stone roof far above. He could see the tension in Kaelen’s shoulders, the way the mage’s fingers twitched at his side, seeking a magic that wasn’t there.

"Let him go," Elias said.

His voice was calm, stripped of any threat, but it carried clearly across the empty space. The goblin’s head tilted. He looked from Elias to the door behind him, then back again.

"Open the door," Elias continued, taking a single, deliberate step forward. "Or we take the others. And we leave. And you are alone."

The goblin’s grip tightened on the spear. The wood creaked. He was not stupid. He could hear the groan of Torin’s strained muscles, the weak, frightened whimper of Nella. He could see the sword at Elias’s hip, the hammer resting on Maren’s shoulder, the cold, steady gaze of Torin.

He knew they were there. He had always known. And he was waiting for them to make a mistake.

But Elias wasn't making a mistake. He was making a point.

"The chief is behind that door," Elias said. "I know. The layout is simple. You keep your treasures in the cellar, your slaves in the middle, and your leader in the back. It’s a standard arrangement. It’s predictable."

The goblin’s eyes narrowed. He glanced over his shoulder, toward the iron-banded door. For a second, his posture shifted, a subtle tightening of the muscles in his back, a slight turn of the hips. He was listening.

"He’s waiting for you to panic," Elias said, his voice dropping an octave, becoming a low rumble that seemed to vibrate in the stone floor. "He’s waiting for you to shout, to charge, to break the door down. And then he’ll let you in, and he’ll kill you. One by one. It’s a game he plays. It’s how he keeps the fear alive."

The goblin’s hand moved to the hilt of his club. His eyes were fixed on Elias, but his head was turned, just slightly, toward the door.

"Open it," Elias said. "Or I do."

The goblin made a sound, a low, guttural growl, and raised his spear. He was going to attack. He was going to test them. He wanted to see if they were as confident as they looked.

Elias did not draw his sword. He did not shout. He simply watched the goblin move, his eyes tracking the tip of the spear, calculating the angle, the distance, the time it would take to close the gap.

Torin moved.

It was not a charge. It was a step. A single, precise step that brought his sword into the light. The steel caught the faint, dying glow of the torch in the corner, flashing with a cold, deadly promise.

The goblin froze.

He looked at Torin. He looked at the sword. He looked at the way Torin’s body coiled, his weight shifting forward, his eyes locked on the goblin’s throat.

He understood.

He was not a warrior. He was a guard. And he was outmatched.

The goblin lowered his spear. It was a slow, deliberate movement, a surrender that was more about survival than honor. He turned his back to Elias, his eyes fixed on the door.

He did not open it.

He walked to the side of the door, his hand moving to the heavy iron latch. He fumbled with it, his fingers shaking, the metal clinking softly in the silence. Then, with a sharp, decisive pull, the latch gave way.

The door swung inward.

The goblin did not step aside. He stood in the doorway, his body blocking the entrance, his eyes wide and terrified. He was not letting them in. He was letting them see.

Elias saw the room beyond.

It was small, cramped, and dark. A single torch burned in a sconce on the wall, casting long, dancing shadows. In the center of the room, a figure sat on a crude wooden chair.

It was Skrit Ironjaw.

He was larger than the guard, his skin a deep, mottled grey, his eyes hard and sharp. He held a dagger in one hand, the blade glinting in the firelight. He did not look surprised. He did not look afraid.

He looked bored.

"Ah," Skrit said, his voice a low, rasping growl. "The heroes."

The corridor narrowed, the ceiling dropping until the air felt less like a volume of space and more like a compressed weight against Elias’s temples. The stone here was rougher, the mortar between the blocks crumbling into gritty powder that coated the soles of their boots. Elias kept his hand on the pommel of his sword, not in threat, but in readiness, his fingers tracing the familiar groove of the leather grip to keep his mind anchored.

Torin moved ahead of them, his head bowed low, his eyes scanning the low archway with the systematic patience of a man counting coins. He stopped abruptly, holding up a clenched fist. The gesture was silent, but the halt was immediate.

Elias stepped forward, his breathing controlled, and looked where Torin was pointing. The floor ahead was uneven, a series of flagstones that appeared, at a casual glance, to be no different from the rest of the path. But Torin’s gaze was fixed on a single stone, slightly darker than its neighbors, its edges worn smooth by friction that seemed inconsistent with the surrounding masonry.

Elara knelt beside him, her satchel clattering softly as she produced her vial of quartz dust. She didn’t pour it; instead, she held the glass stopper over the suspicious stone, letting a single grain fall with a delicate precision that spoke of years of practice. The grain landed on the dark surface and rolled slightly before settling.

For a moment, nothing happened. The grain lay there, inert, a speck of white against the grey stone. Then, a faint shimmer rippled outward from the point of contact, a distortion in the air that looked like heat rising from pavement in summer, except it was cold. The shimmer faded, leaving behind a faint, almost invisible line of residual energy that Elara traced with a fingertip.

"It’s a pressure plate," she said, her voice low. "But it’s not connected to a pit or a spike. The residual magic suggests a warning. If the weight is applied, it likely triggers a sonic pulse. A gong, or a chime. Something loud."

Maren, who had been standing behind them with the broken cart axle strapped to her shoulder, shifted her weight. The leather straps creaked, a dry, sharp sound that cut through the silence. She didn’t look at Elara. She looked at the stone.

"Can we just lift it?" Maren asked.

Elias looked at Torin. Torin shook his head slowly. "If it’s rigged with a counterweight, lifting it might trigger the release mechanism on the other end. We don’t know the setup. We assume it’s simple, but in a goblin keep, simple is a luxury they don’t have."

Maren rolled her shoulders, the movement wincing slightly as the axle bit into her injured frame. "Then how do we get past it?"

Elara stood up, brushing the dust from her knees. "We don’t lift it. We remove the trigger. If the mechanism relies on the stone being depressed to hold a tension spring or a pin, removing the stone entirely should relieve the tension."

"That’s a gamble," Torin said.

"It’s a calculation," Elara corrected, though her voice lacked the usual sharpness. She was tired, her eyes dark circles under the pale light of Kaelen’s dimmed gauntlet. "I can see the energy signature. It’s static until disturbed. If we remove the stone slowly, without applying lateral pressure, the spring should remain locked in its resting position."

Elias looked at Kaelen, who was leaning against the wall, his gauntlet pulsing with a faint, rhythmic amber light. The mage looked pale, his skin clammy with the effort of maintaining even this minimal projection of magic.

"Kaelen," Elias said quietly. "Can you see the mechanism?"

Kaelen rubbed his forehead, a gesture of pure physical exhaustion. "I can see the ward, Elias. It’s thin. Like a stretched sheet. If I touch it, it might snap. But if you’re asking if I can hold the stone in place while you remove it, I can’t. I don’t have the strength for a shield, let alone a grip."

Elias nodded. He understood the limitation. He had learned it in the last few days, watching the light dim from Kaelen’s hands, watching the mage’s hands tremble when he tried to lift a cup of water. Magic was a resource, not a fountain, and they were running dry.

"Then we do it manually," Elias said. He looked at Maren. "You’re the strongest. Can you hold the stone steady while I pry it out?"

Maren looked at the stone, then at Elias. There was a moment of silence, a negotiation of pride and pain. She nodded. "I can do it. But I need you to do the prying. I can’t risk dropping it."

Elias moved to the stone. He drew his dagger, the blade short and sturdy, well-suited for leverage rather than cutting. He wedged the tip under the edge of the dark flagstone. He looked at Torin, who was watching with intense focus.

"Elara, watch the energy line," Elias said. "If it starts to ripple again, we stop."

Elara crouched again, her eyes fixed on the faint shimmer in the air.

Elias applied pressure. The stone resisted, grinding against the mortar beneath it. A low, grinding sound emanated from the floor, a sound that seemed to vibrate in their teeth. Maren braced her hand against the stone, her fingers digging into the rough surface, her jaw set as she fought the weight of the keep itself.

Elias gritted his teeth, pushing the dagger deeper. He could feel the tension in the mechanism, a tightness that spoke of a spring coiled in the darkness below. He applied more force, a grunt escaping his throat.

The stone shifted. It wasn’t a sudden collapse, but a slow, grinding release. The stone tilted, then lifted, revealing a dark cavity beneath it. Inside, a metal pin was visible, retracted into a slot.

"It’s safe," Elara whispered, her voice barely audible. "The energy is gone. The ward is broken."

Elias exhaled, the breath shuddering out of his lungs. He leaned back, his muscles aching from the strain. Maren pulled her hand away from the stone, rubbing her wrist. The axle on her shoulder seemed to weigh double as they stood there, the silence returning to the corridor.

"Good work," Elias said. He didn’t mean it as a compliment, but as an acknowledgment of a job well done.

Maren grunted in response, adjusting the straps on her back. "Don’t get used to it. I’m not a mason."

Torin moved forward, stepping over the exposed cavity. He didn’t look back. His eyes were already on the path ahead, which opened out into a wider space. The air changed here, carrying a different scent. It was no longer just damp stone and old blood. There was a smell of wood smoke, unwashed bodies, and something sour, like stale milk.

The goblins’ living quarters.

Elias followed Torin into the larger room. It was a low-ceilinged chamber, cluttered with rough-hewn beds, piles of clothes, and cooking pots. The fire in the center of the room was dying, the embers glowing red in the dark. Two goblins were asleep near the fire, their snorts and grunts audible in the quiet.

But it was the far end of the room that drew Elias’s eye. There was a door, heavy oak, banded with iron. And beside it, a small window, barred with rusted iron, let in a sliver of moonlight.

Torin pointed to the window. "That’s our exit," he said. "It’s small, but it leads to a drainage channel. I saw it when we scouted the perimeter. If we need to leave, we go out that way. If we need to stay, we go through the door."

Elias looked at the door. He could hear muffled voices inside, the low chatter of goblins. He could also hear something else, a sound that made his stomach clench. A cough. A weak, dry cough, followed by a whimper.

Nella or Hobb. One of them.

Elara stood beside him, her face pale in the dim light. She was looking at the goblins, analyzing their behavior. "They’re not just guarding," she said. "They’re watching. See how they’re positioned? They’re not sleeping. They’re resting, but their eyes are open. They’re waiting for something."

"Waiting for the raid?" Elias asked.

"Waiting for us," Elara said. "They know we’re here. They’ve known for a while. That’s why the traps are so simple. They don’t need to kill us immediately. They want to play with us. Or they want to take us back to their chief."

Torin’s hand went to his sword. "Then we don’t wait. We move."

Elias looked at Maren, who was standing by the door, her hammer resting against her shoulder. She looked tired, her eyes hard.

"Agreed," Maren said. "But we need to be careful. If we go in there, we’re in the lion’s den. And we’re the smallest lions in the room."

Elias nodded. He looked at Kaelen, who was standing in the corner, his arms crossed, his face grim. "Kaelen, how are you feeling?"

Kaelen looked up, his eyes meeting Elias’s. There was a flash of determination in his gaze, a defiance that belied his exhaustion. "I’m ready," he said. "When you are."

Elias took a deep breath, the air cold and stale in his lungs. He looked at the door, then at the window. The choice was clear. They could go through the door, into the heart of the goblin camp, or they could go through the window, into the darkness, and risk a fall.

He chose the door.

"Stay close," Elias said. "And stay quiet."

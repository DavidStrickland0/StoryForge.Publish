# Chapter 1: The Weight of Fifty Gold

The fire in the hearth of the Crooked Stag crackled with a dry woodsmoke scent that was far less welcome than the rain outside. Elias Thorne stood on the tavern’s flagstone floor, his leather armor feeling heavier than usual after weeks of travel. He adjusted the strap of his sword hilt and looked toward the table where the village elder sat. The man had cleared a small space on the scarred wooden surface for the contract scroll, which lay open alongside a heavy iron purse that caught the lantern light.

Elias folded his hands behind his back, keeping his posture loose but ready. “We’ve heard enough about the raids,” Elias said, his voice carrying the steady weight of someone who had seen more than most villagers in their entire lives. “But I’d like to make sure we’re on the same page before we move beyond this door.”

The elder nodded, tapping a calloused finger against the parchment. “That’s fair. The purse is fifty gold pieces for the rescue and any stolen goods recovered intact. We’ll pay another five gold per head if we bring them back alive without injury. And if you find Nella or Hobb’s tools, that goes into the reward too.”

Elias glanced at Maren, who was leaning against a table near the entrance, her hammer resting casually in one hand. “Is that all? Or is there something else I should know about the terms?”

The elder smiled thinly. “Just that you’ll be responsible for the village’s safety while we’re away. If any raids happen during your absence, we can’t afford to pay extra for that.”

Elias nodded once. “Understood. We’ll handle whatever comes our way. But I want to clarify something first.” He stepped closer to the table, leaning over the contract scroll. “You mentioned fifty gold pieces for the rescue itself. Is that a flat fee, or does it depend on how many goblins we encounter? Or if we succeed in stopping them from raiding again?”

The elder’s expression tightened slightly. “It’s a flat fee. Fifty gold pieces. That covers the entire operation—from taking the contract to bringing the villagers back safe.”

Elias straightened up, meeting the elder’s gaze. “And what happens if we recover the stolen goods but don’t find Nella or Hobb? Or if they’re injured when we bring them back?”

The elder hesitated before answering. “If they’re found alive and return safely, we’ll pay the full amount plus any additional compensation for injuries. If they’re dead…” He trailed off, looking away briefly.

Elias didn’t press further. Instead, he reached into his satchel and pulled out a small, folded piece of parchment. “This is our standard contract from the guild. It spells out everything clearly—payment terms, responsibilities, contingencies.” He slid it across the table toward the elder. “We’d prefer to use ours instead of your simple scroll.”

The elder frowned, picking up his own document and holding it up against Elias’s. “This one says the same thing, just more wordy. And we don’t need a guild stamp for this job.”

“It does,” Elias replied firmly. “It outlines exactly what we’re responsible for, how much we’re entitled to in case of complications, and who pays if things go wrong.” He paused, letting his words sink in before continuing. “We’ve done this kind of work before. We know the risks. And we want to make sure everyone knows what’s expected of us.”

The elder sighed and set his own scroll aside. “Fine. Use yours. But remember—we’re paying you fifty gold pieces total, whether you find them or not.”

Elias nodded again, then turned to Maren and Torin, who were watching the exchange quietly from the corner. “Maren, Torin—can we proceed with your terms?” he asked.

Maren crossed her arms. “As long as it’s fair, I don’t care about the formality of it.”

Torin nodded in agreement. “Just make sure we get paid for our time and effort.”

Elias turned back to the elder. “Alright. Here’s how it works: We take the contract with our terms, which include the guild’s standard clauses. Once we sign it, you give us the purse containing fifty gold pieces. We’ll spend that on supplies before leaving, so we’re not taking anything from your village’s reserves.”

The elder raised an eyebrow but nodded slowly. “And if we don’t find them?”

“Then we still get paid the full amount,” Elias said evenly. “Because that’s what we agreed to in our contract. If they’re gone forever, then that’s a failure of the mission, not a failure of payment.”

The elder studied him for a moment before nodding again. “Very well. But I want you to know—we’ll be watching closely.”

“Good,” Elias replied with a slight smile. “Because we intend to do our job right.”

He took out his pen and signed the contract, then handed it back to the elder. The elder read over it quickly before stamping it with an official seal. “There. Now for the purse.”

The elder reached into his pocket and pulled out a small leather pouch. He placed it on the table beside the contract. “This is our advance payment. Fifty gold pieces exactly.”

Elias picked up the pouch and set it aside carefully, checking its weight before opening it to count the coins inside. One by one, he laid them out on the table, arranging them in neat rows of five. There were ten rows of five, making fifty gold pieces total. He counted twice more just to be sure, confirming each row matched perfectly.

“Perfect,” Elias said, closing the pouch again and placing it back near his satchel. “We’ll use these for supplies before we leave.”

The elder nodded approvingly. “Good. Don’t forget—you’re also responsible for any stolen goods you recover. If you find Nella or Hobb’s tools, they count toward your reward too.”

Elias glanced at Maren and Torin, who had already moved to inspect the tavern’s supplies room. “Understood. We’ll make sure everything is accounted for when we return.”

The elder smiled faintly before turning back to his work. “Good luck with this job, then. And remember—the village needs you safe and sound.”

Elias gave a final nod before heading toward the door where Kaelen and Elara waited patiently outside. “Let’s get some supplies packed,” he called over his shoulder. “Then we’ll head out at dawn.”

As they stepped away from the tavern, Elias felt a sense of relief wash over him. The contract was signed, the payment verified, and now they could focus on what truly mattered—the mission ahead. He glanced back at the Crooked Stag one last time before turning toward the road that led into the woods.

The rain had softened to a drizzle by the time Elias reached the group near the tavern’s rear entrance, where Maren and Elara stood huddled under the eaves of a lean-to shed attached to the Crooked Stag. Their breath misted in the cool evening air, though the chill was less biting than the dampness clinging to their clothes. Inside the village, the elder’s voice still echoed faintly in Elias’s memory, reminding them that every moment spent away from safety increased the risk of being tracked back to the inn. But the contract was signed, the gold counted twice, and now they had a purpose beyond mere survival.

“Right then,” Maren said, shifting her weight from one boot to the other as she inspected the edge of her hammer’s wooden haft. The wood was sound, but the grain looked slightly dry where it met the iron head—a sign that moisture levels were changing faster than expected. “We’ve got time before dawn if we move smart. But first, we hit one of the farms out on the ridge. Elder said Nella went to repair a barn door near the creek bend.”

Elias nodded, adjusting his grip on the short sword at his hip. “Agreed. Let’s walk to the nearest farmhouse, check the scene, and see what we can carry back with us.” He paused, glancing toward Kaelen and Torin who had followed closely behind. “Torin, you take point? Elara, let her follow Maren—she needs that extra space to work.”

Torin stepped forward without hesitation, his hand resting on the hilt of his longsword as he scanned the path ahead. The road wound uphill through a patch of young oak saplings, their leaves already brown and brittle underfoot. Raindrops pooled in hollows between roots, forming small dark puddles that reflected the gray sky above. Torin moved deliberately, testing each step for hidden debris or loose stones before committing his full weight to the ground.

Maren led the way toward a cluster of farmhouses clustered near the creek, their timber frames blackened by soot and age. Smoke rose faintly from chimneys where fires still burned late into the night—the villagers’ futile attempt to ward off rain and cold. One house stood particularly isolated, its thatched roof sagging under the weight of wet straw. That was Nella’s destination.

They approached cautiously, keeping their voices low as they moved along the fence line separating the road from the farmstead. Inside, the air smelled of wet hay, woodsmoke, and something metallic—blood, perhaps, or rusted tools left outside in the rain. Maren raised a hand to pause the group before stepping onto the porch. She knelt beside a pile of discarded gear lying near the door: leather straps, metal clamps, and a hammer with its wooden handle splintered at the base.

Elara crouched down beside her, pulling a small notebook from her satchel as she began sketching the arrangement of objects on the ground. “Look here,” she whispered, pointing to a set of footprints pressed into mud near the threshold. “These are human—size ten or eleven—and they’re heading away from the house. But these…” She traced another set with her finger, dipping it into the wet earth. “These are wider, deeper, and show partial claw marks along the edges. Not bear tracks. Too small for a dire wolf, too irregular for anything natural.”

Maren grunted in agreement, scooping up a handful of dirt and letting it sift through her fingers. “Goblins,” she confirmed flatly. “They’ve been here at least twice since Nella left. Maybe three times if we count the scuff marks on the step.” She stood, brushing her knees against the damp wood of the porch. “And look at this.” She gestured to a small iron arrowhead lying near the edge of the doorframe. It wasn’t buried; it had fallen from someone’s pocket or pouch while they hurried away.

Elias stepped closer, examining the object with careful attention. The blade was jagged but still sharp enough to cut flesh, its tip bent slightly inward as if struck against stone or metal during a struggle. “This belongs to one of them,” he murmured. “We can use it later—maybe even leave it near their camp to draw attention away from us.”

“Or not,” Maren countered sharply. “If we leave it there, they might follow the trail back here first thing in the morning. We don’t want that.” She glanced at Elara’s sketchbook again. “You’re mapping everything?”

Elara nodded, adding a few lines to her drawing of the footprint patterns. “Yes. If I mark where each set leads, we’ll know exactly which direction they took after leaving this house. And if there are multiple camps…” She trailed off, her voice softening as she looked up at Elias. “We might find more than just one group out here.”

Elias considered that possibility for a moment before answering. “Good point. But let’s not get ahead of ourselves. Right now, we need to confirm whether Nella and Hobb are still missing or if they’ve already been taken. Once we know that, we can decide how much risk is worth taking.”

Maren nodded again, though her expression remained grim. “I’ll check the cellar first. If anyone’s been dragged down there, I’ll hear it before you do.” She turned and pushed open the door leading into the farmhouse, her boots sinking slightly into the muddy floorboards as she stepped inside. The interior was cluttered with farming implements and scattered bundles of grain, most of them wet or damaged by rainwater seeping through cracks in the roof.

Elara followed closely behind, kneeling beside a crate marked with Nella’s initials scratched into the side. She opened it slowly, revealing layers of leather straps wrapped around metal fittings—tools intended for heavy lifting or repairing structures. A few pieces were missing from the top layer, their absence obvious even without counting every single item.

“Two large tools gone,” Elara noted quietly, closing the lid again. “Likely taken by goblins. Maybe sold to a merchant nearby.” She paused, her eyes scanning the rest of the room. “But wait…” She pointed to something half-hidden beneath a pile of straw near the far corner. “A rope fragment.”

Maren stepped over to investigate, pulling the tattered end free from its hiding place. It was crude, woven from coarse hemp fibers that had frayed at both ends. “They left this behind when they fled,” she said, holding it up for Elara to examine. “No sign of struggle here—just hasty retreat.”

Elias watched silently as Maren and Elara worked together, their movements efficient and purposeful despite the gloom outside. He felt a strange mix of pride and unease watching them operate without him needing to intervene. They were competent enough on their own, yet he knew better than to underestimate the dangers lurking beyond the village limits.

“Let’s gather what we can before heading out,” Elias called softly, his voice carrying just enough authority to remind them they weren’t alone in this mission. “Pack the tools if you think they’re useful. Leave the rest for now.”

Maren nodded once more, tossing the rope into her pack alongside a broken cart axle she’d found earlier that evening. “Done. Come on, Elias—we’ll head back toward the road and regroup with Torin and Kaelen.”

As they exited the farmhouse, Elara lingered behind to inspect the footprint patterns one last time before sealing her notebook shut. The rain had begun falling harder again, washing away some of the finer details but leaving enough evidence for their current needs. She tucked her notes into her satchel along with a small vial of quartz dust she’d collected during their earlier travels—a reminder that even mundane tasks could have unexpected consequences in worlds where magic existed alongside ordinary life.

Outside, Torin and Kaelen waited near the edge of the property, having taken shelter beneath an overhang while Maren and Elara worked. Kaelen held his brass gauntlet loosely in one hand, using its faint glow to illuminate the wet ground around them without drawing attention from nearby wildlife—or worse, goblin scouts patrolling the perimeter.

“Alright,” Elias said finally, stepping into view with a weary smile. “We’ve got our supplies packed, and now we know where the goblins are heading next.” He gestured toward the treeline beyond the farmhouses. “Time to follow their trail before they vanish deeper into the woods.”

Torin adjusted his grip on his sword as he spoke up. “I’ll take lead. Maren, you stay close to Elara—she’s still mapping things, right?”

Elara nodded absently, already pulling out her notebook again despite Elias’s earlier warning to pack up. “Almost done,” she muttered, adding a final note about the rope fragment before closing the book once more. “Just need to cross-reference with the tracks from yesterday’s raid.”

Maren rolled her eyes but held her tongue, falling into step behind Torin as they moved toward the tree line. Kaelen followed close beside them, casting occasional glances at the ground beneath their boots—checking for signs of disturbance or movement that might indicate danger ahead.

Their progress was slow and deliberate, each member aware of their role within the group dynamic. Elias led from the front, keeping an eye on both the terrain and the party’s morale; Torin took point physically, scanning the horizon for any sign of approaching threats; Maren handled logistics—checking equipment, managing supplies, and ensuring everyone stayed together despite fatigue or distraction; Kaelen provided magical support where needed, using his limited abilities to obscure their movements or detect invisible hazards; and Elara analyzed evidence as they went, turning scattered clues into actionable intelligence.

Rain lashed against the rough-hewn bark of the oak where Elias stopped, turning the forest floor into a slick, mud-churned trap that demanded constant attention. The sky had opened up fully again, a sudden shift from the gentle drizzle of the village to a proper deluge that smelled of wet wool and turned earth. It was the kind of weather that made a traveler’s clothes heavy with water and his boots fill within minutes, yet Elias kept his stride steady, eyes scanning the line of trees for any unnatural break in the canopy or a shadow that didn’t belong. He held his cloak back against the wind, letting it flap slightly behind him, while keeping one hand near the hilt of his sword—not out of fear of immediate attack, but because the rain made every step into the unknown feel like a choice between caution and momentum. Behind him, Maren adjusted her pack with a grunt that sounded less like pain and more like satisfaction at having finished checking the gear. The broken cart axle she had dragged from the farm’s edge was strapped to her shoulder now, a clumsy but necessary weight that balanced against the hammer slung across her back. Her hands were gloved in thick leather, though the cuffs showed signs of dampness already soaking through; she’d ignored the earlier concern about her burned skin because the cold rain kept her from feeling the sting too much.

“We move now,” Elias said, his voice low and steady despite the wind trying to tear it away. “If we wait for the storm to break, the trail washes out.” Maren nodded once, tapping the axe haft against her palm as if checking its balance rather than comforting herself. “Mud’ll hide tracks in half an hour,” she replied, voice practical and clipped. “But goblins don’t care about mud. They leave their own mess regardless of rain or sun.”

Elara stepped forward with a notebook clutched to her chest, water dripping from the rim of her hood onto the pages where she’d sketched footprints earlier. She didn’t open it, but she did glance at Elias over the top of her spectacles, which were fogged by the humidity. “The arrowhead’s still sharp,” she murmured. “And the rope fragment—” She stopped mid-sentence as Kaelen flicked his gauntlet with a soft *click*, casting a faint ring of golden light through the gloom ahead. The brass relic pulsed gently, its inner glow steady despite the storm, offering enough illumination to read the trail markers without straining eyes or risking exposure to the dark corners of the woods.

“Good,” Elias said, glancing at the mage’s hand. “Keep it on until we’re clear.” Kaelen gave a small shrug, his expression unreadable in the dim light; he didn’t seem bothered by the effort it took to maintain the glow, nor did he comment on how much energy such magic must cost him over time.

Torin moved ahead into the tree line, boots sinking slightly into the mud as he tested each foothold with a practiced rhythm. He wasn’t scanning for threats so much as testing the ground itself—checking where branches had fallen, whether roots had shifted under pressure, and if any animal tracks might confuse their path. “Left side’s slicker,” he called back over the sound of wind rattling leaves. “Right bank holds better.”

Maren stepped up beside him, taking point on that flank now while Elias kept watch from the rear. “Lead me where it doesn’t slip,” she said, voice firm despite the rain. “I’ll follow your lead if you’re sure.”

Torin didn’t hesitate. He gestured toward a narrow ridge that ran parallel to the creek bend, its surface hardened by recent wind and exposed to less runoff than the lowlands below. “That way,” he said. “Steeper but firmer footing.” They began moving again, stepping carefully over roots and fallen branches that the rain had loosened. Elias walked near the center of the group, watching both ahead and behind, making sure no one lagged or strayed too close to the edge where the creek widened into a rocky embankment. The broken cart axle creaked against Maren’s shoulder with every step, a dull reminder of what they’d left behind at the farm. She didn’t complain about it; instead, she shifted her weight occasionally to redistribute the load, letting Elias see that she understood the necessity of carrying it even if it slowed them down.

“We’ll need to lighten up once we cross the tree line,” he said quietly. “Leave extra rope and tools there.”

“Agreed,” Maren replied without hesitation. “But I’m not leaving Nella’s straps or the iron fittings behind unless there’s no choice.”

“Not leaving anything vital,” Elias added. “Just the non-essentials. We’ll grab what we can once we’ve secured the captives.”

Elara walked slightly ahead of him now, using her own small lantern to check for loose stones or hidden dips in the trail. She carried a satchel filled with chalk and brushes, ready to mark any new evidence they found along the way. “The rope fragment’s still intact,” she said, holding it up so Elias could see it better. “It was tucked under straw near the doorframe. The goblins must’ve used it to drag something heavy out quickly.”

Elias nodded, studying the frayed ends where hemp fibers had been pulled apart by force. “Hasty retreat,” he confirmed. “They didn’t want to be seen here.”

Torin paused briefly at a cluster of bushes that seemed unusually bare of leaves, though the season wasn’t late enough for autumn shedding. He leaned forward and sniffed the air, his nose twitching slightly as if catching something faint beneath the scent of wet wood and rain. “Something’s off,” he said after a moment. “Not goblin smell, but… stale fear.”

Elias frowned, stepping closer to inspect the bush himself. Inside, near the base of the trunk, lay a small smear of dried blood—human, not animal—and something else that looked like charred fabric caught on the bark. “Burnt gear?” Elara asked, reaching out to take a sample with her gloved hand. She held it up for inspection, noting how the fibers had blackened unevenly, as if caught in a sudden fire or exposed to heat quickly before being discarded. “Could be from Nella’s crate,” she suggested. “Or maybe they burned something else first.”

Maren stepped forward and touched the charred cloth with her hammer’s grip, testing whether it was still hot enough to burn her skin. “Cold enough to touch,” she said. “But too fresh to have been there long.”

Torin nodded again, signaling them to move on without lingering. “Keep moving,” he said. “If they’re burning things, they’re preparing for something bigger than just raiding farms.”

Elias didn’t press further; he knew that speculation would only slow them down when they had limited time and energy. Instead, he focused on keeping the group together and watching for signs of goblin movement beyond what they could already see in the gloom. As they crossed into denser woods where light barely penetrated, Kaelen’s gauntlet dimmed slightly, its inner glow struggling against the rain-induced haze. He didn’t mention it, though Elias noticed the change immediately and adjusted his own pace accordingly.

“Save your energy,” he said quietly. “We’ll need you when we hit the keep.”

Kaelen gave a small smile that barely registered in the dim light. “I’ve got enough left for smoke screens,” he replied. “And maybe a shield if things get ugly.”

Elias nodded again, trusting that judgment more than any spell slot count could explain. The storm continued to pour down around them, rain falling hard enough to blur outlines and make speech difficult without raising voices. Yet the group kept moving forward, each member aware of their role in maintaining cohesion under these conditions. Maren led with her hammer ready, Torin watched the path ahead for traps or ambushes, Elara recorded findings as they went, Kaelen managed visibility and defense, and Elias coordinated everything while keeping an eye on morale. They weren’t heroes by some grand design—they were five people who had agreed to work together because no one else would help them survive another night of goblin raids. And tonight, with the rain masking their approach and the trail guiding them toward a ruined keep that sat beyond the tree line, they were closer than ever to proving themselves capable enough to face whatever lay ahead.

The corridor narrowed, the stone walls pressing in until the air felt thick and stagnant. The rain above had faded into a distant, muffled hiss, replaced by the immediate, damp chill of the deep earth. Here, the light from Kaelen’s gauntlet was not just dim; it was a struggle. The brass surface, once a steady source of amber warmth, now flickered with a sickly, intermittent pulse, like a candle in a draft.

Maren’s breathing was a rhythmic, wet sound in the silence. Each step she took was a negotiation with the broken cart axle strapped to her shoulder. The wood, rough and splintered, dug into her injured side with every shift of weight. She did not slow down, but the effort was written in the rigid line of her spine and the white-knuckled grip she maintained on the handle of her hammer. Her boots slipped twice on the slick flagstones, each time requiring a sharp, stabilizing correction that sent a jolt of pain up her ribs.

Elias walked a half-step behind her, his eyes fixed not on the path ahead, but on her posture. He could see the tremor in her left leg, the way she favored her right side to compensate. He said nothing. To offer help now would be to acknowledge a failure in their planning, and to offer criticism would be to break the fragile focus they required. He simply matched her pace, ensuring that if she stumbled, he would be there to catch her, not to scold her.

Kaelen walked on the other side, his hand resting lightly on the hilt of his dagger, a futile gesture of comfort. His other hand hovered near his gauntlet, fingers twitching as if he could physically push more energy into the fading light. His face was pale, the skin around his eyes drawn tight with the kind of exhaustion that transcends sleep. He had pushed himself too hard in the earlier chamber, using the last of his reserves to maintain the smoke veil and the ward-disruption. Now, he was running on fumes, his mind sharp but his body a hollow shell.

"Keep your eyes down," Kaelen whispered, his voice thin, stripped of its usual dry wit. "The light... it’s not holding. If I let it go, we’re blind. If I keep it, I’m... I’m running out."

Elias nodded, his gaze shifting to the gauntlet. The amber glow had dropped to a dull, brownish smear. It was enough to see the immediate floor, enough to avoid tripping, but it offered no depth, no sense of direction. It was a fragile thread of visibility in a vast, dark void.

They reached the junction.

It was a T-intersection, the main corridor splitting into two smaller passages. To the left, the passage descended a short flight of stone steps into a deeper darkness. To the right, the corridor continued straight, slightly upward, toward what Elara had identified on the map as the storage chamber.

Maren stopped. Her chest heaved, a sharp, painful intake of air that sounded too loud in the confined space. She leaned heavily on her hammer, using it as a crutch, her knuckles white against the leather grip. The broken axle shifted against her side, and she hissed through her teeth, a sound of pure, unadulterated pain.

Elias stepped forward, placing a hand on her shoulder. He didn’t touch her; he just let her feel the presence, the stability. "We’re here," he said, his voice low and steady. "The junction. We need to choose."

Maren straightened, forcing the pain to recede, though it remained a dull, throbbing presence in her bones. She looked down the left-hand passage, then up the right. The light from Kaelen’s gauntlet barely reached the first step of the descent on the left. The right-hand passage was a black hole, indistinguishable from the wall itself.

"Left is down," Maren said, her voice raspy. "Right is straight. Elara said the captives are in the storage chamber. That’s straight."

Elias nodded. He looked at Kaelen. The mage was leaning against the wall, his eyes closed, his face a mask of concentration. He was trying to hold the light, trying to keep the gauntlet from going completely dark. His lips moved silently, a prayer or a command, but nothing changed. The light flickered again, weaker this time.

"Kaelen," Elias said softly. "How much more can you do?"

Kaelen opened his eyes. They were bloodshot, the irises dilated in the gloom. "Not much," he admitted. "Maybe... maybe enough to get us to the next turn. After that, I’m done. If I try to push it, I might black out. Or the gauntlet might shatter. I don’t know. It’s not just the magic, Elias. It’s the strain. My hands are shaking."

Elias looked at Maren. She was watching Kaelen with a mixture of sympathy and irritation. She understood the cost of magic, the way it drained the body as well as the mind. She had seen it before, in other mages, other situations. It was a tax they all paid, but Kaelen’s was the highest.

"We go right," Elias decided. "Straight to the storage chamber. We don’t take the stairs. Not yet. We need to be in position before we make our move."

Maren nodded, a sharp, affirmative motion. "I can do straight. I can’t do stairs. Not tonight."

Elias looked at the dark passage to the right. It was empty, silent, and waiting. The air coming from it was colder, drier, and carried a faint smell of old iron and damp stone. It was the path to Nella and Hobb. It was the path to the end of this night.

"Stay close," Elias said. "Kaelen, keep the light low. Maren, keep your hammer ready. We move slowly. We move carefully. We do not rush."

Maren adjusted her grip on the hammer, the pain in her side a constant, grinding reminder of her limitations. Kaelen straightened, his hand resting on his gauntlet, his fingers trembling slightly. Elias took a step forward, into the darkness, his sword sheathed, his eyes sharp, his mind clear.

They moved into the right-hand passage, one by one, into the cold, silent unknown. The light from the gauntlet faded behind them, swallowed by the stone, leaving only the faintest trace of amber in the darkness. They were alone now, in the deep, in the dark, with the weight of their choices and the pain of their bodies, moving forward toward the storage chamber, toward the captives, toward the end.

The corridor stretched ahead of them, a narrow throat of cold stone that seemed to swallow the faint light from their lanterns. The air here was still, lacking the damp chill of the outer courtyard, but it carried a heavier scent—a mix of wet earth, old wood, and the faint, metallic tang of rusted iron. They moved with the steady, inevitable pace of a tide, their breathing synchronized, their hearts beating in a rhythm that was as much a testament to their training as it was to their relief.

They had slipped past the first guard. They had maintained their cover. The goblins remained unaware of their presence. The stealth approach had worked.

The path was clear. The next step was theirs to take.

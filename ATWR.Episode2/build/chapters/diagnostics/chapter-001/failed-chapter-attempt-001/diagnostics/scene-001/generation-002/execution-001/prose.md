The sign hanging crookedly over the door of the Crooked Stag was not an omen; it was simply a sign, the wood weathered to a dull grey by wind and rain. The village of Pinehollow clung to the edge of a deep valley, its stone houses clinging to the slope like barnacles on a hull. Elias Thorne stepped off the cobblestones and onto the dirt floor of the tavern’s entrance hall. The air inside smelled of roasted woodsmoke, stale ale, and the damp wool of five travelers who had not yet taken their boots off.

Inside, the room was packed with locals, a mixture of farmers, herders, and children playing at the edges of the warmth. A man in his late forties, dressed in a rough-spun tunic stained with soil and sweat, stood behind the bar. He held a ledger open with one hand and leaned on it with the other, looking as though he had not slept in days. This was Old Man Heston, the village elder.

Elias moved toward him, his boots silent on the packed earth. Maren followed close behind, her heavy hammer resting comfortably on her shoulder. Torin brought up the rear, scanning the room with an eye that missed nothing but offered no commentary until necessary. Kaelen and Elara lingered near the door, giving the group a moment to establish their presence without immediately drawing attention.

Heston did not smile as Elias approached. His face was a map of worry lines, deepening around eyes that looked tired. He pointed to Elias with a trembling hand, and the man’s grip on his ledger tightened.

“You know who we are,” Heston said. His voice rasped from disuse or perhaps just age. “We’ve watched you pass through the valley many times now.”

Elias nodded. “And we know who you are.”

“Then hear me out before you speak of your guild or your fees,” Heston interrupted, his tone sharp despite its weariness. “We have no coin left to pay a stranger with more coin than we have in our pockets. We cannot afford to feed five mouths for weeks while you wander the hills.”

Elias paused. He had expected resistance, but not this specific kind of desperation. “That’s why we’re here, Heston,” he said softly. “We don’t ask for much. We take a job and we get paid when the work is done.”

Heston looked at Elias as though testing the weight of his words against the reality of their situation. Then he gestured toward a table near the window where a small group of farmers sat huddled together. They were staring at their boots, their hands clutching something that looked like bread crusts or perhaps just dirt wrapped in cloth.

“Sit,” Heston said quietly. “Let me tell you what happened.”

Elias lowered himself onto a stool and crossed his arms on the table. Maren stood by the door, watching the room with her usual quiet intensity. Torin leaned against the wall near the hearth, keeping an eye on the patrons but giving them space to speak freely. Kaelen and Elara remained in the shadows of the entrance, listening without interrupting.

Heston turned back to his ledger and began to recount the events that had brought the party to Pinehollow. It was a tale of missing livestock, stolen grain, and a boy named Nella who had vanished while delivering tools to an outlying farm. He spoke of a farmer named Hobb whose cart had been found overturned near the edge of the woods, its wheels broken by something sharp and unnatural.

As he finished, he looked up at Elias again. “We need help,” he said simply. “But we can’t pay much.”

Elias considered this for a moment. He reached into his pouch and pulled out a small leather bag containing a few gold pieces—enough to cover their own expenses for the next few days but not enough to make a significant difference in the village’s coffers. He placed it on the table between them.

“This is what we’ve brought,” Elias said. “Take care of us until the job is done, and we’ll take whatever you have left when we return.”

Heston stared at the gold pieces, then at Elias, and finally at the others who had gathered around him. He hesitated, as if weighing whether to accept their offer or reject it outright. Then he nodded slowly and reached out to touch the gold bag with a rough hand.

“Thank you,” he said, his voice cracking slightly. “But we can’t afford to feed five people for weeks like that.”

Elias shook his head. “We’ll share what we have.” He gestured toward Maren and Torin. “And if you need help finding food or shelter, we’ll do whatever we can.”

Heston looked at the others, then back at Elias. Finally, he nodded again and turned to the room. “Friends,” he called out. “There are five strangers here who’ve agreed to help us. They don’t want payment, only a chance to earn their keep.”

The room fell silent for a moment, then erupted into murmurs of surprise and curiosity. Some looked at Elias with suspicion; others with gratitude. A few children stopped playing and watched the newcomers with wide eyes.

Elias stood up and walked toward the group of farmers huddled near the window. He knelt beside them and spoke quietly, explaining who they were and why they had come. The farmers listened intently, their faces reflecting a mixture of fear and hope.

When Elias returned to the table, he found that Heston had already begun counting out a small purse of silver coins—just enough to cover their meals for the next day or two. Elias accepted the purse without comment and placed it in his own pouch.

“Thank you,” he said. “We’ll take care of ourselves.”

Heston nodded again and turned back to his ledger, though his hands trembled as he wrote down the names of the new arrivals and the amount owed. He looked at Elias one more time before speaking.

“You’re not like most adventurers I’ve seen,” Heston said. “Most want gold and glory. You seem more interested in helping us.”

Elias smiled faintly. “We’re here because we can help, not because we need the money.”

Heston looked at him for a moment longer, then nodded again and turned back to his work. The room seemed quieter now, the tension easing slightly as the farmers began to talk among themselves in hushed tones. Elias watched them briefly before turning to Maren and Torin.

“Well?” he asked. “What do you think?”

Maren crossed her arms and gave a small shrug. “They’re scared,” she said. “But they’re also stubborn. Once we start working, they’ll stop worrying about the money and focus on getting their lives back together.”

Torin nodded in agreement. “And we’ll get our payment when we finish the job,” he added. “That’s all that matters.”

Elias glanced at Kaelen and Elara, who were still standing near the door. They had been quiet throughout the conversation, observing the room and the people within it with practiced detachment.

“Alright,” Elias said. “We’ll start tomorrow morning.”

Heston looked up from his ledger again. “Tomorrow?” he asked. “Are you sure?”

Elias nodded firmly. “Yes. We’ll rest tonight and be ready to leave first light.”

Heston sighed and put down his pen. “Alright,” he said. “Let’s hope we don’t regret this decision.”

The group moved out of the tavern shortly afterward, leaving Heston and the villagers behind with a renewed sense of purpose—and perhaps a little less fear. As they walked up the road toward the edge of the woods where the ruined keep stood, Elias couldn’t help but feel a flicker of doubt in his chest. Would they be able to do what they set out to do? Could they really save Nella and Hobb before another day passed without them?

But then he remembered the way the farmers had looked at him when he promised to help, and the way Heston had accepted their offer with such relief. Doubt faded quickly, replaced by a quiet determination that had guided him through many adventures before this one.

They reached the edge of the woods and paused to look back at Pinehollow. The village was small and unremarkable, but it felt important to Elias in ways he couldn’t quite explain. It was home now, even if only temporarily.

“Alright,” Elias said finally. “Let’s get some rest.”

He turned to lead the way into the woods, where the ruined keep waited beyond the tree line. Behind him, Kaelen and Elara exchanged a quick glance before falling into step with the group. Torin and Maren followed close behind, their expressions serious but focused.

As they disappeared into the shadows of the forest, Elias couldn’t help but wonder what tomorrow would bring.

Skrit did not run. He did not scream. He stood in the center of the main hall, his boots planted firmly on the worn flagstones, and watched the gallery with the calm, assessing eyes of a man who had already calculated the odds and found them wanting. The silence that followed his emergence was not empty; it was heavy, pressurized, a held breath that seemed to vibrate against the stone walls.

Elias let the silence stretch. He knew the psychology of the moment better than anyone. The goblins below were looking to Skrit, their small, twitchy bodies coiled, waiting for a command. They were not looking at the sword at Elias’s hip, nor at the hammer resting against Maren’s shoulder. They were looking at their chief, hoping he would find a way out that didn’t involve the dark, cold certainty of defeat.

"Skrit," Elias called out. His voice was not a shout. It was a level, clear tone that carried easily across the open space of the hall, cutting through the damp air without effort. "You have two choices. You can throw the grain, and we will let you and your people walk out of here. Or you can try to take back what you stole, and we will end this right now, in this hall, with you in the center of it."

It was a bluff, of course. They had no time to disarm a dozen goblins if they decided to charge, and Kaelen’s empty reserves meant no magical deterrent. But the bluff was built on a foundation of absolute competence. The party had moved with a precision that left no room for doubt. They had not stumbled, not panicked, not made a single wasted movement. To the goblins, that precision was a threat more terrifying than any weapon.

Skrit looked at the grain sacks piled in the center of the hall. They were the pride of his band, the spoils of the last three raids. Then he looked at the man on Torin’s back, the heavy, unconscious weight that represented a failure of his security. Finally, he looked at Elias.

"You think you are better than us," Skrit said. His voice was rough, a gravelly rasp that seemed to scrape against the back of his throat. He did not raise his knife. He did not move his hand to his hip. He simply stood there, his head tilted slightly, as if listening to a distant sound.

"I think you are tired," Elias replied. "I think you are scared. And I think you are smart enough to know that fighting us is the wrong choice."

A low murmur rippled through the goblins below. One of them, a young one with a missing ear, shifted his weight, his eyes darting between the grain and the gallery. He looked at Skrit, then at the grain, then back at Skrit. The message was clear: *We are losing.*

Skrit’s jaw tightened. He looked at the knife at his hip, then at the heavy iron-banded door behind him, the one that led to the cellar where the rest of his band waited. He knew the layout of the keep better than anyone. He knew the exits. He knew that if he ran, they would follow. But he also knew, with a cold, sinking certainty, that they would not follow far.

He reached out and took the knife from his hip.

The motion was slow, deliberate. He held the knife out in front of him, the blade catching the faint light from the torches that still burned in the sconces along the walls. Then, with a flick of his wrist, he tossed it onto the floor. It clattered against the stone, a sharp, final sound that seemed to echo in the silent hall.

The goblins flinched. The young one with the missing ear let out a small, high-pitched whimper. The others began to move, slowly, awkwardly, shedding their weapons. A spear dropped. A crude axe clattered to the ground. One by one, the band of raiders laid down their arms, their heads bowed, their bodies hunched in a posture of defeat.

Elias watched them. He did not relax. He did not smile. He simply watched, his eyes tracking every movement, every shift in weight, every subtle change in posture. He knew that fear was a powerful motivator, but it was also a volatile one. A single wrong move, a single spark of anger or desperation, could turn this surrender into a massacre.

"Torin," he said quietly. "Put him down."

Torin moved with a fluid grace that belied the weight on his back. He set Hobb down gently, easing the man against the wall. Hobb groaned, a low, pained sound, but he did not wake. The water of life had done its work, keeping his heart steady, but the pain of the fracture was still there, a constant, grinding reminder of his fall.

Maren stepped forward, her hand resting on the hilt of her hammer. She did not draw it. She did not need to. Her presence was enough. She looked at Skrit, her eyes hard and unyielding, and Skrit met her gaze for a brief, uncomfortable moment before looking away.

"Elara," Elias said. "Check the door."

Elara moved to the iron-banded door, her fingers tracing the rough-hewn stone of the frame. She listened, her head tilted, her eyes closed. After a moment, she nodded. "It’s clear," she said. "No one is waiting on the other side. They are all here."

Elias nodded. He looked at Skrit, who was standing in the center of the hall, his hands raised, his eyes fixed on the floor. He was a small, pathetic figure, stripped of his power, his dignity, his pride. He was just a man, a frightened man, who had made a series of bad choices and was now paying the price.

"Walk out," Elias said. "Take your grain. Take your weapons. But you stay out of Pinehollow. You stay out of the valley. If we see you again, we will not be so generous."

Skrit nodded. He turned, and began to walk toward the door. The other goblins followed, moving in a slow, shuffling procession, their heads bowed, their eyes downcast. They were defeated, but they were alive. And that, Elias knew, was the most important thing.

As the last of the goblins disappeared through the door, the silence in the hall shifted. It was no longer the heavy, pressurized silence of a held breath. It was the quiet of a storm that had passed, the stillness of a world that had been saved, however briefly, from chaos.

Elias let out a breath he didn’t realize he had been holding. He looked at his companions, at the exhaustion in their eyes, the pain in their bodies, the quiet, steady strength that had held them together. They had done it. They had saved the people, they had stopped the raids, and they had done it without losing anyone.

It was a good ending. It was a simple ending. And it was enough.

Kaelen didn't need to search long. The floor of the keep was a graveyard of small debris, and he plucked a loose flagstone chip from a crack near Maren's boot. It was heavy, jagged, and perfect for making a sound that resembled a dropped pot rather than a falling man.

He held it up, his fingers trembling slightly from the cold and the exertion of the last hour. He looked at Elias, a silent question in his eyes. *Is this enough? Is this enough for all of us?*

Elias nodded. The gesture was small, barely a dip of the chin, but it carried the weight of the entire plan.

"Ready," Elias whispered.

Kaelen stepped forward, his movements slow and deliberate. He moved to the edge of the corridor where the wall offered a slight lip, a narrow ledge of stone that jutted out just enough to provide a perch. He looked down the length of the passage, into the gloom where the shouts of the goblins were growing louder, a cacophony of panic and broken language. He could see the flicker of their torches, the blurred shapes of bodies moving in the distance.

He took a breath, steadied his hand, and let the stone drop.

It didn't fall straight. It caught on a roughness in the floor, bounced once, twice, and then clattered against a metal bucket that sat near the far wall. The sound was sharp, bright, and startling in the enclosed space.

For a heartbeat, there was only silence.

Then, the goblins erupted.

The shouting changed pitch, becoming higher, more frantic. There was a scuffle of feet, the heavy thud of bodies colliding, and the distinct crash of something heavy being shoved aside. Elias didn't wait to listen to the words. He didn't need to. He gave a sharp nod to Torin.

Torin was already moving. He had shifted Hobb's weight, adjusting the man's limbs to distribute the load more evenly across his back. The cart driver was unconscious, his breathing shallow but steady, the water of life having done its quiet, vital work. Torin's face was grim, a bead of sweat tracing a line down his temple despite the chill in the air. He didn't look at his shoulder. He didn't need to. He simply began to climb.

The staircase was narrow, a spiraling throat of stone that wound upward into the darkness. The steps were worn smooth by centuries of use, slick with condensation. It was a difficult ascent for anyone, but for Torin, carrying a man, it was a test of endurance that demanded every ounce of his strength.

Elias watched him go, his own body tense with the effort of maintaining his balance on the uneven rungs. He kept his hand on the wall, feeling the cold dampness seep through his leather gauntlet. He was the second to move, following Torin's path with careful precision. Behind him, Maren took Nella's arm, guiding the blacksmith with a firm, reassuring grip. Nella was pale, her eyes wide and unfocused, but she was moving. She was alive.

Kaelen and Elara brought up the rear. Kaelen's face was gray, his lips pressed into a thin line. He was exhausted, his body trembling with the residual aftershock of the magic he had burned through. But he was still there. He was still part of the machine.

The climb was slow. Each step required a negotiation with the stone, a test of grip and balance. The air grew thinner, colder, carrying the scent of old dust and the faint, metallic tang of the upper levels. Elias could hear the goblins below, their voices muffled by the stone but still present, a constant reminder of the threat they had left behind.

Halfway up, Torin stopped.

He didn't say anything. He just stood there, his chest heaving, his hands gripping the railing tightly. His right arm hung limp at his side, the muscles in his shoulder visibly strained. He was in pain. It was evident in the way he held his breath, in the slight tremor that ran through his frame.

Elias paused behind him, his hand resting on the back of Torin's neck. It was a gesture of support, not command. He didn't need to ask if the man was okay. He could feel the tension in the soldier's body, the way his spine was rigid with effort.

"Take a breath," Elias said softly.

Torin nodded, a jerky motion. He closed his eyes for a moment, focusing on his breathing, pushing the pain down into a place where it wouldn't interfere. When he opened them, his gaze was clear, steady. He looked at Elias, and in that look, there was a silent acknowledgment. *I'm still here. I'm still moving.*

Torin began to climb again.

The rest of the ascent was faster, though no less difficult. They moved as a single unit, their movements synchronized by instinct and necessity. There was no talking, no unnecessary noise. Just the scrape of boots on stone, the soft rustle of clothing, and the steady rhythm of their breathing.

When they reached the top, the world opened up.

The main hall of the keep was vast, a cavernous space with high, vaulted ceilings that disappeared into the shadows. The air here was drier, less damp, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and old leather. In the center of the room, a large fire burned in a stone hearth, casting long, dancing shadows across the floor.

And there were goblins.

Dozens of them.

They were scattered throughout the hall, some sitting in piles of stolen goods, others sharpening weapons, and a few standing guard near the doors. They were agitated, their eyes darting around the room, their voices a constant, low-level murmur of fear and confusion.

Elias looked out from the shadows of the gallery, his heart beating steadily in his chest. He could see them clearly now. He could see their fear. He could see their disorganization.

He turned to the others. They were all there. Torin, still carrying Hobb, his face grim but steady. Maren, supporting Nella, her eyes sharp and alert. Kaelen, leaning against the wall, his face pale but his expression calm. Elara, her hand on her satchel, her eyes scanning the room with a scholar's precision.

They were in position.

Elias looked at the goblins, then at his companions. He didn't need to say a word. They all knew what had to be done next.

The stage was set.

Elias did not wait for the goblins to finish their frantic shouting. He stepped out from the shadows of the gallery railing, his boots making a soft, deliberate sound on the worn stone floor above.

Below, in the dim light of the main hall, the movement of the goblin horde slowed. They were a chaotic tangle of matted furs and rusted iron, huddled around a central pile of stolen grain sacks. The noise in the room was a low, jagged roar of confusion, a sound like a hive of angry wasps that had been kicked.

Elias raised his voice, letting it carry down into the open space without shouting. He projected it with the calm, steady authority of a man who had every intention of staying exactly where he was.

"Skrit," he called out. "I know you are in there."

The silence that followed was heavy, pressurized. The goblins below stopped their shuffling. A dozen yellow eyes snapped upward, fixed on the figure standing on the gallery. The air in the hall grew thick with a sudden, sharp tension, the kind that precedes a strike.

Elias turned to his companions. He nodded toward the group standing in the shadows behind him. Torin, still bearing the weight of Hobb on his back, shifted his stance to face the center of the hall. Maren moved to the edge of the gallery, her hand resting on the haft of her hammer, her eyes scanning the goblins below with cold, clinical precision. Elara and Kaelen flanked them, their postures relaxed but ready, a silent wall of competence behind Elias.

He looked back down at the goblins. He could see the way their eyes darted, looking for the others, looking for the threat. He saw the fear in their wide, dark pupils, a fear that was easily readable in the way their shoulders hunched and their hands gripped their crude weapons.

He knew Skrit Ironjaw was not a warrior. He was a raider, a scavenger, a petty king of a small, frightened band. He was not a hero. He was a man who had taken what he could and was now facing a group of people who had the strength, the skill, and the will to take it all back.

Elias took a step forward, placing one foot on the lowest rung of the gallery's support ladder.

"Come down, Skrit," Elias said, his voice cutting through the silence of the hall. "We have your people. We have your grain. And if you don't show yourself, I'm going to start taking it all. Piece by piece."

The goblins held their breath. The silence stretched, taut and brittle.

Then, from the darkness of the chief's room, a door creaked open.

A figure emerged. He was shorter than Elias, thicker, with a head that looked like it had been squeezed in a vice. He wore a patchwork of leather and chainmail, a crude crown of twisted iron on his brow. His face was a map of scars, his eyes small and hard, like two beads of black glass.

Skrit Ironjaw stepped into the light.

He did not run. He did not shout. He walked with a slow, deliberate pace, his eyes fixed on Elias, his hand resting on the hilt of a short, curved knife at his hip.

The goblins in the hall shifted, their bodies leaning toward their chief, their fear now tempered by a sense of hope. They had their king. They had their leader. And he was coming.

Elias watched him approach, his own posture steady, his gaze unblinking. He could see the calculation in Skrit's eyes, the rapid, frantic search for an opening, a weakness, a flaw in their formation.

But he found none.

He was faced with a wall of steel, of silence, of a quiet, deadly certainty that was more frightening than any shout, any blow, any display of power.

Skrit stopped at the center of the hall, his eyes sweeping over the gallery, over the five figures standing there, over the captured man on Torin's back, over the woman in Maren's arms.

He looked at Elias.

And for the first time, in the long, dark history of his small, frightened band, Skrit Ironjaw felt the weight of the world settle onto his shoulders.

He felt the silence.

And he felt the end.

The silence in the hall was not empty; it was heavy, pressing against the eardrums like deep water. Skrit Ironjaw stood motionless in the center of the flagstones, his curved knife still sheathed at his hip, his yellow eyes fixed on the gallery where the five companions stood. Below him, the goblins huddled around the pile of stolen grain sacks had gone still, their earlier panic replaced by a brittle, waiting tension. They looked to their chief, and he looked up at the humans, and for a long moment, neither side moved.

Elias let the quiet stretch, allowing the weight of the situation to settle into the bones of the room. He did not raise his voice. He did not draw his sword. He simply waited, letting Skrit measure the distance between the gallery and the floor, between his own small, scarred figure and the five armed, patient people looking down.

"Skrit," Elias said. His voice was low, carrying easily in the still air. "You have a choice to make. You can throw that knife, and we will put it back in your hand by the time you finish throwing it. Or you can put it away, and we can talk about what happens next."

Skrit's head tilted, a jerky motion that reminded Elias of a dog listening to a tone. The goblin's shoulders were hunched, his thick arms crossed over his chest, a defensive posture that seemed at odds with the casual way he held himself. He did not speak. He did not reach for the knife. He simply watched.

Maren shifted her weight, the broken cart axle strapped to her shoulder creaking softly under the strain. She was holding Nella, who was pale and trembling, her eyes wide and unseeing. Maren's hand rested on the hilt of her hammer, not gripping, just resting, a silent promise of consequence.

Torin stood behind them, his back straight despite the unconscious weight of Hobb slung across it. The cart driver's breathing was shallow, a faint rattle in his throat, but his body was limp, a burden that Torin carried with the same steady, uncomplaining grace he applied to everything. The soldier's own right shoulder was stiff, the old injury flaring under the strain, but his posture was rigid, a wall of quiet competence.

Elara stood to Elias's left, her satchel tight against her hip. She was watching the goblins below, her eyes tracking their subtle movements, the way their fingers twitched toward their own weapons, the way their eyes darted to the shadows. She said nothing, but her presence was an anchor, a reminder that observation was a form of power.

Skrit's gaze flickered to the grain sacks, then to the captives, and back to Elias. There was a calculation in his eyes, a cold, pragmatic assessment of odds. He was a raider, a scavenger, a creature of small, immediate concerns. He was not a warrior in the true sense; he was a bully who had found a wall to hide behind. And now that wall was gone.

The goblins below began to murmur, low, guttural sounds of confusion and fear. They looked at their chief, then at the gallery, then at their own hands. One of them, a young goblin with a missing tooth, shifted his weight, his eyes darting to the door behind him. He was looking for an exit, a way out, a way to survive.

Skrit saw it. His head snapped toward the young goblin, and for a second, his expression hardened, a flash of authority and control. But it was a bluff. Elias could see it in the way Skrit's fingers tightened on his belt, the way his throat worked as he swallowed. He was afraid. He was afraid of the humans, and he was afraid of his own people.

"Put the knife away, Skrit," Elias repeated. It was not a command, but a statement of fact. "It is not going to save you. It is only going to slow you down."

Skrit's hand moved. It was slow, deliberate, and full of hesitation. His fingers closed around the hilt of the knife, and for a second, Elias saw the tension in the goblin's arm, the effort it took to keep his hand still. Then, slowly, with a grinding reluctance that seemed to cost him more than the physical act, Skrit drew the knife from its sheath.

He held it out in front of him, the curved blade catching the faint light from the gallery. It was a crude weapon, poorly made, with a nick in the edge and a chip in the handle. It was not a symbol of power. It was a tool, and it was useless.

Skrit looked at Elias, and in his eyes, Elias saw not defiance, but relief. The goblin had been waiting for an out, a way to avoid the pain of failure, and the humans had offered it to him.

With a slow, heavy motion, Skrit dropped the knife. It hit the flagstones with a sharp, metallic clang that echoed in the silent hall. The sound was final, a period at the end of a sentence that had been dragging on for too long.

The goblins below flinched. Their hands fell to their sides. The tension in the room did not disappear, but it changed, shifting from a coiled spring to a slack rope. They were still afraid, but they were no longer fighting. They were surrendering.

Elias did not move. He let the moment hold, letting the goblins feel the weight of their decision, letting them see that the humans were not going to attack, were not going to punish, were not going to do anything but wait.

"Elara," Elias said, his voice low. "Get the rope."

Elara nodded, her hand moving to her satchel. She produced a length of thick, coarse hemp, the same kind that had bound the captives. It was rough, abrasive, and effective. She held it out to Maren, who took it with one hand, her other arm still supporting Nella.

"Torin," Elias said. "Set Hobb down. Gently."

Torin shifted his weight, easing the unconscious cart driver off his back and lowering him to the floor. Hobb's head hit the flagstones with a soft thud, and he stirred, his eyes fluttering open, but he did not wake. He was too tired, too drained, too broken to fight.

Maren knelt beside the goblins, her hammer resting on the floor, her hand steady as she began to bind their wrists. She worked quickly, efficiently, her movements precise and unemotional. She did not look at their faces. She did not speak to them. She was a professional, doing a job, and she did it with the same cold, pragmatic competence that she brought to everything.

The goblins did not resist. They stood still, their hands bound, their heads bowed. They were small, broken things, defeated by the weight of their own fear.

Skrit stood in the center of the hall, his hands empty, his eyes fixed on the floor. He looked smaller than he had when he had first emerged from the shadows. He looked tired. He looked defeated.

Elias watched him, and for a moment, he felt a flicker of something that was not quite pity, but was close. It was the recognition of a shared burden, a shared understanding of the cost of failure. Skrit had tried to be strong, to be a leader, to be a raider. And he had failed. Not because he was weak, but because the world had changed, and he had not.

"We are going to leave you here," Elias said. His voice was calm, steady, and final. "You are going to be bound, and you are going to wait for us to return. If any of you move, if any of you try to run, if any of you try to fight, we will come back. And we will not be gentle."

Skrit did not answer. He did not need to. The message was clear, and it was understood.

Elias turned to the group. "We are done here," he said. "Let's go."

They moved out of the hall, their footsteps soft and steady on the flagstones. The goblins watched them go, their eyes wide with a mixture of fear and confusion. They did not understand why the humans were leaving, why they were not attacking, why they were not punishing. They did not need to. They only needed to know that they were safe, and that the danger was over.

As they reached the door, Elias paused. He looked back at Skrit, at the small, scarred goblin standing alone in the center of the hall. For a second, their eyes met. And in that second, Elias saw not a monster, not a raider, not a threat, but a man. A small, frightened, broken man who had lost everything.

And then Elias turned, and he walked out into the dark, leaving the goblins to their silence, their fear, and their surrender.

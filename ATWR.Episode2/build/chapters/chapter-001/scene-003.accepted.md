Rain lashed against the rough-hewn bark of the oak where Elias stopped, turning the forest floor into a slick, mud-churned trap that demanded constant attention. The sky had opened up fully again, a sudden shift from the gentle drizzle of the village to a proper deluge that smelled of wet wool and turned earth. It was the kind of weather that made a traveler’s clothes heavy with water and his boots fill within minutes, yet Elias kept his stride steady, eyes scanning the line of trees for any unnatural break in the canopy or a shadow that didn’t belong. He held his cloak back against the wind, letting it flap slightly behind him, while keeping one hand near the hilt of his sword—not out of fear of immediate attack, but because the rain made every step into the unknown feel like a choice between caution and momentum. Behind him, Maren adjusted her pack with a grunt that sounded less like pain and more like satisfaction at having finished checking the gear. The broken cart axle she had dragged from the farm’s edge was strapped to her shoulder now, a clumsy but necessary weight that balanced against the hammer slung across her back. Her hands were gloved in thick leather, though the cuffs showed signs of dampness already soaking through; she’d ignored the earlier concern about her burned skin because the cold rain kept her from feeling the sting too much. “We move now,” Elias said, his voice low and steady despite the wind trying to tear it away. “If we wait for the storm to break, the trail washes out.” Maren nodded once, tapping the axe haft against her palm as if checking its balance rather than comforting herself. “Mud’ll hide tracks in half an hour,” she replied, voice practical and clipped. “But goblins don’t care about mud. They leave their own mess regardless of rain or sun.” Elara stepped forward with a notebook clutched to her chest, water dripping from the rim of her hood onto the pages where she’d sketched footprints earlier. She didn’t open it, but she did glance at Elias over the top of her spectacles, which were fogged by the humidity. “The arrowhead’s still sharp,” she murmured. “And the rope fragment—” She stopped mid-sentence as Kaelen flicked his gauntlet with a soft *click*, casting a faint ring of golden light through the gloom ahead. The brass relic pulsed gently, its inner glow steady despite the storm, offering enough illumination to read the trail markers without straining eyes or risking exposure to the dark corners of the woods. “Good,” Elias said, glancing at the mage’s hand. “Keep it on until we’re clear.” Kaelen gave a small shrug, his expression unreadable in the dim light; he didn’t seem bothered by the effort it took to maintain the glow, nor did he comment on how much energy such magic must cost him over time. Torin moved ahead into the tree line, boots sinking slightly into the mud as he tested each foothold with a practiced rhythm. He wasn’t scanning for threats so much as testing the ground itself—checking where branches had fallen, whether roots had shifted under pressure, and if any animal tracks might confuse their path. “Left side’s slicker,” he called back over the sound of wind rattling leaves. “Right bank holds better.” Maren stepped up beside him, taking point on that flank now while Elias kept watch from the rear. “Lead me where it doesn’t slip,” she said, voice firm despite the rain. “I’ll follow your lead if you’re sure.” Torin didn’t hesitate. He gestured toward a narrow ridge that ran parallel to the creek bend, its surface hardened by recent wind and exposed to less runoff than the lowlands below. “That way,” he said. “Steeper but firmer footing.” They began moving again, stepping carefully over roots and fallen branches that the rain had loosened. Elias walked near the center of the group, watching both ahead and behind, making sure no one lagged or strayed too close to the edge where the creek widened into a rocky embankment. The broken cart axle creaked against Maren’s shoulder with every step, a dull reminder of what they’d left behind at the farm. She didn’t complain about it; instead, she shifted her weight occasionally to redistribute the load, letting Elias see that she understood the necessity of carrying it even if it slowed them down. “We’ll need to lighten up once we cross the tree line,” he said quietly. “Leave extra rope and tools there.” “Agreed,” Maren replied without hesitation. “But I’m not leaving Nella’s straps or the iron fittings behind unless there’s no choice.” “Not leaving anything vital,” Elias added. “Just the non-essentials. We’ll grab what we can once we’ve secured the captives.” Elara walked slightly ahead of him now, using her own small lantern to check for loose stones or hidden dips in the trail. She carried a satchel filled with chalk and brushes, ready to mark any new evidence they found along the way. “The rope fragment’s still intact,” she said, holding it up so Elias could see it better. “It was tucked under straw near the doorframe. The goblins must’ve used it to drag something heavy out quickly.” Elias nodded, studying the frayed ends where hemp fibers had been pulled apart by force. “Hasty retreat,” he confirmed. “They didn’t want to be seen here.” Torin paused briefly at a cluster of bushes that seemed unusually bare of leaves, though the season wasn’t late enough for autumn shedding. He leaned forward and sniffed the air, his nose twitching slightly as if catching something faint beneath the scent of wet wood and rain. “Something’s off,” he said after a moment. “Not goblin smell, but… stale fear.” Elias frowned, stepping closer to inspect the bush himself. Inside, near the base of the trunk, lay a small smear of dried blood—human, not animal—and something else that looked like charred fabric caught on the bark. “Burnt gear?” Elara asked, reaching out to take a sample with her gloved hand. She held it up for inspection, noting how the fibers had blackened unevenly, as if caught in a sudden fire or exposed to heat quickly before being discarded. “Could be from Nella’s crate,” she suggested. “Or maybe they burned something else first.” Maren stepped forward and touched the charred cloth with her hammer’s grip, testing whether it was still hot enough to burn her skin. “Cold enough to touch,” she said. “But too fresh to have been there long.” Torin nodded again, signaling them to move on without lingering. “Keep moving,” he said. “If they’re burning things, they’re preparing for something bigger than just raiding farms.” Elias didn’t press further; he knew that speculation would only slow them down when they had limited time and energy. Instead, he focused on keeping the group together and watching for signs of goblin movement beyond what they could already see in the gloom. As they crossed into denser woods where light barely penetrated, Kaelen’s gauntlet dimmed slightly, its inner glow struggling against the rain-induced haze. He didn’t mention it, though Elias noticed the change immediately and adjusted his own pace accordingly. “Save your energy,” he said quietly. “We’ll need you when we hit the keep.” Kaelen gave a small smile that barely registered in the dim light. “I’ve got enough left for smoke screens,” he replied. “And maybe a shield if things get ugly.” Elias nodded again, trusting that judgment more than any spell slot count could explain. The storm continued to pour down around them, rain falling hard enough to blur outlines and make speech difficult without raising voices. Yet the group kept moving forward, each member aware of their role in maintaining cohesion under these conditions. Maren led with her hammer ready, Torin watched the path ahead for traps or ambushes, Elara recorded findings as they went, Kaelen managed visibility and defense, and Elias coordinated everything while keeping an eye on morale. They weren’t heroes by some grand design—they were five people who had agreed to work together because no one else would help them survive another night of goblin raids. And tonight, with the rain masking their approach and the trail guiding them toward a ruined keep that sat beyond the tree line, they were closer than ever to proving themselves capable enough to face whatever lay ahead.

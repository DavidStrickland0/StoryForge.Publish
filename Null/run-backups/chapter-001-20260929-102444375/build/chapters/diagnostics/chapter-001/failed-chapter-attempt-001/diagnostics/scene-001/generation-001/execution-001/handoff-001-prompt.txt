The neon sign for The Rusty Anchor buzzed with a low, persistent hum that Ethan felt in his molars before he saw the light. It was a Friday, which meant the street outside was a river of bodies flowing toward the door, and the air inside smelled of stale beer, spilled pretzels, and the sharp, metallic tang of ozone that always accompanied supers.

Ethan adjusted the strap of his messenger bag, trying to keep his posture relaxed, though his shoulders had already begun to creep upward. He had come for the cheap wings and the free Wi-Fi, a mundane combination that usually kept him grounded. Tonight, however, the air felt heavy with a different kind of pressure. A low thrumming vibration, like a large engine idling in the next room.

He spotted the source near the back booth, a corner that usually went unclaimed because the lighting was poor and the table was bolted to the floor. There, draped across the table with the casual arrogance of someone who had never been told "no" by a wall, was Kael.

Kael was a Class-A Strength and Durability super. He was seven feet of dense muscle, wrapped in a leather jacket that looked like it had been tailored for a man half his size. His skin had a faint, pearlescent sheen under the bar lights, a side effect of his invulnerability that most people found unsettling. He wasn’t drinking; he was holding a glass of water, the condensation beading on the surface but refusing to drip, seemingly frozen in his grip.

The deference around him was a physical thing. The bartender, a small man with a thick neck, moved with the careful, fluid grace of someone navigating a minefield. He didn’t look at Kael’s face. He looked at the glass, refilling it from a distance, his hand steady despite the fact that Kael had just tapped the table with two fingers. The tap had cracked the laminate surface, sending spiderweb fractures racing across the wood. No one had said a word. No one had called the police. No one had even flinched.

Ethan watched a group of college students at the next table. They were laughing, but their eyes kept darting toward Kael, checking his position, measuring the distance. One of them, a young woman with bright red hair, had pulled her chair slightly back from the table, creating a small, invisible buffer zone. When Kael shifted, leaning forward to rest his chin on his hand, she stopped talking mid-sentence. She didn’t look away, but her body went rigid, a statue in a world of moving parts.

This was the hierarchy. It wasn’t written in the law books, though the laws certainly bent around figures like Kael. It was written in the way people moved, in the way they spoke, in the silent, collective agreement that some people were above the rules. They were celebrities, strategic assets, gods in human skin. And the rest of them? They were just background noise.

Ethan took a sip of his water, the glass feeling normal and fragile in his hand. He could feel the weight of the room’s attention, even if no one was looking directly at him. It was a subtle thing, a gravitational pull. Everyone was orbiting Kael. The air in the bar felt thicker, charged with the static of contained power.

He realized he was gripping the table edge. His knuckles were white. He let go, smoothing his hand out on the sticky surface, and tried to remember why he was here. Cheap wings. Free Wi-Fi. He was seeking normalcy.

But normalcy felt like a thin coat in a cold wind. He watched Kael take a sip of water, the liquid disappearing into his mouth with a sound that seemed too loud in the quiet space between the patrons. The super didn’t even blink. He was waiting for something. Or perhaps he was just existing, imposing his presence on the room like a storm front that hadn’t yet broken.

Ethan checked his phone. 8:42 PM. He had been here for twenty minutes. He had barely touched his food. The realization that he was still sitting there, watching a super drink water, was enough to make him feel foolish. He should leave. He should go home, open a book, and pretend the world wasn’t a place where people could break tables with a tap and walk away without consequence.

He stood up, his chair scraping against the floor. The sound was sharp, cutting through the low murmur of conversation. Heads turned. Not Kael’s. He didn’t turn. He didn’t need to. The others turned, their eyes flicking to Ethan with a mixture of curiosity and mild irritation. He had interrupted the flow. He had broken the silence.

Ethan offered a small, apologetic smile to the woman with the red hair, then turned and walked toward the door. His heart was beating fast, a frantic rhythm in his chest. He felt exposed, thin, and entirely ordinary. He pushed through the heavy glass door, stepping out into the cool night air, and didn’t look back.

The neon sign outside the window buzzed with a low, persistent hum that seemed to vibrate right through the glass and into the floorboards. Ethan leaned his elbow on the sticky surface of the bar, watching the condensation bead on the inside of his glass and slide down in a slow, erratic path. He had come here because it was close to his apartment, because the beer was cheap, and because the overhead television was currently showing a highlight reel of a superhero catching a falling cargo plane with one hand. The audience in the studio, visible in the bottom right corner of the screen, erupted in applause that had been edited to sound like a thunderclap.

Ethan took a sip of his lager. It tasted like copper and cold water. He preferred it that way; it reminded him that he was a person who could drink and then walk home, rather than fly over the city or bench a city bus. The bar, The Rusty Anchor, was a mid-tier establishment that catered to the working class and the occasional off-duty hero. There were three supers present tonight. One was a woman with skin that shimmered like oil on wet pavement, sitting alone in the corner booth, reading a paperback. Another was a man whose hair stood on end in a permanent static charge, laughing loudly with two friends at a high-top. The third was the one who had just walked in.

The door swung open, admitting a gust of cool autumn air and the smell of rain. The man who entered was large. Not just tall, but wide in a way that seemed to compress the space around him. He wore a black t-shirt that stretched across his shoulders, and his jaw was set in a rigid line. As he walked toward the bar, the floorboards creaked under his weight. Ethan watched him with the detached curiosity of a man observing a shark swim past a diving bell. The man didn’t look at anyone. He didn’t need to. His presence announced itself before his voice did.

"Two whiskeys," the man said, his voice a low rumble that seemed to originate from the back of his throat.

The bartender, a young woman with nervous hands, poured the drinks with a speed that suggested she was trying to get them out of the way. The man paid with a crisp bill, leaving a generous tip, and then turned to the bar. He chose a spot at the far end, near the exit, but close enough to the center of the room to command attention. Ethan shifted his own stool slightly, angling his body away from the man’s line of sight. It was a small, unconscious movement, a reflex born of years of navigating a world where someone like that could break a table with a flick of his wrist.

Ethan turned back to his glass, trying to focus on the television. The news anchor was talking about a new legislation bill that would require supers to register their powers with the federal government. The headline read: *Regulation or Revolution? The Future of the Powered.* Ethan snorted quietly. Registration. As if a form and a fee could contain the fact that a man could lift a car or a woman could stop a bullet with her skin. The law was a fiction, a paper shield that dissolved the moment a super decided to ignore it. He had seen it happen before, in the news, in the rumors, in the way the police sometimes stood back and let a dispute between two heroes play out while they kept their hands visible but their distance safe.

The man at the far end of the bar took a long drink, then set the glass down. He looked around the room, his eyes sweeping over the patrons with a dismissive scan. He was looking for something, or perhaps just asserting that he was there. Ethan caught his eye for a brief second. The man’s gaze was cold, appraising. Ethan looked away first, a habit of deference that he knew was futile but couldn't shake. He was a normal person. He was the kind of person who paid taxes, worried about rent, and checked the weather forecast before going outside. He was not a threat. He was not a prize. He was background noise.

The television switched to a commercial for a brand of energy drinks that featured a superhero holding a can in one hand while smiling at the camera. The tagline was: *Power in a Can.* Ethan felt a sudden, sharp irritation. It wasn’t the ad itself, but the casualness of it. The way the power was packaged, sold, and consumed. It made the rest of the world feel small. It made his own life feel like a rough draft of someone else’s masterpiece.

He finished his beer and signaled the bartender. She brought him a fresh one, and he paid for it, adding a tip that was larger than the previous one. He was trying to be good. He was trying to be a good citizen in a world that didn’t really have a place for him. He was trying to be someone who could look at the man at the far end of the bar and feel nothing but a mild, distant curiosity.

But the man was moving. He had left his stool and was now walking toward the center of the bar. He wasn’t walking with purpose; he was walking with weight. His steps were heavy, deliberate. He stopped near the corner booth where the woman with the shimmering skin was sitting. She looked up, her eyes widening slightly, and then she looked away, her gaze dropping to her book. The man said something to her. Ethan couldn’t hear the words, but he could see the tension in her shoulders, the way her hands tightened on the edges of the paperback.

Ethan’s heart rate began to climb. He didn’t know why. He had no reason to care. The woman was a super. She could probably throw the man through the wall if she wanted to. Or she could simply walk away. But she didn’t. She sat still, her face a mask of polite indifference, but her eyes were darting toward the exit.

The man leaned in closer. He said something else, and this time Ethan caught a fragment of it. *...you don’t have to be so difficult...* The man’s voice was low, but it carried. The other patrons at the high-top had stopped laughing. They were watching. The bartender was wiping a glass that was already clean, her movements jerky and fast.

Ethan felt a cold prickle at the base of his neck. He looked at the door. It was twenty feet away. He could get there in five seconds. He could get out of the bar, into the street, and disappear into the crowd. He could go home. He could lock his door and drink a glass of water and forget that he had ever seen this.

But the man was still leaning in. The woman was still sitting still. And Ethan was still sitting at his stool, his glass untouched, his heart hammering against his ribs. He was a coward. He knew that. He was a normal man in a world of gods, and he was too afraid to be anything else. He was too afraid to be hurt. He was too afraid to be wrong.

He pushed his stool back from the bar. The movement was small, but it was enough. The man’s head turned, his eyes locking onto Ethan’s. There was no anger in his gaze, only a cold, calculating interest. He was assessing Ethan. Measuring him. Deciding if he was worth the effort.

Ethan met his gaze. He held it for a second, then looked away. He turned his back to the man and started walking toward the door. His legs felt heavy, as if the air around him had thickened. He could feel the eyes of the other patrons on his back. He could hear the man’s footsteps behind him, slow and deliberate.

He reached the door. He pushed it open. The cool air hit his face, and for a moment, he felt a sense of relief so profound it was almost painful. He stepped out into the street, the noise of the city washing over him. The sound of cars, of people talking, of life continuing. He turned and looked back through the glass. The man was still standing there, watching him.

Ethan walked away. He did not look back again. He walked until the bar was a blur of neon light in the distance, and then he turned the corner and disappeared into the shadows of the alleyway. His heart was still beating fast, but the panic was fading, replaced by a dull, aching guilt. He had left. He had run. He had done what he always did.

He pulled his phone out of his pocket and checked the time. It was late. He had to be home before his mother called. She would worry. She would ask him if he was okay. He would lie. He would say he was fine. He would say he had just had a bad day.

He walked home, his steps quick and uneven. The city hummed around him, vibrant and indifferent. Somewhere in the distance, a siren wailed, a thin, reedy sound that cut through the night. Ethan kept his head down, his eyes fixed on the pavement. He was a normal man. He was a good man. He was a man who knew his place.

And for now, that was enough.

The rain hit his face before the door clicked shut behind him.

Cold. Soaking. The kind of cold that seeped into the fibers of his jacket and settled in his bones, a sharp, physical reminder that he was alive, that his skin still registered the world’s texture, that he was a body and not just a pair of eyes watching from the safety of a stool. He didn’t wipe it from his eyes. He let it run down his cheeks, mixing with the heat that was still flushed across his skin, a confusing, miserable cocktail of adrenaline and shame.

He turned his back on The Anchor.

The neon sign buzzed overhead, casting a sickly red glow onto the wet pavement. Through the glass, he could see the shapes of the patrons, blurred and static. The man in the suit was still there. The woman was still there. The tableau hadn’t broken. For a second, a morbid part of his brain wanted to turn around, to check if the air had shifted, if the tension in the room had snapped, if the heavy, oppressive weight he’d felt pressing against his chest had lifted.

He didn’t turn around.

Instead, he walked.

His legs felt like they were moving through water, sluggish and uncooperative. The beer bottle in his hand was a cold, hard anchor, the only real thing he had left. He gripped it until his knuckles ached, a dull, grounding pain that kept him tethered to the present moment. He didn’t throw it. He didn’t crush it. He just held it, a useless, heavy thing that reminded him he was still solid, still here.

The street was empty. It was late, and the rain had driven everyone indoors, leaving the city streets slick and reflective. The sound of the rain was a constant, rhythmic drumming against his hood, a white noise that drowned out the thoughts he was trying to suppress.

*You left her.*

The thought was simple, brutal, and accurate. He had looked at a woman who was terrified, who was cornered, who was about to be hurt by a man who could bench press a car, and he had chosen himself. He had chosen his pulse. He had chosen the beating of his heart and the steady rhythm of his breathing. He had chosen survival.

It was the right choice. He knew it was the right choice. Everyone knew it was the right choice. You didn’t intervene against a super. You didn’t step in when a god was angry. You watched, you recorded, you prayed, and you left. That was the social contract. That was the law of the world. Supers were the weather; you didn’t argue with a hurricane. You got out of the way.

But getting out of the way felt like a betrayal.

He walked faster. The pavement was slick, and he had to be careful not to slip, his shoes losing their grip on the wet concrete. He kept his eyes on the ground, on the puddles that reflected the city lights in distorted, dancing shapes. He needed to keep moving. If he stopped, the guilt would catch up to him. It was already there, a heavy stone in his stomach, but movement kept it at bay.

He passed a row of closed shops, their windows dark and silent. A stray cat darted out from under a parked car, hissing at the rain, and disappeared into the shadows. Ethan watched it go, envying its instinct, its simple, direct connection to the world. It didn’t have to calculate. It didn’t have to weigh the odds. It just reacted.

He reached the corner of 5th and Main. The traffic light was red. He stood at the curb, the rain pouring down on him, the bottle still in his hand. Cars splashed through the intersection, their headlights cutting through the downpour, blinding and bright. He waited for the light to change, for the cars to pass, for the world to keep moving without him.

When the light turned green, he crossed the street.

The other side was quieter. The buildings were taller, older, their facades stained with age and grime. He walked toward the apartment complex where he lived, a small, unassuming brick building that felt like a shell, a protection against the world outside. He didn’t know why he was going there. He didn’t know why he was walking at all. His body was moving on autopilot, driven by a need to be somewhere safe, somewhere private, somewhere where he could take off his jacket and feel the weight of the bottle and the weight of the guilt and the weight of the rain.

He reached the entrance. The door was heavy, made of thick oak, and it took all his strength to push it open. The lobby was dimly lit, the air stale and warm. He could smell the dust, the old carpet, the faint scent of the cleaning products the manager used. It was a smell that was entirely human, entirely ordinary, and it grounded him.

He took the stairs.

One flight. Two flights. Three flights.

His legs burned. His breath came in short, sharp gasps. The beer bottle was sweating in his hand, the condensation mixing with the rain on his skin. He reached his apartment, fumbled for his keys, and finally, after a few frustrating moments, the lock clicked open.

He stepped inside.

The apartment was small, sparse, and quiet. The rain was muffled by the walls, a distant, soft hum. He closed the door behind him and leaned against it, the bottle still in his hand. He looked at the room, at the simple furniture, the unmade bed, the stack of books on the desk. It was a life. A small, ordinary, fragile life.

He didn’t throw the bottle away. He didn’t turn on the lights. He just stood there, in the dark, in the rain, in the silence, feeling the weight of the choice he had made.

He had left her.

And he had survived.

And that was enough.

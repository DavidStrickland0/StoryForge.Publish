The Anchor smelled of stale hops and aggressive perfume, a contradiction that felt appropriate for a place that tried to be cozy and industrial simultaneously. It was a Tuesday, which was the best kind of night for Ethan. Tuesday meant the regulars were there, but the tourists and the date-night couples who required ambient lighting and conversational fillers were thinner on the ground. The noise floor was a steady, manageable hum rather than a chaotic roar.

He sat at the end of the bar, near the restrooms but away from the main traffic flow. He liked the observation. It was a passive hobby, one that had become increasingly necessary as the years went by and the skyline of his city began to sprout new, impossible architecture.

A woman in a tailored suit sat at the bar three stools down, typing furiously on a tablet. She didn’t look up when the bartender poured her drink. She simply slid the glass over, took a sip, and continued. Ethan watched her for a moment. He didn’t know who she was, but the way she occupied space without apologizing for it told him everything he needed to know. She was someone for whom the world had been designed to be convenient.

This was the reality of the modern era. The supers had arrived with a slow, tectonic shift in the social contract. Ten years ago, a man with super strength would have been a novelty, a freak show, a subject of intense scientific scrutiny. Now, they were just… people. Or at least, they were people who had decided that the rules applied differently to them.

Ethan took a sip of his beer. It was cold and bitter, exactly as he liked it. He was a software developer, a job that required long periods of quiet focus and a tolerance for the mundane. It was a good job. It paid the rent on his small apartment, allowed him to eat dinner without worrying about expiration dates, and gave him a reason to exist that didn’t involve looking up at the sky and wondering if anyone was falling.

He was twenty-eight, average height, average build, with eyes that were a shade of brown that blended into the background of any group photo. He had no special abilities, no hidden heritage, no tragic backstory that explained his competence. He was just a guy who knew how to fix a leaky faucet and code a database. In a world where people could throw cars or turn invisible, that was a specific kind of vulnerability.

He didn’t feel afraid, exactly. Fear was a physical thing, a tightening in the chest, a quickening of the pulse. What Ethan felt was a low-level hum of awareness, a constant background noise of calculation. He knew, with the certainty of a man who had read the news too much, that if he bumped into the wrong person, if he was in the wrong place during the wrong rescue, if he was standing too close to someone who decided to fly indoors, the consequences would be severe. And the law, he had learned, was a flexible thing when it came to collateral damage.

The bar was half-full. The lighting was dim, casting long shadows across the polished wood of the counter. The bartender was a large man with a gentle face, wiping down the glass with a rag that looked as though it had been used to clean the engine of a tractor. He moved with a slowness that suggested he had been doing this job for a long time and had made peace with the mediocrity of it.

Ethan picked up his pretzel and tore off a small piece. He chewed slowly, watching the other patrons. A couple in the corner were arguing about something, their voices low but sharp. A group of young men near the entrance were laughing, their bodies relaxed, their posture confident. They were probably supers, or at least they thought they were. Ethan couldn't tell. In this city, you learned not to judge by appearance. The woman in the suit could have been a telepath. The bartender could have been invulnerable. The group of young men could have been able to stop time.

The uncertainty was part of the game. You had to assume everyone was dangerous until proven otherwise. That was the new social contract. You didn't ask, "Are you a super?" You asked, "How close should I stand?"

Ethan finished his pretzel and set the plate down. He felt a small sense of satisfaction. He had chosen the right spot. He had chosen the right time. He had chosen the right drink. He was in control of his immediate environment, which was all he could realistically ask for.

He looked out the window. The street outside was busy with the evening rush. Cars glided past, their headlights cutting through the dusk. Pedestrians hurried along the sidewalk, some of them looking up at the sky, checking for any signs of the heroes who often resolved their traffic problems. A few of them were flying, their silhouettes sharp against the darkening sky. They moved with an ease that made Ethan’s legs feel heavy.

He turned back to the bar. The woman in the suit had finished her drink and was gathering her things. She stood up, her movements fluid and efficient. She dropped a bill on the counter, didn't wait for change, and walked out the door. The bartender didn't even look up.

Ethan watched her go. He felt a pang of something he couldn't quite name. Envy, perhaps. Or maybe just recognition. She was moving through the world with a confidence that he could only aspire to. She didn't worry about the laws of physics or the limits of human frailty. She just went where she wanted to go, and the world made way for her.

He took another sip of his beer. The foam on his lip was cold and sticky. He wiped it away with his thumb and looked down at his hand. It was a normal hand. It had veins, it had knuckles, it had a small scar on the thumb from a paper cut years ago. It was a hand that could type, that could hold a mug, that could shake another person's hand. It was a hand that was limited by the same constraints as everyone else's.

And that was fine. That was his life.

He sat back in his chair and let out a slow breath. The hum of the bar seemed to settle around him, a protective shell against the chaos of the world outside. He was safe. He was ordinary. He was home.

For now.

He watched the door for a moment, waiting to see if anyone else would leave. The bar remained quiet, the conversation a low murmur that faded into the background. Ethan picked up his beer and took another sip, his mind drifting to the work he needed to do tomorrow. He had a bug in the code that had been bugging him for three days. It was a simple thing, a logical error that he just couldn't find. But now, in the quiet of the bar, with the taste of the beer on his tongue and the steady rhythm of the city outside, he felt a sense of calm wash over him.

He was just a man sitting in a bar, enjoying a drink and a quiet moment. It was a simple thing, but in a world that was constantly changing, it was a luxury he could afford. And for now, that was enough.

The front door chimed, a bright, brassy note that cut through the low hum of the refrigerator and the distant rain against the windows. Ethan didn’t look up immediately. He took a slow sip of his beer, letting the cold bitterness settle on his tongue, and watched the reflection of the bar in the darkened mirror behind the taps. The door opened again, wider this time, and the smell of wet wool and ozone drifted in before the figure did.

He was large. That was the first thing that registered, a simple fact of geometry that seemed to displace the air in the room. He wore a tailored charcoal suit that strained at the shoulders, the fabric pulled taut across a chest that looked like it had been carved from granite. His hair was slicked back, dark and severe, and his eyes were a pale, washed-out blue that seemed to scan the room with the detached efficiency of a predator checking for prey. He didn’t look at the bartender. He didn’t look at the couple in the corner, who were suddenly very quiet, their conversation dissolving into a shared, uncomfortable silence.

Ethan felt a prickle of unease at the base of his neck, a low-level alarm that had no logical trigger. He was just a man sitting in a bar. He had a job, a modest apartment, and a life that moved at the speed of walking. This man, however, moved with the heavy, deliberate grace of someone who had never been told he could not do something. He walked to the center of the room, his footsteps silent on the polished wood, and stopped.

The woman in the corner, the one who had been nursing a gin and tonic with a look of polite detachment, was still there. She had set her glass down, her hand resting on the table, her fingers drumming a nervous, irregular rhythm against the wood. She was small, unremarkable in her grey blazer, and she was looking at the man with a mixture of fear and a desperate, clinging hope that he might simply walk away.

He didn’t walk away. He pulled out the chair opposite her and sat down, the movement so smooth it looked like liquid. He didn’t speak. He just looked at her, his pale eyes fixed on her face, and waited.

The bartender, a burly man with a thick beard, wiped a glass with a rag that was already clean. He glanced at Ethan, then at the man in the suit, then back at the glass. He didn’t ask for an ID. He didn’t check the time. He simply slid a fresh ice pitcher onto the table in front of the large man, the sound of the cubes clinking like a tiny, sharp warning.

Ethan’s heart began to beat faster, a steady thud against his ribs that he could feel in his fingertips. He watched the woman’s hand. It was trembling. The man reached out, not to touch her, but to pick up the ice pitcher and pour himself a drink of water. He drank it in one long, steady pull, his throat moving in a rhythmic, hypnotic pulse. When he set the glass down, he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, a gesture of casual, absolute dominance.

The social contract of the bar, the unspoken rules of where you could sit and who you could talk to, seemed to evaporate. The couple in the corner had stopped talking entirely. The bartender had turned his back, pretending to organize the taps, but his shoulders were hunched, his movements stiff. The air in the room had grown thick, charged with a static electricity that made the hairs on Ethan’s arms stand up.

Ethan looked at the door. It was ten feet away. He could get up. He could walk to the door, step out into the rain, and be gone in less than a minute. He could be safe. He could be home by the time the man in the suit finished his second drink. The thought was simple, logical, and immediately accompanied by a sharp, hot flash of shame.

The woman was still sitting there. She had picked up her glass and was holding it with both hands, her knuckles white. She was trying to be brave, or perhaps she was just too terrified to move. She looked like a normal person, a person who had a job and a life and a right to sit in a bar without being treated like a piece of furniture to be inspected by a god.

Ethan’s hand tightened on his beer bottle. The glass creaked under the pressure, a small, fragile sound that seemed to hang in the air. He could feel the weight of the room on him, the collective holding of breath, the unspoken plea for someone to do something. Everyone in the bar was watching the man in the suit, waiting to see what he would do next, waiting to see if he would break something, or someone, or the world itself.

He was a normal person. He knew that. He knew that if he stood up, if he said a word, if he made a move, the man in the suit would look at him with those pale, empty eyes and he would be nothing. He would be a bug. He would be erased. The laws of the land, the police, the courts, they were all just paper and ink, useless against a man who could crush a tank with his bare hands.

The man in the suit set his glass down. He leaned forward, his elbows on the table, his face inches from the woman’s. He didn’t smile. He didn’t sneer. He just looked at her, his expression one of mild, curious interest, like a child watching an insect crawl across a pavement.

Ethan’s breath caught in his throat. His hand was still gripping the bottle, his knuckles white, his muscles tense. He could feel the heat rising in his face, a flush of anger and fear that burned in his chest. He wanted to stand up. He wanted to throw the bottle. He wanted to do something, anything, to break the spell of his own paralysis.

But he didn’t move. He sat there, frozen, his eyes fixed on the door, his mind racing through the geometry of escape. He could go left, through the kitchen, where the bartender was standing. He could go right, through the crowd, where the couple was sitting. Or he could go straight, through the front door, where the man in the suit was sitting.

Every path seemed blocked. Every option seemed to lead to the same place: the man in the suit, and the terrible, crushing weight of his power. Ethan swallowed, his throat dry, his heart pounding in his ears. He was trapped in his own body, a prisoner of his own caution, watching a normal woman be dismantled by a superhuman, and doing nothing.

The man in the suit reached out and placed his hand on the table, between them. His fingers were long, elegant, and utterly still. He didn’t touch her. He didn’t need to. The space between them was enough. The silence was enough.

Ethan looked down at his beer. The liquid was dark, almost black, and it trembled slightly in the glass, a subtle vibration that he couldn’t quite place. He looked up again, and for a second, the man in the suit’s eyes flickered, just for a moment, toward the door.

Ethan’s hand moved. It wasn’t a conscious decision. It was a reflex, a primal urge to survive that overrode every other thought, every social expectation, every shred of pride. He stood up, his chair scraping against the floor, a harsh, jarring sound that cut through the heavy air.

The man in the suit didn’t look up. The woman didn’t look up. The bartender didn’t look up.

Ethan walked toward the door. His legs felt weak, his knees trembling, but he kept moving. He pushed through the crowd, his shoulder brushing against a table, his eyes fixed on the exit. He was going to leave. He was going to get out. He was going to be safe.

And as he stepped through the door, the heavy, oppressive weight in the room seemed to shift, just slightly, just enough to let him breathe.

The rain hit his face before the door clicked shut behind him.

The cold was soaking, a sharp, physical reminder that he was alive, that his skin still registered the world’s texture, that he was a body and not just a pair of eyes watching from the safety of a stool. He didn’t wipe it from his eyes. He let it run down his cheeks, mixing with the heat that was still flushed across his skin, a confusing, miserable cocktail of adrenaline and shame.

He turned his back on The Anchor.

The neon sign buzzed overhead, casting a sickly red glow onto the wet pavement. Through the glass, he could see the shapes of the patrons, blurred and static. The man in the suit was still there. The woman was still there. The tableau hadn’t broken. For a second, a morbid part of his brain wanted to turn around, to check if the air had shifted, if the tension in the room had snapped, if the heavy, oppressive weight he’d felt pressing against his chest had lifted.

He didn’t turn around.

Instead, he walked.

His legs felt like they were moving through water, sluggish and uncooperative. The beer bottle in his hand was a cold, hard anchor, the only real thing he had left. He gripped it until his knuckles ached, a dull, grounding pain that kept him tethered to the present moment. He didn’t throw it. He didn’t crush it. He just held it, a useless, heavy thing that reminded him he was still solid, still here.

The street was empty. It was late, and the rain had driven everyone indoors, leaving the city streets slick and reflective. The sound of the rain was a constant, rhythmic drumming against his hood, a white noise that drowned out the thoughts he was trying to suppress.

*You left her.*

The thought was simple, brutal, and accurate. He had looked at a woman who was terrified, who was cornered, who was about to be hurt by a man who could bench press a car, and he had chosen himself. He had chosen his pulse. He had chosen the beating of his heart and the steady rhythm of his breathing. He had chosen survival.

It was the right choice. He knew it was the right choice. Everyone knew it was the right choice. You didn’t intervene against a super. You didn’t step in when a god was angry. You watched, you recorded, you prayed, and you left. That was the social contract. That was the law of the world. Supers were the weather; you didn’t argue with a hurricane. You got out of the way.

But getting out of the way felt like a betrayal.

He walked faster. The pavement was slick, and he had to be careful not to slip, his shoes losing their grip on the wet concrete. He kept his eyes on the ground, on the puddles that reflected the city lights in distorted, dancing shapes. He needed to keep moving. If he stopped, the guilt would catch up to him. It was already there, a heavy stone in his stomach, but movement kept it at bay.

He passed a row of closed shops, their windows dark and silent. A stray cat darted out from under a parked car, hissing at the rain, and disappeared into the shadows. Ethan watched it go, envying its instinct, its simple, direct connection to the world. It didn’t have to calculate. It didn’t have to weigh the odds. It just reacted.

He reached the corner of 5th and Main. The traffic light was red. He stood at the curb, the rain pouring down on him, the bottle still in his hand. Cars splashed through the intersection, their headlights cutting through the downpour, blinding and bright. He waited for the light to change, for the cars to pass, for the world to keep moving without him.

When the light turned green, he crossed the street.

The other side was quieter. The buildings were taller, older, their facades stained with age and grime. He walked toward the apartment complex where he lived, a small, unassuming brick building that felt like a shell, a protection against the world outside. He didn’t know why he was going there. He didn’t know why he was walking at all. His body was moving on autopilot, driven by a need to be somewhere safe, somewhere private, somewhere where he could take off his jacket and feel the weight of the bottle and the weight of the guilt and the weight of the rain.

He reached the entrance. The door was heavy, made of thick oak, and it took all his strength to push it open. The lobby was dimly lit, the air stale and warm. He could smell the dust, the old carpet, the faint scent of the cleaning products the manager used. It was a smell that was entirely human, entirely ordinary, and it grounded him.

He took the stairs.

One flight. Two flights. Three flights.

His legs burned. His breath came in short, sharp gasps. The beer bottle was sweating in his hand, the condensation mixing with the rain on his skin. He reached his apartment, fumbled for his keys, and finally, after a few frustrating moments, the lock clicked open.

He stepped inside.

The apartment was small, sparse, and quiet. The rain was muffled by the walls, a distant, soft hum. He closed the door behind him and leaned against it, the bottle still in his hand. He looked at the room, at the simple furniture, the unmade bed, the stack of books on the desk. It was a life. A small, ordinary, fragile life.

He didn’t throw the bottle away. He didn’t turn on the lights. He just stood there, in the dark, in the rain, in the silence, feeling the weight of the choice he had made.

He had left her.

And he had survived.

And that was enough.

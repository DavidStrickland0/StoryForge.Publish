The bar was loud, a low hum of conversation and clinking glass that Ethan had long since learned to tune out. He sat at the far end of the counter, his back to the wall, his beer half-finished. The air smelled of stale hops and cheap cleaning solution. Around him, the patrons shifted and spoke, a mundane tapestry of ordinary life that felt fragile, like paper held up against a gale.

Across the room, near the booth that usually reserved for the regulars, a commotion had begun. It was subtle at first, a shift in the ambient noise. The laughter around a specific table died down, replaced by a tense, breathless silence that rippled outward. Ethan didn’t look. He had a rule about that, a survival instinct forged in a world where the strong could break the weak without a second thought. He kept his eyes on the liquid in his glass, watching the condensation slide down the surface.

But the sound carried. A sharp, stifled gasp, followed by the wet, heavy thud of a body being shoved against the back of a chair. The wood creaked, a sound that was far too loud for the size of the room. Ethan’s stomach turned over, a cold knot tightening in his gut. He could feel the heat of the moment, the raw, unfiltered aggression that permeated the air. It wasn’t just noise; it was a physical weight, pressing against his eardrums, a reminder of the disparity that defined their society.

He thought of the woman’s face, glimpsed for a split second before he looked away. Her eyes were wide, filled with a terror that was so primal it made his own fear of it feel distant and abstract. She was just a person, sitting in her chair, and someone with the power to crush stone was treating her like an object. The injustice of it was a physical ache, a bruise on his soul that wouldn’t heal.

Ethan took a sip of his beer, the cold liquid doing nothing to soothe the heat in his chest. He looked at the man at the table. He was broad, his shoulders hunched with a kind of casual, predatory confidence. He didn’t even seem to notice the woman’s distress, or perhaps he did, and simply didn’t care. That was the terrifying part. It wasn’t malice, necessarily; it was indifference. A super could do what they wanted, to whom they wanted, and the world would turn a blind eye because the cost of accountability was too high.

Ethan pushed his chair back. The legs scraped against the floor, a harsh, grating sound that cut through the tense quiet. He didn’t mean to make a scene, but his body was moving before his mind had fully caught up. He needed to be out of here. He needed to be somewhere the air wasn’t so thick with the scent of helplessness. He reached for his wallet, his fingers trembling slightly, and pulled out a few bills. He placed them on the counter, enough to cover the tab and then some.

Gary, the bartender, looked up, his eyes narrowing as he noticed the empty glass and the cash. "Leaving early, Ethan?" he asked, his voice low.

"Just a headache," Ethan said, his voice sounding rougher to his own ears than he intended. He grabbed his jacket from the hook by the door, his movements sharp and deliberate. He didn't look back at the table. He didn't want to see the man’s face, didn't want to see the woman’s expression. He wanted to be gone, to be safe, to be somewhere where the weight of the world didn't press down on his shoulders so heavily.

He walked toward the door, his heart pounding in his chest, a frantic, irregular rhythm that echoed in his ears. He was leaving. He was doing the right thing, wasn't he? He wasn't a hero. He was just a guy who knew when to run. The door was only a few feet away, a sliver of night air and freedom waiting on the other side. He reached for the handle, his fingers closing around the cold metal, ready to pull himself out of the suffocating atmosphere and into the safety of the street.

The Rusty Anchor smelled of stale hops and aggressive cologne. Ethan adjusted his jacket, his fingers drumming a nervous rhythm against his thigh. He was sitting near the window, a position he had chosen for its exit access, but the glass was thick and soundproofed, a barrier that felt less like insulation and more like a tomb.

Across the room, the heat-shimmering man had stood up.

He moved with the easy, predatory grace of someone who had never once in his life been told to stop. His name was Kael, or something close to it; the tabloids called him "The Furnace" because he could cook a steak on his forearm or melt a city bus door with a casual wave. Right now, he was focusing that casualness on a woman in a blue cardigan who was trying to make herself small enough to disappear into the booth cushions.

The woman had been drinking water. She was talking to a friend who was now standing, phone out, recording with trembling hands, but her voice was gone. She was just watching Kael approach.

Ethan’s stomach turned over. He knew the statistics. He knew that when a super like Kael decided to be cruel, the legal system usually treated it as a minor civil dispute or a regrettable accident of collateral damage. The police would arrive, they would take a statement, and they would likely be told that Kael had been "agitated" and that the victim had provoked a response from a being capable of leveling a skyscraper. It was the social contract of the powerful: you could hurt us, but we couldn't hurt you back, and the law was too fragile to bridge the gap.

Kael reached the table. He didn't touch her yet. He just placed one hand on the back of her chair, his fingers long and pale against the dark wood. The air around him shimmered, the heat radiating off his skin in visible waves that made the condensation on the nearby beer glass evaporate instantly.

"Hi," Kael said. His voice was smooth, devoid of any malice. That was the worst part. It wasn't a roar. It was a greeting.

The woman looked up, her eyes wide and wet. She tried to speak, but Kael leaned in, his face inches from hers, and the sheer pressure of his presence, the sheer *weight* of his power, seemed to crush the air out of her lungs. She flinched, a full-body shudder that looked like a shock, and Kael smiled. It was a small, polite smile. He reached out and touched her wrist.

The skin beneath his fingers didn't burn. He was controlling it, precisely. He was showing off.

Ethan felt a cold knot tighten in his chest. He looked around the bar. People were watching. Some were filming. Others were looking at their phones, or the bar, or the floor. A man at the far end of the counter was laughing at a joke on his screen. No one moved. No one stepped between Kael and the woman. They were normal people, just like Ethan, and they understood, with a primal, instinctive clarity, that intervening meant death. They chose survival. They chose to look away.

Ethan could not look away. He hated that he couldn't. He hated that his brain was already calculating the distance to the exit, the angle of the table, the likelihood of Kael’s attention snapping to him if he stood up. If he moved, he became a target. If he stayed, he was complicit.

He stood up.

His chair scraped against the floor, a sharp, grating sound that cut through the low hum of the bar. Three heads turned. Kael did not. Kael was still leaning over the woman, his other hand moving to her shoulder, his touch deliberate and heavy. The woman’s head dropped, her hair falling forward to hide her face, her hands gripping the edge of the table until her knuckles were white.

Ethan took a step toward the door. Then another.

He was walking past the center of the room, the space between the patrons and the stage. The heat from Kael was a physical thing, a wall of humidity that pressed against his skin. He could feel the vibration of Kael’s power, a low-frequency thrum that he couldn’t hear but could feel in his teeth. It was a reminder of the gulf between them. He was made of meat and bone and fragile nerves; Kael was made of fire and certainty.

Ethan’s hand reached for the door handle. His fingers closed around the cold metal, and for a second, the simple, tactile reality of the bar door grounded him. He was leaving. He was choosing to leave. He was choosing his own life.

The guilt was a physical weight in his gut, heavy and sour. He pulled the door open.

The night air rushed in, cool and clean, a stark contrast to the stifling, heated atmosphere of the bar. He stepped out, the door swinging shut behind him with a solid, reassuring thud. He didn’t look back. He didn’t need to. He knew where he was, who he was, and exactly how much of a target he was in this city. He walked briskly toward the street, his mind already turning to the warm, safe chaos of his own life, leaving the world of the powerful to its own heavy, glittering gravity.

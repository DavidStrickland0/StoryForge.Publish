The first thing he noticed was the silence that had nothing to do with the music. The bass thrummed through the floorboards, a steady, mechanical heartbeat that usually helped drown out the clatter of glasses and the low murmur of conversation. But above that rhythm, a specific pocket of sound had vanished. People near the center table stopped talking. They didn’t just stop; they went rigid.

Ethan shifted his weight on the stool, the wood groaning beneath him. He glanced toward the source of the hush. The table was occupied by a woman he had seen earlier, sitting alone with a glass of water untouched beside her elbow. She was still there. Across from her sat the man with the heat shimmering off his skin, the one who had been laughing too loudly and leaning in too far toward other patrons. Now, the man was leaning over the table completely. His hands were on the table’s edge, knuckles white, and his face was tilted down toward the woman with a predatory intensity that made Ethan’s stomach drop.

The woman’s shoulders were hunched. Her eyes were wide, fixed on a spot somewhere above the man’s head, and her hands were pressed flat against the wood as if trying to anchor herself to the earth. The other patrons in the immediate vicinity had backed away. Some were staring. Others had turned their backs, pretending to look at their phones or the bar counter, their faces tight with a mixture of horror and a cold, paralyzing self-preservation. No one moved. No one reached for a phone. The air in that section of the room felt thick, pressurized by the sheer weight of the man’s presence and the terrified stillness of everyone else.

Ethan looked at the bartender, Gary, who was wiping down a glass with a rag that seemed to be absorbing the tension of the room. Gary avoided his gaze, his movements slow and deliberate. The social contract was clear, written in the unspoken rules of this city: you did not intervene. You did not draw attention. You did not get between a human and a force of nature that could snap a neck like a dry twig. The law had teeth, but they were dull, and they couldn't bite through the skin of a super.

Ethan’s own hand tightened around his empty glass. His pulse was a sharp, annoying throb in his throat. He looked at the woman again, seeing the way her jaw was clenched so hard the muscles stood out. He felt a sick, heavy dread settle in his chest, a coldness that had nothing to do with the air conditioning. He didn't know what was happening at that table, but the visual cues were enough to make the air feel scarce. He looked down at his own hands, ordinary and pale, and then back at the man’s shoulders, which seemed to expand and contract with a subtle, dangerous energy.

The thought of staying was suddenly impossible. The dread in his chest was no longer an abstract concept; it was a physical weight, pressing against his ribs. He needed to be somewhere else. Somewhere safe. Somewhere where the air didn't taste like copper and fear. He pushed the stool away from the table, the legs scraping against the floor in a sound that felt obscenely loud in the sudden quiet. He stood up, his chair falling backward with a clatter that made several heads turn.

Ethan didn't care. He didn't look at the woman. He didn't look at the man. He just started walking toward the exit, his eyes fixed on the door, his steps quick and steady, leaving the heavy, suffocating gravity of the bar behind him.

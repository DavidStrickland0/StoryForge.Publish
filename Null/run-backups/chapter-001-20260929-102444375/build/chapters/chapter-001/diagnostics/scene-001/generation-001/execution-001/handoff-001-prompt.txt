Ethan Vale adjusted the strap of his messenger bag, the leather digging into his shoulder with a familiar, dull ache. The rain had stopped an hour ago, leaving the streets of New Haven slick and shimmering under the sodium lights, but the air still held a damp chill that settled deep in his lungs. He pulled his coat tighter, his eyes fixed on the pavement ahead, counting the cracks in the concrete. One, two, three. He had counted to fourteen. He had done this on the walk from the office every evening for the last three years. It was a small, private ritual that kept his mind from wandering into the kind of thoughts that usually led to unnecessary expenses or awkward social interactions.

Tonight, however, the walk was interrupted by the low, thrumming bass of the establishment he was approaching: The Gilded Cage. It was a bar that tried very hard to be trendy and failed in a way that suggested the management had simply run out of ideas and budget simultaneously. The neon sign flickered in a rhythmic, dying pulse, casting a sickly pink glow over the wet sidewalk. Ethan had come here for one specific reason, and he had already made his decision before he even stepped through the heavy oak doors.

He had wanted to see if the rumors were true. In this city, where the sky was occasionally blocked out by figures in spandex or glowing with the light of someone’s personal energy output, the rumors were rarely true. They were marketing. Supers were celebrities, sure. They were strategic assets to the government, yes. But they were also brands. And brands required visibility. Ethan had a particular interest in the local hero known as "Aegis," a man whose public persona was built on unyielding strength and moral authority. Ethan wanted to see if Aegis was, in fact, a person, or just another corporate logo with a cape.

The interior of The Gilded Cage was dim, the air thick with the scent of expensive whiskey and cheaper perfume. The crowd was a mix of the affluent and the aspirational, all of them watching the main stage with the rapt attention of the faithful. Ethan moved through the space with the careful, non-invasive gait of a man who had learned long ago that taking up space was a luxury he could ill afford. He kept his head down, his shoulders hunched, making himself as small and unremarkable as his six-foot frame allowed.

He ordered a beer from the bar, the glass fogs in his cold hands. He didn’t drink much, but the ritual of holding the glass provided a sense of normalcy. He watched the patrons around him, observing the subtle shifts in their body language. There was a woman in a red dress laughing too loudly, her eyes darting toward the stage with a hunger that was almost physical. There was a man in a suit who kept checking his watch, his jaw tight with the kind of impatience that usually preceded a bad decision. They were all waiting for something. They were all waiting to be saved, or inspired, or simply entertained by the spectacle of power.

Ethan felt a pang of something uncomfortable in his chest. It wasn’t jealousy, exactly. It was more like the feeling of watching someone else enjoy a meal you had no appetite for. He understood the appeal. He understood the safety of it. In a world where a man with a god-complex could level a city block if he had a bad day, there was a strange, twisted comfort in knowing that the power was out there, visible, and contained within the boundaries of a stage and a spotlight. The law still existed, technically. Courts still met. But everyone knew that the law was a suggestion for the powerful, and a commandment for the rest.

He took a sip of the beer, the bitterness sharp on his tongue. He was just a guy. He paid his taxes. He followed the rules. He didn’t have the luxury of being angry, because anger didn’t change the physics of the world. It didn’t change the fact that if the power in that room decided to leave, the people in this bar would be the first to feel the absence. And if the power in that room decided to act, they would be the last to be protected.

Ethan finished his beer, the glass now empty and cold in his hand. The stage was quiet for a moment, the audience holding its breath. He watched the lights shift, the colors bleeding into one another, and he felt the weight of his own ordinariness settle onto his shoulders. It was a heavy thing, being ordinary. It meant being invisible. It meant being safe, but only in the way that prey is safe when the predator is asleep.

He set the glass down on the bar top, the ceramic clinking softly against the wood. He needed to leave. He needed to get out of this place where the air felt charged with potential violence and the silence felt like a held breath. He needed to go back to his apartment, to his small, quiet life where the only powers he had to manage were his own.

He turned away from the stage, his eyes finding the exit. The door was heavy, the handle cold under his fingers. He pushed it open, the cool night air rushing in to replace the stifling warmth of the bar. He stepped out into the night, the door swinging shut behind him with a soft thud. The street was empty, the rain-slicked pavement reflecting the neon lights in long, broken streaks. He walked on, his footsteps the only sound in the quiet street, his mind already turning over the details of the evening, the faces, the words, the feeling of the air. He didn’t know why he had come. He didn’t know why he had stayed. He only knew that he was leaving, and that the world behind him was already beginning to forget that he had been there at all.

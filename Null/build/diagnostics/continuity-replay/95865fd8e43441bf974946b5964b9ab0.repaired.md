The ambulance did not wait for the crowd to finish its collective gasp. The rear doors slammed shut with a finality that cut through the lingering murmur, and the vehicle pulled away with a screech of tires that sounded obscenely loud in the quiet street. Ethan watched it go. His legs moved before his mind gave the order, a dull, mechanical shuffling that carried him away from the venue’s perimeter and down the block.

He did not look back again. To do so would be to invite the image of the cracked pavement back into his vision, and he was done with geometry for the day.

The street was clogged. Cars idled in the lanes, engines ticking in the cool morning air, drivers leaning against hoods or staring at their phones. The normal rhythm of the city—horns, hurried footsteps, the hiss of crosswalk signals—had been replaced by a stunned, buzzing silence that felt heavier than the noise.

Ethan kept his hands in his pockets, his fingers curled around the rough wool of his jacket lining. He focused on the texture. The roughness. It was a small, tactile anchor in a world that felt like it had been dissolved.

A cluster of news vans had already set up a perimeter three doors down. Red lights pulsed against the grey morning, casting long, rhythmic shadows across the sidewalk. Reporters stood in tight groups, microphones extended like the proboscises of strange, metallic flowers. One woman, wearing a blazer that cost more than Ethan’s weekly grocery budget, was speaking directly into a lens.

"...confirmed that Aurora’s bio-electric signature dropped to zero levels at 9:14 AM. Initial reports from the scene suggest a total system failure. There are no signs of external interference, but we are awaiting an official statement from the Foundation—"

Ethan passed them without slowing. The words washed over him, meaningless noise. *System failure.* *External interference.* They were trying to pin a supernatural event into a box of technical jargon, to make it fit within the parameters of their understanding. He felt a strange, hollow distance from the words, as if they were being spoken in a language he had once understood but had since lost.

He turned onto his own street. The noise of the venue faded behind him, replaced by the mundane sounds of his neighborhood. A dog barked from behind a chain-link fence. The distant rumble of a delivery truck. The faint, rhythmic ticking of cooling metal on an awning, the sky remaining clear.

The transition was abrupt. One block, he was a witness to a miracle undone. The next, he was a man walking home in the morning.

His building stood at the end of the block, the brick facade familiar and solid. The windows were dark, reflecting the pale sky. He looked at the entrance. The automatic door hummed, sensing his approach. It slid open, revealing the sterile white lobby, the doorman’s desk, the smell of floor wax.

Ethan stopped in the doorway. He did not step inside. He stood there, letting the cool air from outside mix with the warm, recycled air of the building. He breathed in. He breathed out.

The door waited. He did not.

The brick wall was cold against Ethan’s back, seeping a damp chill through the fabric of his jacket. He didn’t move for a long time. His breathing was shallow, a rattle in his chest that felt too loud in the quiet street. The air smelled of wet asphalt and the faint, metallic tang of the venue’s ventilation system, a scent that now carried the weight of every scream he hadn’t heard but had certainly felt in his teeth.

He straightened up. His legs felt like they belonged to someone else, heavy and unsteady. He needed to leave. He needed to be somewhere small, somewhere with walls that didn’t open onto the sky.

He turned away from the venue. The street was empty, save for a few stragglers who walked with their heads down, their phones clutched to their chests like talismans. Ethan merged into the flow of traffic, his eyes fixed on the middle distance, refusing to look at the faces passing by. He was invisible. He was nothing. The thought offered a grim comfort.

The walk was a blur of sensory input that his brain refused to process. The green light. The red light. The squeal of brakes. The chatter of people discussing the news, their voices rising in excited, horrified speculation. *Did you see it? I think I saw it. She just stopped.*

Ethan walked faster. The pavement seemed to shift under his feet, the concrete cracking and receding like the scene he had just witnessed. He kept his hands in his pockets, his fingers curled into tight balls. He was afraid to let them unclench. He was afraid that if he let go, he would shake.

The city felt wrong. The buildings, usually solid and permanent, seemed to lean in, threatening to collapse. The sky above was a pale, washed-out blue, indifferent and vast. It was the same sky Aurora had been falling from. The thought sent a fresh wave of nausea through his stomach. He swallowed it down, his throat dry and tight.

He reached the corner of his street. The apartment building stood there, a slab of grey brick and broken windows, unremarkable and safe. The door was ajar, a draft of stale air and dust drifting out. Ethan paused, listening. Nothing but the hum of the city and the distant wail of a siren that had finally reached the venue.

He pushed the door open. The lobby was empty. The doorman, Ray, was behind the desk, his eyes fixed on a tablet. The blue light from the screen illuminated his face, casting sharp shadows under his eyes. He looked up as Ethan entered, his expression neutral, professional.

"Morning, Mr. Vale."

Ethan nodded. He couldn’t speak. His voice felt trapped in his throat, a stone blocking the way. Ray didn’t press him. The doorman had learned, over the years, that Ethan was a man of few words and many silences. He simply gestured toward the elevator.

Ethan took the stairs. He didn’t trust the machinery. He didn’t trust anything that moved without his direct control. The climb was slow, his breath coming in short, sharp gasps. Each step was a deliberate act of will, a negotiation with his own trembling body.

When he reached his floor, the hallway was quiet. The carpet was thin, worn smooth by the passage of feet. He walked to his door, his hand shaking as he fumbled for his keys. They clattered into his palm, cold and heavy. He inserted the key into the lock, turning it with a steady, deliberate motion.

The door opened. The apartment was dark. The light from the hallway spilled in, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air. Ethan stepped inside, closing the door behind him. The lock clicked shut.

He stood in the entryway, his back against the door. The silence was absolute. It was the first time in hours that the world had stopped screaming. Ethan closed his eyes. He was safe. He was alone. He was still here.

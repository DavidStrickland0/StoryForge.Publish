The corner was cold. The brick held the chill of the morning, seeping through the thin fabric of his jacket and settling into his shoulder blades. Ethan leaned his forehead against it, eyes closed, trying to align his breathing with a rhythm that wasn’t there. His lungs felt too large for his chest, drawing in air that tasted of exhaust and damp concrete.

He opened his eyes. The street was empty of the crowd, but it wasn’t empty of attention. Three blocks down, a police cruiser sat idling at the intersection, its blue and red lights pulsing in a slow, hypnotic cycle. No sirens. No shouting. Just the quiet, industrial hum of power asserting itself over the chaos.

Ethan pushed off the wall. His legs felt heavy, as if the gravity of the city had increased tenfold since he’d left the venue. He began to walk.

He did not look at the phones that people held up as he passed. He did not look at the faces that tracked his movement. He kept his gaze fixed on the middle distance, a few feet ahead of his nose, where the pavement was just pavement and the sidewalk was just concrete. If he looked directly at anything, he was afraid he might see the cracks. He was afraid he might see the red.

He walked until the scent of ozone faded, replaced by the familiar, stale smell of his own neighborhood. It was a smell of old carpet and recycled air, a scent that had once been a source of comfort and now felt like a trap.

His building was a grey slab of brick and glass, unremarkable and solid. The lobby was quiet. The doorman, Ray, looked up from his tablet.

"Morning, Mr. Vale."

Ethan stopped. The sound of his own voice in the small space was too loud. He waited for Ray to look at him, to ask if he was alright, to offer a handkerchief or a glass of water. Ray’s eyes flicked up, scanning Ethan’s face, then dropped to his hands.

Ethan’s hands were shaking. He shoved them into his pockets, clenching his fists until his knuckles turned white. He forced a smile. It felt like a mask slipping off his face, leaving the raw, terrified flesh beneath.

"Morning, Ray," he said. His voice was steady. It was the most important thing he had ever achieved.

Ray nodded, a professional gesture that contained more weight than words could carry. He didn’t ask. He didn’t see. He just watched Ethan walk to the elevator, the doors sliding shut with a soft hydraulic sigh that sounded like a finality.

The descent was quick, a drop of three floors that felt longer than it should have. When the doors opened onto the lobby, the morning light was already pouring through the glass facade, washing the sterile white marble floors in a pale glow.

Ethan stepped out onto the sidewalk. The air was cool, carrying the scent of ozone and wet pavement from the night before’s rain. He looked at his hands one last time. They were clean. They were pale. They were his.

He walked until the sound of the crowd faded, until the vibration in his teeth stopped, until the world felt solid again. He stopped on a corner, leaning against a brick wall, his breath coming in short, shallow gasps.

He looked at his hands. They were clean. They were pale. They were his.

And for the first time in his life, he was unsure if that was a comfort or a curse.

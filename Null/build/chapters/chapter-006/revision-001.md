The walk back to his apartment felt less like a journey and more like a drift through a thick, gray fog. Three blocks. That was all. Three blocks of concrete, neon, and the low hum of the city’s nightlife, yet it seemed to stretch into an endless corridor. Ethan’s legs moved on automatic, a mechanical shuffle that kept him upright, but his mind was a white noise of static. He didn’t look at the streetlights. He didn’t look at the people passing him, their faces illuminated by the screens in their hands. He just walked.

The door to his building was heavy, the kind of solid oak that usually felt like a barrier between the chaos outside and the quiet inside. Tonight, it felt like a tombstone. Ray was at the desk, as always. The doorman’s badge, a Security Level 4, caught the light as he glanced up.

"Evening, Ethan," Ray said, his voice flat and professional.

"Hi, Ray," Ethan managed. His voice sounded foreign to his own ears, thin and reedy.

Ray’s eyes flicked to Ethan’s face, then to the street behind him. There was a question there, a flicker of concern that was quickly buried under layers of practiced neutrality. Ray knew Ethan. He knew Ethan’s routines, his quiet demeanor, the way he kept to himself. He also knew what had happened three blocks away. The news was already moving through the airwaves, through the phones, through the very fabric of the city.

"You look like you’ve seen a ghost," Ray said softly.

Ethan shook his head, a small, jerky motion. "Just tired, Ray. Long night."

"Right," Ray said. He didn’t press. He knew better. Some things weren’t meant to be asked about, and some things were too big to be spoken of in a lobby. "Take it easy, Ethan."

Ethan nodded, couldn't quite manage a smile. He swiped his keycard, the green light blinking in the dark, and stepped into the elevator. The doors closed, sealing him in a small metal box that carried him up to the fourth floor. The silence in the elevator was heavy, oppressive. He could hear the faint hum of the machinery, the whir of the cables. He stared at the numbers changing on the display, 1, 2, 3, 4, until the doors slid open with a soft chime.

His apartment was dark when he stepped in. The curtains were drawn, the only light coming from the streetlamps outside, filtered through the gaps in the fabric. It was a dim, blueish glow that painted the room in shades of shadow. He dropped his bag by the door, the sound of it hitting the floor loud in the quiet. He didn’t turn on the lights. He didn’t want to see. He wanted to disappear into the dark, to let the shadows swallow him whole.

He moved to the kitchen, his movements slow and deliberate. He opened the cabinet, his fingers brushing against the cold glass of the blue mug. Mara’s mug. It was a simple thing, a deep, calming blue, with a small chip on the handle. He had bought it at a street market years ago, a time when their life together felt solid, grounded. Now, it was just a piece of ceramic, a reminder of a past that felt increasingly distant.

He took the mug out, his fingers wrapping around the cold surface. He didn’t fill it with water. He just held it, feeling the weight of it, the solidity. It was a real thing. A tangible object. Unlike the world outside, which had just shattered.

He walked to the window, standing in the center of the room. The view was a blur of city lights, the distant glow of the venue three blocks away. He could see the red and blue lights of the ambulance, still pulsing in the distance, a faint, rhythmic throb that seemed to match the beating of his own heart.

He stood there for a long time, the mug in his hand, his eyes fixed on the lights. He tried to think, to process, to make sense of it. But his mind was a blank slate, wiped clean. All he could feel was the cold of the mug, the weight of it, and the terrifying, hollow emptiness in his chest.

He didn’t sleep. He sat in the dark, the mug in his lap, and waited for the morning.

***

The morning light was harsh, unforgiving. It poured through the gaps in the curtains, slicing the room into strips of brightness and shadow. Ethan woke up on the floor, his back aching, the mug still clutched in his hand. He had fallen asleep sitting up, his head resting against the cabinet.

He blinked, his eyes adjusting to the light. The apartment was the same as it had been the night before. The kitchen counter was cluttered with the usual debris of his life: a stack of mail, a half-empty bottle of water, a few dishes in the sink. It was ordinary. It was safe.

But the world outside was not.

He walked to the living room, his legs stiff from sleeping on the hard floor. He picked up his phone from the coffee table. It was dead, the screen dark. He plugged it in, waiting for the battery to charge. When the screen lit up, it was a flood of notifications. Hundreds of them. Texts, calls, messages from news apps, from social media, from friends he hadn’t spoken to in weeks.

He opened the news app. The headline was bold, red, impossible to ignore.

**AURORA FALLS: SUPERHEROINE INJURED IN MYSTERIOUS ACCIDENT.**

The story was everywhere. It was the only story. It dominated the feed, the screens, the conversations. Ethan scrolled through, his heart pounding in his chest.

*“Aurora, the city’s most beloved superheroine, suffered serious injuries early this morning during a public appearance at the Grand Hall. Witnesses report that she was flying over the crowd when she suddenly lost altitude, crashing to the concrete with a force that shattered the pavement. Paramedics rushed to the scene, treating her for what are believed to be multiple broken bones and internal bleeding.”*

Ethan’s hands trembled. He read on.

*“The cause of the accident remains unknown. Authorities are investigating, but there is no evidence of foul play. Aurora’s representatives have declined to comment, citing her private medical matters.”*

*“Experts are baffled. Aurora is known for her invulnerability, a skill that has protected her for years. How could she have been injured? Is this a malfunction in her skills? A new threat? Or something else entirely?”*

The speculation was wild, unchecked. Theories ranged from a secret weapon to a hidden illness. Some claimed it was a trap, a conspiracy. Others suggested a breakdown in the natural order. The public was in shock, in fear. They had relied on Aurora for so long, so completely, that the idea of her being vulnerable, of her being human, was a concept they couldn’t wrap their heads around.

Ethan read the comments. They were a mix of fear, anger, and confusion.

*“Who did this? Who would hurt Aurora?”*
*“Is this a new super? A villain we don’t know about?”*
*“This is a nightmare. What are we going to do without her?”*
*“I was there. I saw it. She just… stopped. Like she hit a wall.”*

That comment. *“Like she hit a wall.”*

Ethan closed the app, his breath coming in short, sharp gasps. He felt sick, his stomach churning. He walked to the bathroom, splashing water on his face. The water was cold, shocking. It helped, a little. It grounded him.

He looked in the mirror. His face was pale, his eyes dark with sleeplessness. He looked like a ghost. Like something that didn’t belong in the world anymore.

He thought about the bar. He thought about FireMan. He thought about the way the super’s strength had vanished, the way the woman had escaped. He thought about the way Aurora had fallen, the way her invulnerability had failed.

Two incidents. Two different places. Two different people.

And he had been there. Both times.

The coincidence was impossible. It had to be a coincidence. It had to be.

But the fear was a cold, heavy stone in his gut, pressing down on him, making it hard to breathe. He was a civilian. He was ordinary. He was nothing.

And yet.

He looked at his hands. They were ordinary hands. Pale, slightly dirty, trembling.

He picked up his phone again. He looked at the message to Ben, the one he had sent days ago, the one that had gone unanswered. *Hey, Ben. You okay? Just checking in.*

No response. Just silence.

Ethan set the phone down, his hand shaking. He needed to leave. He needed to go out, to see the world, to see if it was still real. But he knew he couldn’t. He knew that if he went out, if he saw the news, if he saw the people, he would break.

He sat on the edge of his bed, the convertible sofa, the one that had been his refuge for so long. He pulled his knees up to his chest, wrapping his arms around them. He was small. He was fragile. He was a threat level zero.

And he was the most dangerous thing in the city.

The thought was a loop, a relentless, grinding wheel in his mind. He couldn’t stop it. He couldn’t think of anything else. He just sat there, in the dark, in the quiet, waiting for the world to end.

***

He stared at the ceiling, the plaster cracked and yellowed with age. The apartment was silent, save for the distant wail of a siren that faded into the background noise of the city. His phone lay on the sofa cushion, the screen dark again. He hadn’t charged it fully. He hadn’t looked at it.

The image of the ambulance remained etched in his mind. The way the red and blue lights had pulsed against the concrete. The way the paramedics had moved with a frantic, clumsy efficiency that belied the severity of the situation. They had treated her like a civilian. Like a broken object that needed to be patched up and moved along.

Ethan closed his eyes. He tried to recall the moment before the fall. The crowd had been a sea of upturned faces, a collective gasp of awe. He had been standing in the middle of it, a single grain of sand in a dune. He had looked up. He had seen her.

And then she had stopped.

It wasn’t a gradual loss of power. It wasn’t a waver or a stumble. It was a sudden, absolute cessation. One second, she was soaring, a figure of light and strength. The next, she was falling, a body of flesh and bone plummeting toward the ground.

He had watched her hit the pavement. The sound had been a dull, heavy thud that seemed to vibrate through the soles of his shoes. The crowd had screamed. A wave of bodies had surged forward, a chaotic tide of limbs and voices. He had been pushed back, his back hitting the cold concrete of the stage barrier.

He had seen the blood. It had been dark, thick, spreading across the white fabric of her suit. He had seen the paramedics rush in, their faces masked in professional concern. He had seen the ambulance arrive, its doors swinging open, the stretcher extending like a mechanical tongue.

He had watched them lift her. He had watched the way her body hung limp, the way the blood dripped from her torn suit onto the stretcher. He had watched the way the paramedics’ hands hovered over her, not quite touching, afraid to cause more pain.

They placed her on the stretcher.

And then, as they began to push it toward the ambulance, Aurora’s eyes opened.

They were closed for a second, then they flew open, wide and terrified. She tried to speak, but only a small, broken gasp came out. She looked around, her gaze sweeping the crowd, the stage, the sky. She saw the paramedics, the ambulance, the blood on her suit.

Her eyes found something. Or someone.

Ethan didn’t know who she was looking at. He didn’t know if she was looking at him.

The paramedics finished loading her into the ambulance. They checked her vitals one last time, then closed the doors.

The ambulance began to move, its wheels rolling over the concrete, carrying her away from the venue, away from the crowd, away from Ethan.

He watched it go.

He watched the red and blue lights fade into the distance, until they were just a blur, then a memory, then nothing.

The crowd began to disperse. The panic was fading, replaced by a dazed, stunned silence. People were checking their phones, reading the news, watching the live feeds. The story was already breaking, already becoming history.

Ethan remained on his knees.

The concrete was cold against his legs. The air was still. The world was the same, and completely different.

He stood up. His legs felt weak, unsteady. He walked toward the exit, moving through the thinning crowd without looking at anyone. He didn't run. He didn't hide. He just walked, his hands still in his pockets, his heart still hammering.

He had to get home. He had to sit in his apartment, in the dark, and wait for the world to make sense again.

But as he walked, he felt the weight of his secret pressing down on him, heavy and suffocating. He had been standing there. He had been the cause.

And he was alone.

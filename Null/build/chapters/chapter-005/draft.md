The silence that had held the crowd in a tight, vibrating coil for the last thirty seconds shattered.

It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was a physical sensation, a sudden drop in air pressure that made Ethan’s ears pop. The low-frequency hum that had been vibrating in his teeth, the collective breath of ten thousand people suspended in awe, just... stopped.

Ethan’s eyes snapped open.

Above the stage, Aurora was falling.

She had been descending in a slow, majestic spiral, her white and gold suit catching the stadium lights, her arms outstretched as if she were merely gliding on a thermal. But the glide had stuttered. The air, which should have been solid beneath her, had failed.

She dropped.

It was a freefall. There was no grace in it, no controlled descent. She plummeted from the center of the stage, a streak of white against the dark backdrop, and the crowd’s reaction was a single, choked gasp that swallowed the roar.

Ethan didn’t think. His body reacted before his mind could process the geometry of the event. He saw her trajectory, saw the pavement where she was going to land, and his legs moved. He stepped forward, just one step, his hand coming out of his pocket to brace against the air, to push him toward her.

He was three meters away. Maybe four.

The impact was a wet, heavy thud that seemed to resonate through the soles of his shoes.

For a heartbeat, there was no sound at all. The crowd held its breath, staring at the spot where the sky had broken. Then, a scream. It started from somewhere to the left, a high-pitched wail of pure shock, and it rippled outward, turning into a roar of panic that crashed against Ethan’s chest like a physical wave.

He stumbled forward, his heart hammering against his ribs. He had to help. He had to check if she was breathing.

But the crowd moved faster than he did. A wall of bodies surged toward the epicenter of the impact. Ethan was shoved backward, his shoulder hitting someone’s chest, and he was trapped in the crush of humanity. He pushed, elbows out, shoving past a woman with a phone raised high, past a man in a security vest who was shouting into a radio, but the flow of people was a river, and he was a stone in the current.

He couldn’t get to her. He could only watch.

Through the gap in the crowd, he saw her.

Aurora was lying on her back on the grey concrete. Her suit was torn, the white fabric ripped open at the shoulder and the hip. And beneath the tear, there was red.

Blood.

It was spreading across the pavement, dark and viscous, soaking into the porous stone. Her arm was bent at an angle that made Ethan’s stomach lurch. The forearm was twisted, the skin bulging out in a grotesque, purple bruise that was already forming. Her other hand was splayed out, fingers twitching once, then going still. Her face was turned upward, eyes closed, mouth slightly open.

She wasn’t moving.

The silence returned, heavier than before, broken only by the frantic, overlapping shouts of the security team and the distant, high-pitched sirens of approaching emergency vehicles.

Ethan stood frozen. His hand was still extended, fingers curled in the air, as if he were trying to catch the moment, to hold it in place. His mind was a blank, white void.

*This is a dream,* he thought. *You’re in the apartment. You’re making coffee. The drawer is stuck. You’re listening to the rain.*

But the smell of ozone and sweat was real. The vibration of the crowd’s fear was real. The blood on the pavement was real.

Aurora was dead. Or dying. And he had been standing there, four meters away, watching it happen.

The sirens grew louder. Blue and red lights began to flash in the distance, cutting through the dimming daylight of the venue. The crowd parted, a wide corridor clearing as the emergency team moved in. Paramedics, dressed in high-visibility yellow, rushed forward, their movements efficient and practiced.

Ethan watched, his eyes fixed on the scene, his hands trembling inside his jacket pockets. He felt a cold numbness spreading from his fingertips to his elbows.

One of the paramedics knelt beside Aurora’s head. He checked her pulse, then looked up at the other medic. He shook his head.

"No response to pain," the first medic said, his voice clipped and professional. "Check the extremities."

The second medic moved to Aurora’s broken arm. He reached out to touch it, to assess the damage.

And then he stopped.

He pulled his hand back as if he had touched a flame. He stared at his glove, then at Aurora’s arm. He looked at the blood pooling around her, at the torn fabric, at the twisted bone. He looked at his own hand, as if expecting to see a burn, a scorch mark, something that would explain what he was seeing.

"Did you get a reading?" he asked, his voice tight.

The first medic shook his head. "No. I didn't check. I just... I expected her to be harder. I expected the skin to be... different."

He looked at the other medic, and for the first time, Ethan saw fear in his eyes. Not fear of the injury, but fear of the impossibility of it.

"Her invulnerability is gone," the first medic said, the words hanging in the air like smoke. "Look at the wound. The depth. The tissue damage. This is... this is a human injury."

The second medic knelt beside the broken arm. He gently, so gently, touched the skin around the twisted bone. Aurora didn’t flinch. She didn’t scream. She was unconscious, her face pale and still.

"Check the vitals," the first medic ordered, his voice shaking slightly. "Get the trauma kit. We need to stabilize the fracture before we move her. If we move her wrong, we could sever the nerve."

Ethan watched as they worked. They moved with a frantic, delicate precision, treating the legendary superheroine as if she were made of glass. They splinted the broken arm, wrapped the bleeding shoulder, and monitored her breathing. They treated her like a person. Like a mortal.

And Ethan realized, with a clarity that felt like a shard of ice driving itself into his chest, that this was the only variable that had changed.

He had been standing there.

He had been four meters away.

He had been the only thing between her and the ground.

His mind raced, trying to find a logical explanation. A malfunction in her suit? A sudden health issue? A trap? But the specifics of the paramedics' confusion, the way they were treating her broken bones with the same care they would give to a civilian, told a different story. Her powers were gone. Not suppressed, not hidden. Gone.

And he had been there.

The fear that had been a cold numbness in his limbs now turned into a hot, burning panic. He looked down at his hands. They were ordinary hands. Pale, slightly dirty, trembling. He looked at the crowd around him, at the thousands of faces staring at the scene, at the phones recording, at the cameras broadcasting.

If they found out he was there. If they connected his presence to the loss of her powers.

He was a civilian. He had no skills. He had no powers. He was threat level zero.

But what if he wasn’t?

What if he was the reason she fell?

The thought was so terrifying, so fundamentally at odds with his understanding of the world, that his legs gave out. He sank to his knees, the impact of his knees on the concrete jarring through his bones. He didn’t care. He just needed to be down, away from the eyes, away from the center.

He pressed his hands over his face, his breath coming in short, sharp gasps. The smell of antiseptic and blood filled his nose. He could hear the paramedics talking, their voices low and urgent.

"We need to move her. Now. Get the ambulance here."

Ethan lowered his hands.

The ambulance was being wheeled into the clearing. It was a large, white vehicle, marked with the emergency logo. The back doors were open, the stretcher already extended.

Two paramedics lifted Aurora’s head and shoulders. Another took her feet. They moved her with a synchronized, careful lift, their muscles straining, their faces tight with concentration.

Ethan watched them lift her. He watched the way her body hung limp, the way the blood dripped from her torn suit onto the stretcher. He watched the way the paramedics’ hands hovered over her, not quite touching, afraid to cause more pain.

They placed her on the stretcher.

And then, as they began to push it toward the ambulance, Aurora’s eyes opened.

They were closed for a second, then they flew open, wide and terrified. She tried to speak, but only a small, broken gasp came out. She looked around, her gaze sweeping the crowd, the stage, the sky. She saw the paramedics, the ambulance, the blood on her suit.

Her eyes found something. Or someone.

Ethan didn’t know who she was looking at. He didn’t know if she was looking at him.

The paramedics finished loading her into the ambulance. They checked her vitals one last time, then closed the doors.

The ambulance began to move, its wheels rolling over the concrete, carrying her away from the venue, away from the crowd, away from Ethan.

He watched it go.

He watched the red and blue lights fade into the distance, until they were just a blur, then a memory, then nothing.

The crowd began to disperse. The panic was fading, replaced by a dazed, stunned silence. People were checking their phones, reading the news, watching the live feeds. The story was already breaking, already becoming history.

Ethan remained on his knees.

The concrete was cold against his legs. The air was still. The world was the same, and completely different.

He stood up. His legs felt weak, unsteady. He walked toward the exit, moving through the thinning crowd without looking at anyone. He didn't run. He didn't hide. He just walked, his hands still in his pockets, his heart still hammering.

He had to get home. He had to sit in his apartment, in the dark, and wait for the world to make sense again.

But as he walked, he felt the weight of his secret pressing down on him, heavy and suffocating. He had been standing there. He had been the cause.

And he was alone.

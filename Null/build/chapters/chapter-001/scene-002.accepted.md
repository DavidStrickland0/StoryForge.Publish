The silence in the bar stretched, thick and suffocating under the low hum of the bass. Ethan’s knuckles whitened around his glass. The super shifted his weight, the leather of his jacket creaking as he began to move. He didn’t walk so much as glide across the carpet, his eyes locked on Elena with the detached, predatory focus of a man choosing which piece of furniture to break next. David stood up immediately, his chair scraping back against the floor with a harsh shriek. He moved to the super’s side, his face a mask of eager, servile agreement, ready to do whatever was required.

Elena didn’t rise. She didn’t reach for her purse or check the exit. She just sat there, her wine glass trembling slightly in her hand, her jaw set in a rigid line that spoke of pure, adrenaline-fueled defiance. The super reached out and pinched her chin, his thumb pressing hard into her jawline, tilting her head back so he could look down at her with open, mocking amusement. The other patrons in the bar had stopped talking, the music seeming to fade into a distant, muffled drone. Everyone was watching. Everyone was terrified. No one was moving.

Ethan felt a hot, acidic burn in his gut. He looked at the super’s hand, then at his own. He was a twenty-six-year-old office worker with a bad knee and a history of minor sports injuries. The man in front of him had once been shown on the news casually lifting a city bus off a family. If Ethan tried to intervene, he would end up in a hospital, or worse, and the super would not even register the effort. The social contract of the world clearly did not apply to him.

Ethan looked at the exit. It was across the room, blocked by the super’s broad frame and the magnetic pull of his attention. But if he stayed, he was complicit in the helplessness of the room. He was just another bystander watching a predator play with its food. He couldn’t save her. He knew that with a clarity that cut through the fear. The only thing he could do was leave. He needed to be out of there, out of the blast radius of whatever violence the super decided to unleash.

Ethan pushed his chair back. The legs dragged against the wooden floor, a loud, deliberate scrape that cut through the ambient noise of the bar. He didn’t look at the super, but he felt the shift in the room’s energy, the sudden, sharp intake of breath from the patrons as the super’s gaze flicked toward the sound. Ethan stood up, his heart hammering against his ribs. He didn’t run. Running would make him a target, a fly buzzing around a bat. He kept his movements slow, deliberate, and casual. He grabbed his jacket from the back of his chair, his fingers shaking, and began the long walk toward the exit. He had to pass right by the super. He had to pass right by the table. He had no choice but to walk through the danger zone.

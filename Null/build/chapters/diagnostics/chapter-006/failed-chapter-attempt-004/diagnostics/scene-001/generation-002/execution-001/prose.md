The street was quiet, but it was the wrong kind of quiet. It wasn’t the peaceful silence of an empty room; it was the held breath of a crowd that had been scattered by panic. Ethan moved through the gap left by the fleeing pedestrians, his shoes scraping against the asphalt. He kept his eyes fixed on the pavement, counting the cracks in the sidewalk as he walked. One, two, three. The numbers helped. They were solid things. They didn’t bleed. They didn’t fall.

He turned the corner onto his street, the familiar brick facades rising around him like a wall. The air here tasted of exhaust and damp earth, a grounding scent that felt safer than the sterile, ozone-heavy atmosphere of the venue. He kept walking, his pace steady, refusing to let his legs turn to jelly. His hands were still in his pockets, fingers curled tight around the rough fabric of his jeans. He could feel the heat of his own skin, the pulse in his wrists. He was solid. He was here.

The apartment building loomed ahead, a five-story structure of red brick and glass. He passed the kiosk where he usually bought his morning coffee, the owner behind the counter looking at his phone with the detached focus of someone checking a score or a price. The man didn’t look up. He didn’t see Ethan. He didn’t see the tremor in Ethan’s shoulders or the way his jaw was clenched so hard his teeth ached.

Ethan reached the building’s entrance. The automatic doors slid open with a soft hiss, revealing the lobby with its polished floor and the security desk. Ray was there, leaning back in his chair, a tablet propped up against his arm. He looked up as Ethan approached, his expression shifting from boredom to professional alertness.

"Mr. Vale," Ray said, his voice neutral. "Rough morning?"

Ethan opened his mouth to answer, but no sound came out. He stared at Ray, at the calm set of the man’s shoulders, at the steady rhythm of his breathing. Ray was normal. Ray was safe. Ray was part of the world that still made sense.

"I’m fine," Ethan finally said. His voice sounded thin, scraped raw.

Ray’s eyes flicked to Ethan’s face, lingering on the pale skin and the dark circles under his eyes. He didn’t ask questions. He didn’t need to. In a city of supers, a civilian who looked like he’d seen a ghost was a common enough sight. Ray simply nodded, his gaze dropping back to his tablet.

"Door’s on the left, Mr. Vale."

Ethan nodded, his throat tight. He walked past the security desk, the hum of the lobby’s air conditioning filling the silence. He took the elevator up to the third floor. The doors closed, sealing him in a small metal box. He stood there, watching the numbers climb. The elevator displayed the number three. The elevator displayed the number two. The elevator displayed the number one.

When the doors opened, the hallway was empty. The carpet was beige and worn, smelling of industrial cleaner. Ethan walked to his apartment, key in hand. He didn’t look back. He didn’t look at the other doors. He just walked, his footsteps muffled by the thick pile, until he reached his own door.

He unlocked it and stepped inside, closing the door behind him. The lock clicked, a small, definitive sound. The apartment was dark, the curtains drawn against the morning sun. The air was still, carrying the faint scent of lavender detergent and the coffee grounds he’d left in the machine the previous day.

Ethan stood in the center of the room, his hands still in his pockets. He could hear the hum of the refrigerator, the tick of the clock on the counter, and the distant, muffled rumble of a truck passing on the street outside. It was a list of sounds that used to comfort him, a soundtrack to a life that made sense.

Now, every noise seemed to carry the echo of the night before.

He looked at the Lens dock on the table. The device sat there, inert and black, a tiny window to a world he was supposed to navigate. He had left it disconnected. For the first time in months, he had walked home by feel, by the rough texture of the pavement under his shoes and the smell of the rain that had started to fall near the park. It had been a small victory, a reclamation of the mundane.

But now, the mundane felt like a trap.

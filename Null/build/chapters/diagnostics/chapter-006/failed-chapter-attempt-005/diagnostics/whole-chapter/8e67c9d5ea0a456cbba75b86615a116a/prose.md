The brick wall was cool against Ethan’s back, a solid, unyielding reality that grounded him more effectively than the pavement beneath his feet. He stood there for a long time, his breath still coming in shallow, ragged gasps, his hands hanging loose at his sides. The street was quiet now. The initial shock had passed, replaced by the low, droning hum of people realizing they were merely witnesses to a tragedy, not participants in a miracle.

He checked his watch. He had been standing there for twenty minutes. He should have gone home. He should have gone to work. He should have done anything that involved moving his legs, because standing still felt like a form of confession.

Ethan pushed off the wall and began to walk.

He did not take the direct route. He cut through the park, where the grass was still damp from the morning rain, and took the long way around the commercial district, passing the newsstands that usually displayed glossy covers of celebrities and political scandals. Today, the covers were different.

They were all the same.

Aurora’s face, frozen in a moment of serene confidence, was plastered across every display. But the headlines were not about her achievements. They were questions.

*WHAT WENT WRONG?*
*THE FALL OF THE UNBREAKABLE.*
*MYSTERY AROUND AURORA’S SUDDEN DESCENT.*

Ethan walked past a group of teenagers who were arguing about it, their voices loud and unguarded.

“Did you see the feed? It wasn’t a malfunction. Her lights just went out. Total darkness. Like she was unplugged.”

“Maybe she got tired. Maybe she pushed too hard.”

“No, you’re an idiot. Supers don’t get tired. Look at the medical reports. They said her bio-field was flat. Zero. She was just a person for three seconds. Three seconds where she could die.”

Ethan kept his head down, his hands buried in his pockets. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic, bird-like flutter that he tried to ignore. He wasn’t a person who wanted to be near the center of a mystery. He was a person who wanted to be forgotten.

He reached his apartment building, the familiar smell of stale carpet cleaner and recycled air hitting him as he stepped into the lobby. Ray, the doorman, was behind his desk, but he didn’t look up. He was watching a small television screen mounted on the wall, the volume low. The news anchor was speaking, her voice calm and professional, but her eyes were sharp, focused.

“…reports indicate that Ms. Vale is currently in a stable condition at City General Hospital. Her medical team has stated that while her injuries are significant, they are not life-threatening. However, the cause of the temporary suppression of her abilities remains under intense investigation…”

Ethan nodded to Ray, who gave a small, distracted nod in return. He didn’t ask if Ethan had seen the event. He didn’t need to. The silence in the lobby was heavy with the unspoken weight of the day’s events.

Ethan took the elevator to the third floor. The doors slid open with a soft hydraulic sigh, revealing the hallway. He walked to his apartment, unlocked the door, and stepped inside.

The apartment was quiet. The silence here was different from the silence of the street. It was a vacuum. It was the absence of the world’s noise, the absence of the crowd, the absence of the screaming.

He locked the door behind him, the click of the latch echoing in the small space. He turned the deadbolt, then the chain, then he stood there for a moment, his hand resting on the wood of the door.

He walked to the window and pulled the curtains closed. The light from outside was blocked, replaced by the dim, yellow glow of the apartment’s overhead lights. He turned them off.

The room was dark.

He stood in the center of the living room, in the dark, and listened to the hum of the refrigerator. He could hear the tick of the clock on the counter, the distant, muffled rumble of a truck passing on the street outside. It was a list of sounds that used to comfort him, a soundtrack to a life that made sense.

Now, every noise seemed to carry the echo of the fall.

He walked to the kitchen and opened the refrigerator. The light inside was harsh, clinical. He looked at the mustard, the jar of pickles, the half onion in a container too large for it. He closed the door.

He didn’t want to eat. He didn’t want to move. He wanted to disappear.

He walked to the sofa and sat down. The cushions were soft, worn in by months of his sitting. He pulled his knees up to his chest, wrapping his arms around them. He was small. He was always small.

He thought about the bar. He thought about Elena. He thought about the way the air had felt thick, heavy, as if the world itself was holding its breath.

He thought about Aurora.

He thought about the way her body had crumpled, the way the blood had pooled, the way her eyes had rolled back into her head. He thought about the medic’s face, the confusion, the disbelief.

He thought about the word *flesh*.

It was a word that used to mean something to him. It meant that he was real. It meant that he was part of the world, that he had a place in it, even if that place was on the bottom.

But now, it meant something else. It meant that he could break. It meant that he could fall. It meant that he was just as fragile as she was.

He closed his eyes.

He didn’t sleep. He sat in the dark, in the silence, in the quiet of his apartment, and he waited for the world to stop turning.

He didn’t know if it would.

He didn’t know if he was safe.

He didn’t know if he was the cause.

He only knew that he was alone, and that the weight of the secret was sitting in his chest, heavy and cold, and he could not move it.

He could not explain it.

He could not hide it.

He could only sit with it.

And wait.

The hours passed. The light from the street lamps outside began to fade, replaced by the stars. The city continued to turn, to live, to breathe.

But in the small, dark apartment on the third floor, Ethan Vale sat in the silence, and he did not move.

He did not eat.

He did not sleep.

He only waited for the morning, and the news, and the questions.

He only waited for the world to ask him why.

And he had no answer.

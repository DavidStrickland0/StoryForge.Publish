The neon sign for *The Anchor* buzzed with a low, irritating hum that Ethan had tried to tune out for the last twenty minutes. It was a Tuesday, which meant the bar was half-full of people who wanted to feel like they belonged to something bigger than their own modest paychecks. The air smelled of stale beer, lemon cleaner, and the faint, ozone-sharp tang that always seemed to cling to the back booths.

Ethan sat at the end of the counter, his elbows resting on the sticky wood, nursing a whiskey he hadn’t taken a sip of. He was watching the man in the corner.

The man’s name was Kael, or at least that was what the news anchor called him when he rescued cats from trees or stopped traffic accidents. In person, Kael looked less like a savior and more like a statue carved from granite and bad attitudes. He was massive, his shoulders blocking out the dim light of the booth, his skin possessing a faint, unnatural sheen that suggested the invulnerability he advertised on his social media feeds. He wasn’t wearing a costume. He didn’t need one. His presence was the costume.

Across from him sat a woman. She looked young, maybe a few years older than Ethan, with a smear of red wine on her sleeve that she kept trying to dab away with a napkin. Her hands were shaking. Ethan could see it from across the room, the fine tremor in her fingers that betrayed a fear she was desperately trying to hide.

Kael leaned back in his chair, the leather creaking under his weight. He didn’t speak, but he didn’t need to. He simply stared at her, a heavy, predatory silence settling over their table like a physical weight. The other patrons, sensing the shift in the air, had begun to drift toward the bar or the exit, giving the corner a wide berth. It was a subtle choreography of avoidance, a dance Ethan knew well. You didn’t look at the super. You didn’t engage with the super. You gave them space, because if you didn’t, they might decide that your space was theirs to occupy.

The bartender, a thick-necked man with a tattooed forearm, wiped down the counter with a rag that did nothing to clean the grime. He glanced at the corner, then back at Ethan, his eyes widening slightly. He reached for the register, perhaps to check the time, perhaps to find a reason to intervene, but his hand hovered over the keys for a moment before he dropped it back to the counter. He shook his head, a small, defeated gesture, and turned back to the bottles.

Ethan felt a tightness in his chest, a familiar, dull ache that he had learned to ignore. This was the world. This was the hierarchy. Supers were celebrities, strategic assets, and, when they got bored or angry, untouchable gods. The law was a suggestion, a piece of paper that could be shredded by a fist that could crush a tank. If Kael decided to break the woman’s arm, or worse, the police would arrive too late, or they would arrive and be told that it was a misunderstanding, a joke that had gone too far. The super would apologize with a media team standing by, and the woman would be left with the bruise and the silence.

He took a sip of his whiskey. It burned, a welcome, grounding sensation. He looked at the woman again. Her eyes were fixed on the table, her jaw set tight. She wasn’t crying. She was enduring it. That was the part that made Ethan’s stomach turn. She was treating the situation as a weather event, something inevitable that she just had to wait out.

Ethan looked down at his own hands. They were ordinary. Pale, slightly calloused from his work in the hardware store, unremarkable in every way. He was twenty-four years old, and he had never once in his life felt the power to change a situation like this. He had no flight, no strength, no invulnerability. He had a degree in logistics and a lease on a one-bedroom apartment that he was one bad month away from losing.

He shifted in his seat, the stool scraping against the floor. The sound was small, easily lost in the background noise of the bar, but it felt loud to him. He watched the woman’s hand again. It was reaching for her phone, then pulling back. She was probably trying to call someone, but she didn’t want to draw attention. She didn’t want to make it worse.

Ethan’s mind began to calculate. The distance to the exit was twenty feet. The distance to the corner was thirty. If he stood up, Kael would see him. If Kael saw him, Kael might talk to him. And if Kael talked to him, Ethan would have to say something, and whatever he said would be wrong. He was a normal person. He was a target.

He thought of the news clips he had seen that morning. A super in Chicago had accidentally crushed a car while trying to stop a robbery. The driver had survived, but the super had been fined a token amount and given a public relations nightmare. The super had come back the next day, smiling at a press conference, and the driver had been left to file a lawsuit that would take years to resolve.

Ethan took another sip of his whiskey. The burn was still there, but it was fading. He looked at the woman, really looked at her, and saw the exhaustion in her eyes. She was tired. She was tired of being afraid. She was tired of being small.

He stood up.

The motion was slow, deliberate. He didn’t rush. Rushing would look like an attack, or a flight, and both were invitations. He placed his wallet on the counter, leaving a twenty-dollar bill on top of it. He didn’t look at the bartender. He didn’t look at the woman. He looked at the floor, at the pattern of the tiles, counting the steps as he moved toward the exit.

One. Two. Three.

The air in the bar seemed to thicken. He could feel the eyes on his back, the weight of the room’s attention shifting from the corner to him. He kept his head down, his shoulders relaxed, his hands at his sides. He was not a threat. He was not a hero. He was just a man leaving a bar.

Four. Five. Six.

He passed a table where two men were laughing, their voices too loud, their confidence born of the assumption that nothing bad could happen to them because they weren’t in the corner. Ethan didn’t look at them. He focused on the door, the glass pane with the *PULL* sign painted in peeling white letters.

Seven. Eight.

He was close now. The exit was ten feet away. He could smell the cold night air seeping through the gap under the door. He could feel the relief, the desperate, clawing need to be somewhere else, anywhere but here.

Nine. Ten.

He reached for the handle. It was cold under his fingers. He turned it, and the door swung open with a soft creak. The night rushed in, smelling of rain and asphalt, a sharp contrast to the stale air of the bar.

Ethan stepped out.

He didn’t look back. He didn’t want to see if Kael had watched him leave, or if the woman had looked up. He didn’t want to know if they were relieved, or angry, or disappointed. He just wanted to be gone.

He closed the door behind him, the latch clicking into place with a finality that felt like a promise. He stood on the sidewalk, the wet pavement reflecting the neon glow of the sign, and took a deep breath. The air was cool against his face, and for a moment, he felt like he was breathing for the first time in hours.

He began to walk, his footsteps echoing on the wet street, leaving the bar and its terrible, silent violence behind him. He didn’t know if he had done the right thing. He didn’t know if he had done enough. He only knew that he was safe, and that was the only thing that mattered to him right now.

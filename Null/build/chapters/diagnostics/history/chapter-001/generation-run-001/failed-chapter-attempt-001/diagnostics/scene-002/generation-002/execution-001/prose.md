The street outside was a river of people, and Ethan flowed with them, his stride matching the steady, purposeful pace of commuters heading toward subway stations or bars they intended to leave before they got too drunk. The air was cooler here, carrying the scent of rain on hot asphalt and the faint, metallic tang of exhaust from a delivery truck idling at the curb. He breathed in, the cold air filling lungs that had felt tight and constrained inside The Gilded Cage. His shoulders, which had been hunched up toward his ears for the last ten minutes, slowly unclenched. The tension in his jaw, where he had been grinding his teeth so hard his muscles ached, began to dissolve.

It was a physical release, a shedding of armor he hadn’t realized he was wearing. He adjusted the strap of his bag again, the leather creaking softly against his shoulder. The weight of his laptop, his charger, his mundane, ordinary life, felt grounding. It was a reminder of who he was: a data analyst who spent his days organizing spreadsheets and his evenings watching other people live lives of extraordinary consequence.

He turned a corner, moving away from the neon glow of the bar’s sign, which was still visible in the distance, a bright red rectangle humming with electricity. The crowd thinned slightly as they entered a quieter residential district, the storefronts changing from high-end boutiques and late-night clubs to dry cleaners, laundromats, and small grocery stores. The noise level dropped from a roar to a murmur. Ethan walked faster, his heart rate still elevated, but the adrenaline was starting to recede, leaving behind a hollow, echoing space in his chest.

He stopped at a crosswalk, waiting for the light to change. The green man flickered to life, and he stepped off the curb, his shoes clicking against the wet pavement. He looked up, instinctively scanning the sky, a habit he had developed since he was a teenager. There, cutting through the low-hanging clouds, was a streak of blue light. It moved with a speed that blurred its edges, a silent, powerful line against the grey backdrop. Another super. Another reminder of the world he lived in.

A world where strength dictated respect, and where the only way to survive was to know your place.

Ethan had always been good at knowing his place. It was a skill he had learned young, in a world where heroes could punch through bank vaults and villains could level city blocks. He had learned early that his voice, his opinions, his very presence, were small things. They were irrelevant in the face of someone who could stop a bullet with their bare hand. So he had learned to be quiet. He had learned to be unobtrusive. He had learned to blend in.

And tonight, in that bar, he had tried to be something else. He had tried to be a witness. A concerned citizen. A normal person who saw a woman being cornered by a super and felt a pang of sympathy that outweighed his fear. It had been a foolish impulse. A dangerous one. He had almost stepped forward, his hand half-raised to intervene, to say something, to *do* something. And Kaelen had looked at him.

The memory hit him with the force of a physical blow. The way the super’s granite-like skin had caught the low light of the bar, reflecting it back in dull, heavy glints. The way his eyes, small and dark in that massive head, had focused on Ethan with a sudden, predatory interest. The way Kaelen had smiled, a slow, cruel expression that didn’t reach those eyes, and said, in a voice like gravel grinding against stone, “What are you, little mouse? Trying to save the day?”

The other patrons had flinched. The woman in the corner, the one Kaelen had been cornering, had looked down at her hands, her face pale, her breath coming in short, quick gasps. She hadn’t looked at Ethan. She hadn’t looked at anyone. She had just shrunken, becoming smaller, trying to disappear into the booth’s worn leather.

Ethan had looked at her, and then at Kaelen, and he had felt the full, crushing weight of the disparity between them. Kaelen was a force of nature. He was a celebrity, a strategic asset, a god in a human body. And Ethan was a man with a bag and a laptop and a life that could be ended with a single, careless flick of a wrist.

So he had retreated. He had put his hands in his pockets, he had lowered his gaze, and he had walked away. He had navigated the crowded bar, moving through the stream of people like a ghost, avoiding eye contact, avoiding attention, avoiding *him*. He had reached the exit, pushed through the heavy door, and stepped out into the night.

He had made the right choice. He knew that with a certainty that settled in his bones. He had preserved his life. He had preserved his small, predictable, safe life. And that was enough. That was all he could do.

He reached his apartment building, a mid-rise brick structure with a fire escape that jangled in the wind. He entered the lobby, the smell of floor wax and old carpet hitting him, and took the elevator to the fourth floor. The elevator dinged, and he stepped out into the hallway. The floorboards creaked under his feet as he walked to his door, pulling out his keys, turning the lock, and stepping inside.

The apartment was quiet. The only sound was the hum of the refrigerator and the distant, muffled roar of traffic on the street below. Ethan dropped his bag on the kitchen counter, the sound of it hitting the wood sharp and final. He walked to the window, looking out at the city. The lights of the skyscrapers twinkled in the distance, a sea of electricity and glass. Somewhere up there, in one of those towers, Kaelen was probably sleeping. Or drinking. Or doing whatever it was that supers did when they weren’t breaking things.

Ethan turned away from the window and walked to the kitchen sink. He filled a glass with water, his hands steady, and drank it in long, slow gulps. The water was cold, shocking his system, and he felt a moment of clarity cut through the fog of fear and guilt.

He had left. He had left a woman trapped with a man who could kill her with a thought. He had walked away. He had chosen himself.

Was that wrong?

He didn’t know. He didn’t know if it was wrong, or if it was cowardice, or if it was just survival. He didn’t know if there was a difference. He didn’t know if he could have done anything else. He didn’t know if he would have done it again.

He set the glass down on the counter, the water rippling slightly. He looked at his reflection in the dark window. He looked ordinary. He looked human. He looked like a man who had just made a hard choice, and who was now trying to convince himself that it was the right one.

Outside, the city continued to turn. The lights flickered. The cars moved. The world went on, indifferent to the small, quiet decisions of a man who lived in the spaces between the giants. And Ethan Vale, for the first time in a long time, felt truly alone.

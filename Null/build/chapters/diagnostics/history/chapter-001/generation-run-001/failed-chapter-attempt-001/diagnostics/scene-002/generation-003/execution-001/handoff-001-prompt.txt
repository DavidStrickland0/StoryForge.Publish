The cold bit into his cheeks, sharper than the indoor air had been, carrying the scent of rain that hadn’t quite fallen. Ethan pulled his jacket tighter, the wool scratching against his skin in a way that felt oddly grounding. The street was a river of sound, a low hum of engines, distant laughter, and the rustle of coats moving in the dark. He let his gaze drift upward, catching the blue streak of a patrol drone sweeping overhead, its lights washing over the wet pavement before vanishing into the clouds. A signature. A reminder.

He adjusted the strap of his bag on his shoulder, the fabric creaking softly under the tension. The movement was small, mechanical, a way to occupy his hands while his mind churned. The night was crisp, the air clean in that specific urban way that smelled of ozone and exhaust, and for a few seconds, the world felt contained. The world felt contained and manageable.

Then he saw the news feed on the large digital board above the intersection.

The screen was dominated by a breaking news ticker, the text scrolling in urgent red. *SUPERHUMAN INCIDENT REPORTED IN SECTOR 4. VICTIM HOSPITALIZED.*

Ethan stopped walking. His heart gave a single, heavy thud against his ribs. He stared at the board, his eyes tracking the words until the blur of motion resolved into sharp, undeniable text. Sector 4 was three blocks away. It was near the bar he had just left.

He told himself it was a coincidence. A different super. A different incident. The city was full of them, and the news cycle was a relentless grinder that chewed up every minor conflict and spat it out for public consumption. He looked around, searching for the context, for the details that would explain the timing.

There were no details. Just the headline.

The guilt hit him before the fear did. It was a cold, sharp stone settling in his stomach, heavier than the bag on his shoulder. He had left. He had walked past the scene of a crisis because he was afraid of being hurt. The thought was so obvious, so damning, that he almost wanted to laugh at the absurdity of his own rationalization. He was safe. He was alive. And somewhere in the shadows of that bar, a woman was lying on the floor, unable to get up, while the man who had broken her was likely walking away, unscathed.

He turned back toward the entrance of The Gilded Cage. The neon sign buzzed, casting a pink and purple glow over the wet sidewalk. The door was open, and the warmth of the interior spilled out, carrying with it the scent of spilled drinks and stale beer.

Ethan didn’t want to go back in. Every instinct screaming inside his head told him to keep walking, to disappear into the crowd, to go home and lock his door and pretend the world was a series of disconnected, harmless moments. He was a small, predictable man in a world of giants. To re-enter the bar was to acknowledge that his choices had consequences, that his safety was not a default setting that the universe would protect simply because he was afraid.

But the image of the woman’s face, frozen in a mask of shock and pain, played on a loop in his mind. It wasn't a dramatic scene. It was mundane. Terrifyingly mundane. The way she had looked at him, not with anger, but with a desperate, silent question. *Why are you doing this?*

He had done nothing. He had done the only thing that made sense for a normal person. And yet, the emptiness in his chest felt like a hollow space where a conscience should have been.

He took a step forward. Then another.

The air near the bar was different. It was thicker, charged with the residual energy of the confrontation. He could hear the muffled sounds of voices inside, the clink of glasses, the low thrum of music that was just loud enough to cover up the quiet, sharp intake of breath from someone in pain.

Ethan stood in the doorway, his hand resting on the doorframe. The wood was cool and damp. He looked inside. The bar was crowded, a maze of bodies and dim lights. He could see the back of a man’s head, the broad shoulders of a bartender, the glint of a glass catching the neon light. He couldn't see the woman. He couldn't see the super.

He was just a silhouette in the doorway. A little mouse in the dark.

The realization that he was watching, that he was choosing to watch, made his skin prickle. He wasn't intervening. He wasn't stopping anything. He was just... He was just present. And yet, the presence of the threat was so heavy, so oppressive, that the air seemed to vibrate around him.

He pulled his hand back from the doorframe. His fingers were trembling. He clenched them into a fist, feeling the nails dig into his palm. It was a small, sharp pain, a reminder that he was flesh and bone, that he could be broken.

He looked at the street again. The blue streak of the patrol drone was still visible in the distance, a distant, indifferent eye. The city was moving around him, indifferent to his fear, indifferent to his guilt.

Ethan turned away from the bar.

But as he turned the corner, putting the neon glow of The Gilded Cage behind him, he felt the weight of the decision settle over his shoulders. It was a heavy, invisible cloak, and he knew, with a cold certainty that settled into his bones, that he would carry it for a long time.

He kept walking.

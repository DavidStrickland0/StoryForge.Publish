He found an open seat near the far end of the bar, a spot where the light from the overhead fixtures was dim enough to cast shadows across his face but bright enough to see the grain of the wood under his hands. He sat down, resting his elbows on the sticky surface, and ordered a whiskey neat.

The bartender, a man with a scar running down his neck, slid the glass across the counter without a word. Ethan took a sip, letting the burn settle in his chest. It was a good drink, or at least a decent one, and it served the purpose of making the moment feel significant. He turned his attention to the room, observing the ecosystem of The Anchor.

Across the bar, a group of three young men were laughing, one of them flexing a bicep that looked slightly too dense, the skin shimmering with a faint, unnatural iridescence. A super. Low-level, probably just enhanced density or strength. They were joking, throwing their heads back, completely unaware of the subtle way the other patrons shifted their weight away from them. It was a small thing, a collective unconscious withdrawal, but Ethan saw it clearly. The humans in the room moved around the supers the way traffic flowed around a pothole. They didn’t stop, they didn’t confront the obstruction, they just adjusted their paths, keeping a respectful, fearful distance.

That was the reality of the world Ethan lived in. Laws existed, certainly. The Supreme Court had ruled on the rights of non-powered citizens, and the police force still took reports of assault. But the enforcement was a suggestion, a formality that crumbled the moment someone with the ability to level a building decided that the law was inconvenient. Ethan had seen it happen to a coworker a year ago. The man had been mugged by a super with the ability to manipulate kinetic energy. The police had arrived, taken a statement, and then, within the hour, the super’s lawyer had filed a motion to dismiss the charges on grounds of "unintentional force fluctuation." The man had lost his job for the incident, the super had paid a fine that was less than the cost of his monthly insurance, and the world had continued to spin.

Ethan watched the iridescent bicep flex again. The young man with the super was leaning over the bar, his elbow close to a woman sitting next to him. She was smiling, but it was a tight, guarded smile, the kind that didn't reach her eyes. She kept her hands flat on the bar, her fingers curled slightly, as if she were ready to snatch a drink or a phone at a moment's notice. Ethan recognized the posture. It was the posture of a person who was being polite because the alternative was dangerous.

It was a feeling that had settled into his bones over the years, a constant, low-level hum of frustration at the sheer arbitrariness of power. He knew that if someone wanted to hurt him, he could not stop them. He could only hope that they didn't want to, or that they were feeling generous.

It was a habit, born of necessity. He always knew where the doors were, where the windows were, and which path offered the least resistance. It was a small, pathetic thing, but it was his.

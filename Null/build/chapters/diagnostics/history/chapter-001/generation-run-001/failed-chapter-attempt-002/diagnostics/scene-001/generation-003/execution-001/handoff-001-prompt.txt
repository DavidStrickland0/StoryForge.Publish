The neon sign outside the window buzzed with a low, persistent hum, painting the wet pavement in shades of electric blue. Ethan Vale pulled his coat tighter, the synthetic fabric crinkling as he adjusted the strap of his messenger bag. Inside, a laptop, a stack of technical manuals, and a sandwich he’d bought at the deli on Fifth Street clinked together with every step. It was a Tuesday, which meant the bar was doing the business that kept it open. The place was called The Anchor, a nod to the maritime heritage of the district, though the only thing anchored here right now was the smell of stale beer and aggressive citrus cleaner.

He took a seat at the far end of the bar, the one where the light was dimmest and the patrons were mostly heads bent over phones or glasses. He liked the end. It offered a panoramic view of the room and a clear line of sight to the back exit, which led to an alley he knew was monitored by city cameras. It was a calculated position. Ethan had learned early in his twenty-four years of life that being invisible was a skill that required active practice, especially in a city where invisibility was a privilege reserved for those who could afford it or break through walls to leave.

He ordered a water. The bartender, a man with arms that looked like they had been carved from oak and a tattoo of a lightning bolt on his forearm, poured it without a word. The glass was thick, condensation beading on the surface, and when Ethan set it down, the sound was a dull thud against the wooden counter. He took a sip. It was cold and flat, but it tasted like safety.

To his left, a group of four people laughed over a pitcher of something amber. Their conversation was fragmented, punctuated by bursts of sound that seemed too loud for the size of the room. Ethan watched them the way one watches weather. You note the pressure drop, the cloud formations, the temperature shift. You don’t engage. You just observe. One of them, a woman with hair dyed a violent shade of pink, leaned across the table to touch her companion’s shoulder. As her fingers made contact, the air around them seemed to ripple, a subtle distortion in the light that Ethan had learned to ignore. It was a minor power, a kinetic feedback loop, probably enough to shatter a glass but not enough to cause a structural breach. He saw her pull back quickly, a flush of embarrassment rising in her cheeks, and the group’s laughter redoubled, covering the small social fracture.

This was the world he lived in. A world where the strongest people were also the most careful, and the most careful people were the ones who paid the most attention to the rest. Supers were celebrities now, or strategic assets, or both. They had their own news cycles, their own legal departments, their own version of reality where the laws of physics were suggestions and the laws of man were options. Ethan had a job in data entry, a small apartment above a laundromat, and a set of rules he followed with religious precision. Don’t draw attention. Don’t be in the way. Don’t make eye contact with anyone who looks like they can bench-press a sedan.

He turned his attention back to the rest of the bar. A man near the entrance was arguing with a waitress. The argument was quiet, but the tension was palpable. The man was tall, broad-shouldered, with a face that had seen better days and worse. He wore a suit that cost more than Ethan’s annual rent, and he had the posture of someone accustomed to taking up space. He was a Super, one of the mid-tier enforcers who worked for the city’s private security contracts. Ethan recognized him from the local news channels. His name was something like Kael or Kane, a generic enough name for a man who could stop a bullet with his palm.

Kane was complaining about the service. The waitress was standing rigid, her arms crossed over her chest, her face a mask of professional indifference. She was normal. Ethan could tell by the way she held her breath, by the slight tremor in her hands as she wiped down the counter. She was afraid, but she was keeping her fear in a box, locking it tight and putting it away. That was the normal human response to power. You minimized yourself. You made yourself small. You hoped that by being small, you would be overlooked.

Ethan felt a familiar prickle of irritation at the base of his neck. It was the same feeling he got when he saw a dog barking at a mailman, or a child throwing a tantrum in a grocery store. It was the recognition of a predator testing its boundaries. Kane was not threatening the waitress with violence, not yet. He was threatening her with his presence. He was using his size, his status, his power, to make her feel small. It was a subtle form of dominance, a reminder that in this world, strength was the only currency that mattered.

Ethan took another sip of his water. He looked around the room again. The people in the corner were still laughing, but their eyes kept darting toward Kane. They were afraid, too. They were all afraid. It was a collective anxiety, a low-level hum of fear that underpinned the entire social fabric of the city. People smiled, they chatted, they drank, but underneath it all, there was the knowledge that one wrong word, one wrong look, one moment of carelessness, could turn a casual evening into a medical emergency. And unlike a medical emergency for a normal person, a medical emergency involving a Super was often just the beginning.

He checked his watch. It was 7:15 PM. He had told his supervisor he would be in late. He had a report to finish, a set of data tables to verify, a small and manageable set of tasks that would allow him to go home, eat his sandwich, and watch the news. The news would be full of Supers. There was always a Super in the news. A new rescue, a new disaster, a new lawsuit, a new scandal. It was the diet of the modern age, and Ethan had learned to eat it in small, controlled portions so that he wouldn’t choke.

The door was heavy, made of wood and iron, with a small window in the center that looked out onto the alley. Through the window, he could see the wet pavement, the neon glow, the empty street. It was the same view he had seen when he arrived. The world had not changed. It was still there, waiting for him to walk out into it. He reached for the handle.

It was cold to the touch.

He paused.

The handle was cold. That was unusual. The bar was warm, the air thick with body heat and the warmth of the lamps. The door should have been warm, or at least neutral. But it was cold. It was cold like the outside air, cold like the water in his glass. It was a physical sensation, a tangible reality that reminded him that he was here, that he was real, that he was a body in a world of bodies.

Kane was still arguing. The waitress was still standing rigid. The four friends were still laughing.

Ethan looked at his hand. He looked at the door. He looked at the room.

He had a report to finish. He had a sandwich to eat. He had a life to live.

The stool squeaked again. He picked up his water. He drank.

The hum of the neon sign continued. The laughter of the friends continued. The argument of Kane continued.

The world continued.

And Ethan Vale, sitting at the end of the bar, with his bag on his lap and his water in his hand, watched it all with the quiet, patient attention of a man who knew exactly what he was, and exactly what he was not.

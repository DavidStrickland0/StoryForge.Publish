The rain had thickened, turning the streetlights into smeared halos of amber and white. Ethan pulled his hood lower, the wet fabric clinging to his forehead. His phone screen was fogged with his breath, and he had to wipe it with his thumb to read the time again. 7:23 PM. The blue light reflected in the puddles at his feet, a cold, digital anchor to reality in a world that felt increasingly unstable.

He stopped walking.

One block away. Two blocks would be better. His feet moved on instinct, carrying him past a row of closed storefronts, the windows reflecting the gray sky and the blurred shapes of pedestrians hurrying home. He was safe. He was anonymous. He was a man with a wet jacket and a slightly sore throat from the day’s cough, and that was a much better problem to have than the one left behind in the booth at The Rusty Anchor.

But the image didn’t fade. It sat in the back of his mind, sharp and intrusive, like a splinter he couldn’t dig out. The way the woman’s shoulders had hunched, trying to make herself smaller, as if her body had decided that taking up less space might convince the man at the table that she was less interesting. Less worth the trouble.

Ethan had seen that look before. It was the look of someone who had learned that screaming doesn’t work, that calling for help is a form of theater that the powerful often ignore, and that the best strategy for survival was to become furniture.

He turned a corner, the sound of the rain growing louder, the city noise fading into a dull roar. He checked his phone again. 7:24 PM. He was still on schedule. He was safe. He was ordinary.

And for now, that was enough.

The man in the booth had been easy to identify. In a world where supers were celebrities, strategic assets, and an unofficial ruling class, you learned to spot them before they even spoke. It was a kind of sixth sense that developed over years of watching television, scrolling through news feeds, and navigating a society where the strongest voices were often the loudest and the least accountable. The man had sat with a relaxed, predatory ease, his posture suggesting that the table was too small for him, that the room was too crowded, that the very concept of personal space was a suggestion he was choosing to ignore.

Ethan had noticed the way the other patrons in the bar had shifted. It was subtle, a collective tightening of the social muscles. Conversations died down. Eyes averted. The bartender, a heavyset man with a mustache that looked like it had been sculpted from clay, had moved away from the register, his hands clasped behind his back in a gesture of deliberate, passive distance. They knew what they were looking at. They recognized the danger, and they had made a pragmatic choice: to be invisible.

That was the social contract of the superhuman era. You did not engage. You did not witness. You did not create a record. Because in the eyes of the law, and certainly in the eyes of the man with the strength to crush a car door like a soda can, a bystander’s testimony was worth nothing. It was an inconvenience, a nuisance, a detail that could be smoothed over with a public relations statement and a generous donation to a local charity.

Ethan had felt that weight in his own chest. The pressure of being a normal person in a room full of normal people who had collectively decided that their safety was more important than their morality. He had seen the woman’s distress, the way her hand trembled as she reached for her glass, only to pull it back when the man’s gaze fixed on her. He had seen the way her eyes darted toward the exit, seeking an escape route that didn’t exist because the man was sitting in the center of the room, occupying the space, owning the air.

And Ethan had done nothing.

He had taken his beer, left a five-dollar bill on the table, and walked out the door. He had felt the heat of the man’s gaze on his back, a physical weight, a threat that hung in the air like the scent of stale smoke and spilled liquor. He had not turned around. He had not looked back. He had walked to the sidewalk, turned the corner, and continued walking until the sound of the bar was lost in the rain.

Now, standing on the corner of 4th and Main, Ethan felt the full weight of his inaction. It was a cold, heavy thing, sitting in his stomach. It was the kind of guilt that didn’t go away with a shower or a night’s sleep. It was the kind of guilt that followed you home, that sat at the foot of your bed, that whispered in the back of your mind when you were trying to fall asleep.

*You could have done something.*

He had not.

He had prioritized his own safety over the woman’s distress. He had chosen self-preservation over heroism. And in a world where heroes were celebrities and villains were often just men with too much power and too little empathy, that was the only rational choice.

Ethan looked up at the sky. The rain was falling in earnest now, a steady, cold sheet that blurred the horizon. He pulled his phone out of his pocket again, his thumb hovering over the screen. He could call the police. He could report the incident. He could give a description of the man, the woman, the time, the place.

But what would happen?

The police would come. They would ask questions. They would take a statement. And then they would leave. Because the man was a super. And supers did not go to jail for harassment. Supers went to therapy. Supers gave interviews. Supers made public apologies that were sincere enough to satisfy the cameras and empty enough to satisfy their egos.

Ethan lowered his phone. He tucked it back into his pocket. He adjusted his hood, feeling the cold water drip down his neck. He was safe. He was ordinary. He was alive.

And the woman in the booth?

She was still there. Probably waiting for the rain to stop. Probably waiting for the man to leave. Probably waiting for someone to help.

Ethan didn’t look back. He couldn’t afford to. If he looked back, he might see something that would make him feel guilty, and guilt was a luxury he couldn’t afford.

He turned the corner, the sound of the rain growing louder, the city noise fading into a dull roar. He checked his phone again. 7:25 PM. He was still on schedule. He was safe. He was ordinary.

And for now, that was enough.

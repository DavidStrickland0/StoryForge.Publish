The first thing Ethan noted was the exit.

It wasn’t a dramatic realization or a sudden spike of adrenaline. It was simply the first data point in the checklist he ran through whenever he entered a space crowded with strangers. The door was glass, heavy and thick, leading out to the sidewalk where the rain still slicked the pavement. It wasn’t locked. The handle was brass, smooth and unobtrusive. From his current position near the back booth, the path to that door was clear, unobstructed by tables, servers, or the low-hanging neon sign buzzing above the bar.

He took a sip of his beer. It was warm, flat, and tasted faintly of the aluminum can it had been poured from, but he drank it anyway. The main reason he was in The Rusty Anchor, a dive bar that smelled of stale hops and industrial cleaner, was because it was the only establishment in the district that didn’t advertise its security measures with the gleaming, predatory confidence of a chrome-plated weapon. Most bars in the city had supers on staff—people who could bench-press a sedan or see through walls. Those places felt like walking into a lion’s den wearing a vest of zebra print. Here, the bouncer was a tired man in his fifties with a bad knee and a clipboard. The patrons were mostly locals, people who kept their heads down and their voices low.

Ethan checked his phone. 7:14 PM. He had planned to be home by eight. His apartment was quiet, the walls thin enough that he could hear the muffled thump of bass from the neighbors’ speakers, but it was his. In his apartment, he didn’t have to calculate the trajectory of a fist or the likelihood of a ceiling panel being punched through by a drunk super who thought the furniture was an insult to his intelligence.

He put the phone away and watched the room.

It was a Tuesday, so the crowd was sparse. There were maybe twenty people in the place. A few couples huddled together over cheap plates of nachos. A group of students laughing loudly at something on a screen. A solitary man in the corner nursing a whiskey that had probably gone flat an hour ago. None of them were supers. Ethan had learned to spot them, or rather, to spot the *lack* of them. Supers moved with a certain weight, a subtle assertion of space that ordinary people didn’t have. They didn’t duck when someone walked past them. They didn’t flinch when a glass clinked too loudly. They occupied the room as if they owned it, because in many ways, they did.

The man in the booth across from Ethan was the only exception.

He was large. That was the first thing. Not just tall, but broad in a way that seemed to defy the limits of the booth’s cushioning. He was wearing a suit that cost more than Ethan’s car, the fabric cutting diagonally across his chest to show off the muscle beneath. His hair was slicked back, perfect, as if he had just stepped out of a wind tunnel. He was talking to a woman who sat on the other side of the table, her hands wrapped around a glass of water, her shoulders hunched forward.

Ethan didn’t know them. He didn’t know their names, their stories, or their reasons for being there. He knew, however, that the woman’s body language was screaming. Her gaze was fixed on the table, but her eyes were darting, calculating. Her hands were trembling, the glass rattling slightly against the coaster. The man, on the other hand, was relaxed. He was leaning back, one arm draped over the back of his chair, his elbow resting on the headrest of the seat behind him. He was smiling. It was a polite, practiced smile, the kind used by politicians and salesmen, but it didn’t reach his eyes.

Ethan watched the man’s hand rest on the table. He didn’t touch the woman. He didn’t need to. His presence was enough. The air around them seemed to thicken, the ambient noise of the bar fading into a dull hum. Ethan could see the tension in the woman’s jaw, the way she held her breath. It was a familiar dynamic. He had seen it in the news, on the street, in the quiet desperation of people trying to navigate a world where strength was the ultimate currency.

He turned his chair back toward the wall. He finished the rest of his beer, the taste of the metal now sharp on his tongue. He left a five-dollar bill on the table, enough for the beer and a tip that would make the server smile for a few minutes. He didn’t look back at the couple in the booth. He didn’t need to. He had already assessed the situation, and the conclusion was clear: this was not his fight. He was not a hero. He was not a sidekick. He was a twenty-four-year-old graphic designer who liked his coffee black and his weekends quiet.

He stood up, smoothed the front of his jeans, and walked toward the exit.

The path was clear. He moved quickly, but without urgency. He didn’t want to draw attention. He kept his head down, his hands in his pockets, his steps steady. As he passed the bar, he caught the eye of the bartender, a woman with a tattoo of a snake wrapped around her neck. She nodded at him, a brief acknowledgment of the unspoken rule: *don’t cause trouble, don’t get involved, don’t be a problem.*

He nodded back.

The door was heavy when he pushed it open. The cool air of the night hit his face, carrying the scent of wet asphalt and distant exhaust. He stepped out onto the sidewalk, the rain beginning to fall in earnest, a fine mist that soaked into his jacket. He pulled his hood up, the fabric darkening instantly under the downpour.

He walked.

He didn’t know where he was going, exactly. He just kept moving, away from the bar, away from the man in the booth, away from the woman who was still sitting there, probably still waiting for the rain to stop, or for the man to leave, or for someone to help. Ethan didn’t look back. He couldn’t afford to. If he looked back, he might see something that would make him feel guilty, and guilt was a luxury he couldn’t afford.

He turned the corner, the sound of the rain growing louder, the city noise fading into a dull roar. He checked his phone again. 7:22 PM. He was still on schedule. He was safe. He was ordinary.

And for now, that was enough.

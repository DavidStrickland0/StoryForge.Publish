The rain had changed character. It was no longer the light, staccato tapping that had greeted him on the sidewalk, but a steady, gray sheet that blurred the edges of the city into a watercolor smear. Ethan pulled his hood tighter, the fabric damp and heavy against his forehead, and kept walking. His shoes squelched with every step, the sound loud and intimate in the quiet of the street. He was two blocks away now, the neon glow of The Rusty Anchor just a smudge of orange in the misty distance.

He stopped at a bus shelter, the glass panels fogged with condensation and the breath of the few people waiting inside. He wasn’t going to take the bus; he lived in the apartment above the laundromat on Fifth, which was a ten-minute walk in the rain and a three-minute walk if he didn’t stop to think about anything. But the shelter offered a dry surface to lean against, and the hum of the electric heater inside the booth provided a low, comforting vibration that seemed to ground him.

He checked his phone again. 7:24 PM. The message from his landlord, Mrs. Gable, was still sitting at the top of his inbox: *Lease renewal is due by the 1st. I’m raising the rent. You can pay in cash or check. I don’t do Venmo for a man of your... station.* He didn’t have a station. He had a job as a junior data entry clerk for a logistics company that shipped parts for drones he would never see. He had a savings account that was slowly shrinking under the weight of rent, utilities, and the particular, expensive tax of living in a city where a cup of coffee cost eight dollars because the barista had once lifted a truck with her bare hands.

He unlocked his phone to type a reply, then stopped. The screen was a dark mirror, reflecting his own face. He looked tired. The kind of tired that sleep didn’t fix, the kind that came from being constantly, low-level aware that the world was not built for people like him.

He thought about the man in the booth.

He hadn’t seen the man’s face clearly, not really. The light in the bar was dim, and the man had been sitting with his back to the window, a silhouette of broad shoulders and thick arms that seemed to take up too much space for the wooden chair. But Ethan had seen the way the man moved. It was a slow, deliberate economy of motion, the kind of confidence that comes from knowing that gravity is a suggestion and physics is a negotiable term. When the man had reached for his glass, the wood of the table hadn’t creaked. When he had turned his head to speak to the woman, the air around him seemed to still, as if the rest of the bar was holding its breath out of respect or fear.

Ethan had seen the woman, too. She had been sitting across from him, her hands wrapped around a mug that looked like a toy in her grip. She wasn’t drinking. She was just holding it, her knuckles white, her eyes fixed on a point somewhere between the man’s shoulder and the middle of the table. She was small, unremarkable, wearing a jacket that was a size too big for her, the kind of coat you buy because it’s cheap and warm, not because it fits. She had the look of someone who had walked into a room she shouldn’t have, and was now waiting for the door to open so she could leave without being noticed.

But the door wasn’t opening. And the man wasn’t leaving.

Ethan had felt the pull of it, the ancient, human instinct to help. To step in. To say, *Hey, maybe we should give her some space,* or *I think you’re being a bit much,* or simply to stand there, solid and present, and let the man know that someone was watching. It was the right thing to do. It was what his mother had taught him, and what his high school ethics class had emphasized, and what every movie he had ever watched had promised would happen. The hero steps in. The balance is restored. The world makes sense.

But Ethan was not a hero. He was a data entry clerk with a bad knee and a lease that was about to be raised. And the man in the booth was not a villain in a movie. He was a super.

Ethan remembered the news reports. There was one last month, a super named Titan who had punched through a bank vault because the teller had given him a rude time. The police had been there, but they hadn’t done anything. They had stood at the edge of the shattered wall, their hands in their pockets, and watched as Titan walked out with a briefcase full of cash. The news anchor had called it a "regrettable incident," and then moved on to a segment about a new flight school for supers.

There was another one, a woman who could control fire. She had set her own apartment on fire during a fight with her roommate. The fire department had been unable to put it out because the water turned to steam before it touched the flames. She had lost her home, her job, and her license to practice her powers. The news had called her a "tragic figure," and then sold a subscription to a new app that could detect fire hazards in your kitchen.

Supers were not people. They were forces of nature. You didn’t argue with a hurricane. You didn’t negotiate with a flood. You got out of the way, and you hoped that the wind changed direction, and you hoped that you weren’t in the path of the debris.

Ethan had made the right choice. He had left. He had prioritized his own survival over his own morality. It was a cold calculation, but it was a clear one. And now, standing in the bus shelter, watching the rain slide down the glass, he felt a strange, hollow relief. He was safe. He was alive. And that was enough.

But beneath the relief, there was a gnawing, persistent thought.

*What if he hadn’t left?*

What if he had stayed? What if he had tried to help?

The thought was a small, dark seed, and it grew. He imagined himself walking over to the booth. He imagined himself standing next to the woman, his hand on her shoulder, a gesture of solidarity. He imagined the man looking up, his eyes meeting his. And he imagined the look in the man’s eyes.

It wasn’t anger. It wasn’t hatred. It was something worse. It was indifference.

The man would look at Ethan the way a person looks at a bug on the sidewalk. Not with malice, but with a complete and total lack of recognition. *You are not important. You are not real. You are a small, fragile thing that exists in the margins of my life, and I do not care if you are here or if you are gone.*

And then the man would move. And Ethan would be crushed. Or burned. Or thrown. Or simply erased.

The thought was so vivid that Ethan’s stomach turned. He took a step back from the glass, his heart beating in his throat. He looked at his hands. They were shaking, just slightly, a tremor that he couldn’t quite control. He clenched them into fists, forcing the muscles to hold, forcing the body to obey the mind.

He was ordinary. He was weak. He was small.

And that was fine.

He pulled his phone out of his pocket again and typed a reply to Mrs. Gable. *I’ll send the check on the 1st. Thanks for letting me know.* He hit send and put the phone away.

He stepped out of the shelter and into the rain. The cold hit him immediately, a sharp, clean shock that cleared the fog from his mind. He walked faster, his steps quick and purposeful. He was going home. He was going to take a hot shower, put on dry clothes, and eat a piece of toast. And then he was going to go to sleep.

And tomorrow, he would go to work. And he would enter data. And he would pay his rent. And he would be safe.

He turned the corner onto Fifth Street, the laundromat glowing with a warm, yellow light. He could see the sign, the faded paint, the peeling sticker that said *NO SUPERS ALLOWED.*

He stopped.

The sign was new. It had been there for a month, since the incident with the super who had tried to use the washing machines to dry his cape. The manager had put it up, a small, defiant act of normalcy in a world that had forgotten what normalcy felt like.

Ethan looked at the sign. He looked at the rain. He looked at the empty street.

And then he walked into the laundromat.

The bell above the door chimed, a bright, cheerful sound that seemed absurd in the gray evening. The manager, a thick man named Sal, looked up from his newspaper. He saw Ethan, and his face softened into a familiar, weary smile.

"Hey, Ethan," Sal said. "Rough night?"

Ethan shrugged. "Just the rain, Sal."

"Rain’s a bitch," Sal said. "Come on in. Get a coffee. On the house."

Ethan looked at the coffee machine. It was a cheap, plastic thing, the kind that made coffee that tasted like burnt water and regret. But it was coffee. And it was warm. And it was for him.

"Thanks, Sal," Ethan said.

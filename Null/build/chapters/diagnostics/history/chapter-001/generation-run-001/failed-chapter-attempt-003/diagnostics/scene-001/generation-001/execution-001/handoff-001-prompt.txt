The neon sign for The Rusty Anchor flickered with a rhythmic, electric buzz that Ethan had learned to associate with impending headache. He stood on the sidewalk, collar of his jacket turned up against the late autumn damp, and watched the crowd funneling into the bar. It was a Tuesday, which meant the regulars were there, but the presence of a super made the atmosphere thicker, charged with the same static electricity that preceded a thunderstorm.

Ethan wasn’t here for the beer. He was here for the view of the street, which he had already secured, and the exit route, which he had already mapped. He took a sip of the lukewarm water he’d ordered, his eyes scanning the room with the detached precision of a man counting exits. There was the main door, behind him, currently blocked by a cluster of patrons laughing over bottles. To his left, a service door leading to the alley, narrow and dim. To his right, the bar itself, a long, polished counter where the bartender was wiping a glass with a rag that looked more like it had been used to clean a grease trap.

He shifted his weight, angling his body so that his back was to the wall and his line of sight covered the main entrance. This was the first rule of navigating public spaces in a world where physics was a suggestion and liability insurance was a distant memory: always know where you are, and always know where the danger is.

The super was at a table near the center of the room. He was large, a fact that was evident even from a distance, his shoulders broad enough to obscure the light from the hanging lamp above him. He wore a leather jacket that looked expensive and ill-fitting, and his skin had a faint, pearlescent sheen that caught the light every time he moved. Ethan recognized the sheen. It was the mark of a class-three invulnerability, the kind that allowed the wearer to walk through a brick wall if they felt like it, or to shrug off a bullet with a casual grimace.

Ethan felt a familiar tightness in his chest, a low-grade anxiety that had become a background hum in his life since the Supers Act had been repealed. He didn’t hate the man. He didn’t know him. He simply knew that the man was a variable in an equation that Ethan had no control over, and that the equation was currently set to solve for "harm."

The bar was crowded, the air thick with the smell of spilled lager and stale smoke. People laughed, drank, and made conversation, their voices rising and falling in a chaotic symphony of social noise. Ethan watched them, noting their body language. Some leaned in, eager to please, their eyes fixed on the super with a mixture of awe and fear. Others kept their distance, their shoulders hunched, their movements hesitant. They were all aware of the imbalance, even if they didn’t have the words for it.

Ethan’s own posture was relaxed, but his mind was working. He calculated the distance between himself and the super. Twelve feet. That was enough. He could leave through the service door if necessary, or push through the main entrance if the crowd cleared. He didn’t need to be brave. He needed to be efficient.

He took another sip of water, his eyes drifting to the ceiling. The lights were fluorescent, harsh and unflattering, casting long shadows that made the room look smaller than it was. He noticed a crack in the plaster, a jagged line that ran from the corner of the ceiling down to the edge of the bar. It was a small detail, insignificant, but it reminded him that the building was old, that the walls were thin, that the world was fragile.

The super spoke, his voice low and rumbling, and the woman sitting across from him flinched. It was a subtle movement, a slight tightening of her shoulders, a quick dart of her eyes to the door. Ethan watched her, his own heart rate steady. He didn’t intervene. He didn’t have the strength, the speed, or the invulnerability to do so. He was just a man with a glass of water and a good exit strategy.

The bass vibrated through the soles of his shoes, a low rumble that felt more like an earthquake than a party soundtrack. Ethan pressed his back against the rough brickwork near the service door, shielding himself from the thud of dancing bodies. He kept his eyes on the exit, calculating angles and shadows with the practiced detachment of a man who had long ago decided he preferred walking home in the rain to engaging with the gods walking among mortals.

Through the haze of smoke and spilled drinks, he watched the corner table again. The woman sat with her hands wrapped tightly around a plastic cup that held nothing but ice, her knuckles white enough to be visible even in the gloom. Her posture spoke of containment; she was huddled into herself, making as little space as possible, yet the man standing over her towered like a statue carved from arrogance and light.

He knew better than to assume the ordinary rules applied here. The man’s skin shimmered with an unnatural iridescence, shifting from deep violet to bruised purple as he moved, and his suit cost more than Ethan would earn in decades of grinding overtime. When the figure laughed—a sound that carried too clearly across the noisy room, cutting through the music like a blade—it wasn’t just mockery; it was the casual declaration of absolute ownership. The woman hadn’t moved, hadn’t spoken, hadn’t tried to create distance, because she knew there was no way to create distance when the threat itself redefined the geometry of the world around her.

Ethan’s stomach twisted with a cold nausea that had nothing to do with the cheap beer he’d left untouched on his stool earlier. The air around the table seemed heavier, charged with static that made the hair on his arms stand up without wind or current. This wasn’t just intimidation; this was a display of physics rewritten for entertainment, and in this context, the normal person’s only leverage was invisibility through indifference. But indifference felt like a betrayal when someone else was caught in the crossfire.

He shifted his weight, careful not to disturb the trembling glass at his own table, though he didn’t look back. He didn’t care for that particular cup anymore; it felt like an anchor holding him to a sinking ship. If he stayed, he became complicit by association, and if he left now, he was leaving a stranger to face something that would likely end her night with bruises or broken things she couldn’t repair even after the fact. The man’s laughter grew louder, drawing the attention of nearby dancers who looked away quickly, as though acknowledging that some tragedies were too grand for their presence to matter.

Ethan turned his head slightly, watching the way the light caught the shimmering surface of the suit again. The figure had shifted position without making a sound, leaning closer until the woman’s back pressed against the wall, trapped by proximity alone. There was no attempt to hide anything; the power played out in full view for anyone foolish enough to watch, and yet everyone kept their eyes elsewhere. Ethan realized with chilling clarity that watching this unfold meant participating, even if his hands remained on his sides and his feet did not cross the line into the circle of fear.

There was a sound like glass breaking somewhere else in the bar, but he barely registered it. The noise outside had never been so loud; the music felt distant now, muffled by something deeper than walls or volume. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic rhythm that made no sense in a room where people laughed as if nothing mattered. He closed his eyes for a fraction of a second and forced himself to remember why he’d come here in the first place: because he wanted to be somewhere ordinary, where laws still applied and consequences followed actions in ways that didn’t require superhuman intervention to balance.

The exit was only ten feet away, but it felt like crossing an ocean when every step required him to ignore the weight of another person’s terror. He could hear the woman’s breathing catch on a ragged inhale, too quiet to be heard over the music but loud enough to cut through his thoughts. He knew he couldn’t help her—not really. Not without risking being hurt or worse, and not when the rules of the world had been rewritten around someone else’s pleasure. But he also knew that leaving now would make him something he wasn’t willing to be yet, and staying meant becoming something he never wanted to become at all.

So he moved again, his shoes making only faint sounds against the polished floor as he stepped toward the doorway. The bass still pounded beneath his feet, but it no longer felt like music; it was an obstacle between him and what needed to happen next. He didn’t look back once more before stepping out into the cool night air, where the hum of traffic gave way to silence that felt too sharp against the roar of the place he’d just fled.

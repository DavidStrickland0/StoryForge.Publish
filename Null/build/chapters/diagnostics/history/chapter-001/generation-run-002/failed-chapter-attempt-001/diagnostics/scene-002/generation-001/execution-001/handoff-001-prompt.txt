The man in the corner had stopped talking.

That was the worst part.

A moment ago, his voice had been a low, rumbling thing that seemed to vibrate the floorboards, a sound that made Ethan’s teeth ache in a way that had nothing to do with the whiskey. He had been leaning over the woman, his face inches from hers, his massive hand resting on the back of her chair. His fingers were thick as sausages, the knuckles scarred and white, and he had been smiling. It wasn’t a happy smile. It was the look of a cat watching a mouse try to decide which direction to run.

Now, he was just looking at her.

The woman was shaking. She wasn’t making a sound, but Ethan could see the way her hands gripped the edge of the table, her knuckles white, her shoulders hunched up around her ears. She was wearing a simple blue blouse, the kind Ethan saw on his coworkers, and her hair was pulled back in a messy bun that was coming loose. She looked terrified.

The super’s name was Kael. Ethan knew it because the news had featured it last week, when Kael had accidentally cracked the foundation of a parking garage while lifting a sedan out of a flooded lot. The footage had gone viral. Kael had been praised as a hero. The city council had given him a key to the city. He was one of the good ones, or at least, that’s what the people who could afford to look away from the cracks in the wall said.

Ethan watched Kael’s hand move from the chair to the table. He didn’t touch the woman. He just rested his palm flat on the wood, and the table began to hum. It was a low, mechanical thrum, like a diesel engine idling in the next room. The glass of water in front of the woman started to ripple, the surface trembling with tiny, concentric circles.

Kael leaned in closer. "You’re holding your breath," he said. His voice was quiet now, intimate. "That’s bad for you. You’ll pass out."

The woman didn’t answer. She couldn’t. Her mouth was open, but no sound came out.

Ethan felt a tightness in his chest that had nothing to do with the whiskey. He looked around the bar. The other patrons were staring. Some of them had their phones out, recording. Others had turned their backs, pretending they didn’t see. A few were laughing, a low, nervous chuckle that didn’t reach their eyes.

The bouncers were standing by the door. There were three of them, big men in black shirts, their arms crossed. They weren’t moving. They were watching Kael with the same detached curiosity as the rest of the room. They knew who he was. They knew what he could do. They also knew that if they tried to stop him, the consequences would fall on them, not on him.

Ethan looked at the woman again. She was looking at him.

It was a small, desperate look. Her eyes were wide, her pupils dilated. She was looking for help. She was looking for someone to do something, anything.

Ethan felt the weight of that gaze. It was heavy, physical. It pressed against his ribs, against his lungs. He wanted to stand up. He wanted to walk over there, to put a hand on Kael’s shoulder, to tell him to back off. He wanted to be the person who stepped in, who said, "That’s enough."

But he didn’t.

He knew, with a cold, rational clarity that tasted like ash in his mouth, that if he did, he would die.

Kael was a super. He could lift a car. He could punch through a wall. He could snap Ethan’s neck like a twig. And he wouldn’t even feel it. He wouldn’t even remember it. He would just keep smiling, keep leaning in, keep humming his low, mechanical hum.

The law didn’t protect Ethan. The law was a suggestion to people like Kael. The law was a set of rules that applied to everyone else, to the people who didn’t have the power to break them.

Ethan looked at the woman. She was still looking at him. Her eyes were filling with tears.

He looked away.

He picked up his whiskey and took a sip. The alcohol burned his throat, a sharp, clean pain. He drank the whole glass in one go, his hand steady, his face blank.

Kael laughed. It was a short, barking sound. "She’s cute," he said. "She’s always this pretty when she’s scared."

The woman’s head dropped. Her shoulders slumped. The hope in her eyes had died, replaced by a hollow, empty terror.

Ethan finished his drink and set the glass down on the counter. The clink of the glass against the wood was loud in the sudden silence.

He didn’t look back. He didn’t want to see the look on her face. He didn’t want to see the way Kael would react, the way the room would hold its breath, the way the bouncers would shift their weight.

He just wanted to leave.

He didn’t know for what.

He was alive.

He was free.

The words felt hollow. They felt like a lie.

The shaking didn’t stop.

He didn’t know what was happening behind the door. He didn’t want to know.

He picked himself up, his legs still weak, and walked into the alley.

The darkness swallowed him.

He walked home.

He didn’t look back.

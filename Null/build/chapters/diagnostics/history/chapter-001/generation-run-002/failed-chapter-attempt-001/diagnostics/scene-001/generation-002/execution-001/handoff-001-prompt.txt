The neon sign above the door at The Rusty Anchor buzzed with a low, persistent hum that Ethan felt in his teeth more than he heard it in his ears. He adjusted the strap of his messenger bag, the leather digging into his shoulder in a way that reminded him exactly how ordinary his body was. Inside, the bar smelled of stale beer, lemon polish, and the faint, metallic tang of ozone that clung to the air whenever one of the "High-Tier" celebrities visited. Ethan had learned to recognize that scent, a subtle electrical charge that accompanied the presence of someone who could levitate a car or punch through a reinforced steel door.

Ethan slipped through the heavy glass door, shaking the residual chill of the evening off his jacket. The interior was crowded, a sea of faces lit by the blue glow of televisions mounted high on the walls. The screens were all playing the same thing: a live feed from the City Council’s annual gala, where a super named Titan was currently lifting a three-ton chandelier with one hand to demonstrate the structural integrity of the building. The crowd in the bar cheered, a mix of awe and the resigned acceptance of people who knew that if the chandelier fell, no one in the room could catch it.

Ethan scanned the room with the practiced efficiency of a man who had spent the last six years working in risk assessment for a mid-level logistics firm. He noted the two exits: the main entrance he had just used, and a narrower service door near the kitchen. He counted the bouncers—three of them, all broad-shouldered and wearing earpieces, their postures relaxed but their eyes sharp. Then his gaze drifted to the patrons. Most were normal. They sat with their drinks, laughing, talking, trying to appear as though the world hadn’t fundamentally shifted toward those who could break the laws of physics.

But there were always a few others.

In the corner booth, a woman with hair that glowed with a faint, internal luminescence was sipping a cocktail. Her skin had a faint pearlescent sheen, and she didn’t seem to breathe as much as the others. A man nearby had a shadow that detached itself from his body and hovered over the table, acting as a personal fan. These were the casual markers of the superhuman elite. They moved through the world with a distinct separation, a bubble of power that kept the mundane concerns of the rest of humanity at bay.

Ethan moved toward the bar, his movements unobtrusive. He ordered a whiskey, neat, and took a seat at the end of the counter, closest to the service door. It was a habit born from years of watching news footage of super-caused disasters. In every incident, the ordinary people were the ones caught in the crossfire, the ones whose buildings crumbled, whose cars were crushed, whose lives were interrupted by the whims of gods walking among them. Ethan had no powers. He had no enhanced reflexes, no invulnerable skin, no flight. He had a degree, a modest apartment, and a deep-seated understanding of his own fragility.

He swirled the whiskey in his glass, watching the amber liquid catch the light. The liquid was cold, the ice clinking softly against the glass. It was a simple, tangible sensation. The world outside was full of things that defied logic, but the weight of the glass in his hand was a constant.

A waitress approached, her movements quick and efficient. She was a normal human, Ethan could tell. Her eyes were tired, and her smile was the practiced, hollow kind that people in the service industry wore when they spent all day serving people who could accidentally set their hair on fire.

"Rough day?" she asked, her voice slightly muffled by the noise of the bar.

Ethan offered a small, noncommittal smile. "Depends on how you define 'rough.' I made it to the bar without being crushed by a falling building, so I’d say I’m ahead of the curve."

The waitress let out a short, sharp laugh. "Yeah, that’s the best you can hope for these days. The news says Titan’s doing a tour of the financial district next week. They’re expecting some... collateral damage."

"Collateral damage," Ethan repeated, the phrase tasting like ash in his mouth. It was the new euphemism for destruction. When a super broke a window, it was a property crime. When they leveled a city block, it was collateral damage. The language had shifted to accommodate the reality that power was no longer just a physical attribute, but a social currency that dictated who lived and who died.

"Listen," the waitress said, leaning in slightly. Her voice dropped, though the bar was loud enough that she could have easily been overheard. "Stay away from the back corner. There’s a guy in there. One of the... let’s say, less popular types. He’s been causing a scene. Security is trying to handle it, but you know how that goes."

Ethan’s eyes shifted toward the back of the bar. The service door was right there, but the path to it was blocked by a cluster of tables and a group of people who had stopped talking and were watching something with a tense, paralyzed stillness.

He didn’t want to go there. He wanted to finish his whiskey, pay the tab, and walk home in the cool evening air. He wanted to go to his apartment, make a sandwich, and watch a movie that didn’t involve a person turning a police station into a crater. He wanted to be safe.

But the waitress’s warning had hooked something in his gut. A curiosity, mixed with a low-level irritation at the helplessness he felt. He watched the group in the corner. They were huddled together, their bodies tense, their eyes wide. And in the center of that huddle, a figure stood with an arrogance that seemed to push the air around him.

Ethan didn’t move. He kept his eyes on the scene, his glass halfway to his lips. He waited. He watched the man in the corner shift his weight, and the way the other patrons flinched, instinctively pulling their limbs in to protect their heads. He watched the security guards step forward, their hands raised in a gesture that was meant to be authoritative but looked, in the presence of that specific, crushing kind of power, like a plea.

The man in the corner raised his hand, and the air above him shimmered, distorting the light. The table in front of him, heavy oak and solid steel, began to vibrate, a low, grinding sound that Ethan felt in his chest.

Ethan lowered his glass. He took a sip of the whiskey, the burn of the alcohol a familiar, grounding anchor. He watched the man in the corner, his expression unreadable, his power a tangible, oppressive weight in the room. He watched the fear in the faces of the people around him, the way they looked at the man as if he were a natural disaster, something to be endured rather than confronted.

He was still watching, his gaze fixed on the corner, when the man’s head turned.

Ethan didn’t flinch. He didn’t look away. He simply held his gaze, a steady, unblinking point in the sea of fear, and waited to see what would happen next.

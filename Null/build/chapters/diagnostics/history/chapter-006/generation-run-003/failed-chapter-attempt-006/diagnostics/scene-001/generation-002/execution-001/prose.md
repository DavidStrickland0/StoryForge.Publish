The exit gate was a bottleneck of steel and shouting. Ethan moved through it with the slow, deliberate gait of a man whose legs had forgotten how to function. Around him, the air was thick with the smell of sweat and the low, electric hum of thousands of smartphones recording the same event from slightly different angles. He kept his hands visible at his sides, fingers slightly curled, trying to project an air of bored indifference that his trembling muscles refused to support.

A security guard, a woman with a radio clipped to her shoulder, stepped into his path. Her eyes were red-rimmed, her uniform askew. She didn’t ask for his ID. She didn’t check his ticket. She just looked at him, her gaze sliding over his plain grey jacket and his face, before she stepped aside with a sharp nod. He was no one. He was just another frightened civilian in a sea of them.

Outside, the street was a chaos of emergency vehicles. Ambulances with their lights flashing in a stroboscopic rhythm lined the curb. Police cars had created a cordon, their blue lights washing the pavement in a cold, sterile glow. The crowd that had been pressing against the barriers inside had spilled out onto the sidewalk, a dense wall of bodies murmuring, pointing, filming.

Ethan turned away from the main drag. He needed to get away from the noise, away from the cameras, away from the feeling that every eye in the city was somehow fixed on his back. He ducked into a side street, one that smelled of damp brick and old rain. The roar of the crowd faded into a dull, distant thrum, like blood rushing in his ears.

He walked for ten minutes, his pace quickening as his legs regained their strength. The street narrowed, the buildings closing in until the sky was a narrow strip of grey above his head. He stopped when he saw the park. It was a small, neglected square, overgrown with weeds that pushed through the cracks in the pavement. A single bench sat beneath a gnarled oak tree, its leaves dark and heavy.

He sat down.

The wood was cold and damp through his trousers. He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, head in his hands. The silence here was heavy, but it was a manageable kind of silence. It was the silence of a place where no one was watching.

He could still hear the sirens. They were a constant, high-pitched wail that seemed to drill into his skull. He closed his eyes, trying to block them out, but the memory of Aurora’s face, the way it had turned toward the stage, the way the blood had pooled beneath her, refused to leave his mind.

He opened his eyes.

A few yards away, a young man was standing by the edge of the park, his back to Ethan. He was holding a phone up, filming the street. The screen was bright, casting a pale blue light on his face. He was talking to someone, his voice low and urgent.

Ethan watched him. He didn’t know why. Perhaps it was a need to see something other than his own hands. Perhaps it was a desperate attempt to understand what had just happened, to find a narrative that made sense of the chaos.

The young man lowered his phone. He was looking at the screen, his face a mask of confusion and shock. He tapped it, swiped, then tapped it again. The screen changed, showing a different angle.

Ethan’s breath caught in his throat.

He didn’t move. He didn’t breathe. He just watched the phone screen, a small rectangle of light in the gloom, and waited to see what the world was deciding had happened.

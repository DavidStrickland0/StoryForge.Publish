The street outside was a different kind of chaos. Inside, the noise had been a roar of anticipation, a unified wall of sound. Out here, it was a fracturing, discordant mess of individual voices.

Ethan moved with the deliberate slowness of a man trying to blend into the background, but the background was shifting. The morning rush had halted. People stood on the sidewalks, their phones raised like votive candles, recording the spectacle. The air smelled of ozone, stale coffee, and the sharp, metallic tang of fear.

He kept his head down, his hands deep in his pockets to hide the trembling that had taken residence in his fingers. Every time he passed a group, he felt the prick of eyes on the back of his neck. They were looking for the cause. They were looking for the villain. Or maybe they were just looking for a face to blame, a human face to point at while the gods argued in the sky.

He turned a corner, then another, pushing away from the venue’s glass facade. The crowd thinned as he moved toward the quieter residential streets, but the hum of the event followed him, a low-frequency vibration in the pavement. He could hear the sirens now, distinct and sharp, cutting through the ambient city noise.

He needed to be out of sight. He needed to be out of reach.

He stopped under the awning of a closed bakery, the glass door reflecting his pale, hollowed-out face. For a moment, he just stood there, breathing in the scent of burnt yeast and dust. His heart was still hammering against his ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage. He looked at his hands again. They were shaking. He clenched them into fists, forcing the muscles to engage, trying to ground himself in the physical reality of his own body.

He was safe. He was just a civilian. He had done nothing.

But the feeling of safety was a thin veneer over a chasm of dread. He knew, with a cold certainty that settled in his stomach, that he was not just a witness. He was the variable. He was the anomaly.

Ethan turned away from the reflection and walked toward the small park at the end of the block. It was a modest patch of green, bordered by a low brick wall and a few scattered oak trees. The leaves were still wet from the earlier rain, glistening in the morning light. There was a single bench facing the street, empty and waiting.

He sat down.

The wood was cool through his trousers. He leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees, and watched the street. A few pedestrians passed by, their faces tight with concern. A police car sat at the intersection, its light bar flashing in a slow, rhythmic pulse. Blue, red, blue, red.

One of the people standing on the corner, a man in a business suit, was holding his phone up, showing the screen to a woman in a coat. The woman was leaning in, her face a mask of shock. She pointed at the screen, her finger trembling.

Ethan squinted. He couldn’t see the screen, but he could see the way they were reacting. The way they were leaning in, the way their voices were rising in pitch. They were watching the footage. They were seeing the fall.

He felt a pull in his chest, a physical ache that had nothing to do with his muscles. He wanted to see it. He wanted to know what they were seeing. He wanted to confirm that it had happened, that it was real, that she was still there.

But he also wanted to look away. He wanted to go home, to lock the door, and to pretend that the last hour had been a bad dream.

The man in the suit lowered his phone. He looked around, perhaps checking for other witnesses, perhaps checking for the police. Then he walked away, his shoulders hunched.

Ethan remained on the bench. The street was quiet now, save for the distant wail of the sirens and the rustle of leaves. He was alone. He was safe. And he was terrified.

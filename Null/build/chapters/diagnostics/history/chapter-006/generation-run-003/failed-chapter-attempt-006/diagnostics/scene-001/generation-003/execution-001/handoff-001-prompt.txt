The air outside was cooler, carrying the scent of ozone and wet pavement, but the street was far from empty. The morning rush had not paused; it had detonated. A wall of bodies pressed against the chain-link barriers, faces upturned, phones extended like strange, silver flowers blooming from the chest of the crowd. Ethan kept his head down, his shoulders hunched, moving with a deliberate, shuffling gait that made no sense for a man who had been standing still for the last five minutes. He was afraid that if he ran, his shadow would become distinct, would peel itself off the concrete and point a finger at him. He was also afraid that if he walked too slowly, someone would stop him.

He turned a corner, then another. The immediate roar of the venue faded into a low, omnipresent hum, the sound of a city holding its breath. People on the sidewalk stopped to watch their screens, their faces illuminated by the blue glow of notifications. A delivery driver idled his bike, engine ticking, staring at the venue in the distance with a mix of awe and disbelief. A woman near a coffee kiosk was crying, her hand over her mouth, her phone still out.

Ethan didn’t stop. He couldn’t. His legs felt like they belonged to someone else, heavy and unresponsive. He passed a group of teenagers who were filming the sky, where the faint residue of Aurora’s light was already dissipating into the morning haze. They didn’t see him. They were too busy capturing the ghost of a god.

He needed to disappear. He needed to be somewhere that was not here, not near the stage, not near the crack in the pavement where the concrete had spiderwebbed outward. He walked until the sound of the crowd was replaced by the distant murmur of traffic and the rustle of wind in the trees. He found a bench in a small pocket park, tucked away from the main thoroughfare. The wood was damp from the earlier rain. He sat down, the impact jarring through his spine, and let his hands drop into his lap. They were shaking again.

He looked at a passerby, a man in a business suit who was talking into his phone. The man’s voice was sharp, urgent, directed at someone who was not there.

“...can’t believe it. I was right there. The footage is insane. I’m sending it to you now. She just... stopped. Mid-air. Like a bird hitting a glass wall. It’s not a malfunction, right? It can’t be a malfunction. This is Aurora. She doesn’t have malfunctions.”

The man hung up and looked toward the direction of the venue, his face pale, his eyes wide. He shoved his phone into his pocket and hurried away, his stride long and quick, as if he were trying to outrun the implications of what he had witnessed.

Ethan watched him go, the image of the man’s face burned into his memory. *She just stopped.*

He looked at his own hands. They were clean. They were pale. They were his. And for the first time in his life, he was unsure if that was a comfort or a curse.

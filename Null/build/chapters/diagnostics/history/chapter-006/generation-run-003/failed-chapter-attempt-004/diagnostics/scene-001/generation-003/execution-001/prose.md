The walk home took longer than usual, though Ethan’s pace had been steady. He moved with the detached precision of someone navigating a dream, his eyes fixed on the middle distance as if searching for a detail that wasn’t there. The city had changed while he wasn’t looking, or perhaps he had finally stopped pretending it hadn’t. The streets were quieter now, the morning rush giving way to the midday lull, but the air still tasted of dust and adrenaline.

Every few blocks, a cluster of people stood in the middle of the road, pointing toward the venue. They didn’t cheer anymore. They pointed with a grim, accusatory finger, their voices low and urgent. Ethan kept his head down, his hands buried deep in his pockets, feeling the rough texture of the fabric against his palms. He needed the tactile sensation to remind him that he was solid, that he was here, that he hadn’t just fallen through the world like a stone skipping across water.

He passed a group of teenagers holding up their phones, the screens glowing with the same image he couldn’t stop seeing: Aurora, suspended in mid-air before the stall. The video played on loop, a small, silent loop of grace and failure. Ethan looked away, his stomach churning with a nausea that had nothing to do with hunger. He remembered the sound of the crack, not a loud boom, but a wet, final snap, like a branch breaking under too much weight. He remembered the way the crowd had exhaled, a collective release of breath that seemed to suck the oxygen out of the air around him.

He turned the corner onto his street, the brick buildings rising up to block the sky. The familiar sight of the apartment building’s entrance, the rusted gate, the peeling paint on the sign, offered a strange comfort. It was real. It was mundane. It didn’t require him to be a god or a monster.

The elevator was slow, the cables groaning as they lifted him three floors. The reflection in the polished metal door showed a man who looked older than he did twenty minutes ago. His face was pale, his eyes wide and unblinking. He touched his cheek, expecting to find blood, but found only the cool smoothness of skin. He was whole. He was intact.

The doors slid open with a soft chime. Ethan stepped into the hallway, the smell of stale carpet cleaner and recycled air hitting him like a physical blow. He walked to his door, fumbling for his keys. His hands were steady, but his mind was racing, a chaotic storm of images and sounds that refused to settle.

He unlocked the door and stepped inside, the click of the deadbolt echoing in the silence. The apartment was quiet, the refrigerator humming its low, monotonous tune. Ethan stood in the center of the living room, his back against the door, and let out a breath he didn’t realize he’d been holding.

He was home. He was safe. For now.

He looked at the Lens dock on the table, the black device sitting inert and dark. He hadn’t touched it yet. He hadn’t checked the news, hadn’t seen the reactions, hadn’t seen the speculation. He stood there, listening to the hum of the refrigerator, the tick of the clock, the distant muffled rumble of a truck passing on the street outside.

The world was still turning. People were still waking up. The city was still functioning, oblivious to the fact that one of its symbols had just broken on the pavement.

Ethan took a step forward, his feet bare on the cold floor. He needed to ground himself. He needed to do something, anything, to break the spell of the last hour. He looked at the kitchen, at the empty counter, at the window where the morning light was fading into the gray of an overcast day.

He needed tea.

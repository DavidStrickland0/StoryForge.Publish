The walk home took forty minutes. It should have taken twenty.

Ethan moved with the slow, deliberate caution of a man navigating a minefield, though no one else seemed to notice the danger. The city was waking up, the usual morning rush of commuters, delivery trucks, and street vendors resuming their rhythm as if the sky had not just dropped a god. But the air felt different. It was thinner, charged with a static tension that made the hair on his arms stand up.

People stared. That was the new thing. Not with awe, as they did when Aurora was flying, but with a wary, searching intensity. They looked at him as if he might be the next one to fall, or the one who had pushed her. He kept his head down, his hands buried deep in the pockets of his jacket, trying to make himself small.

At the corner of Fifth and Main, a news van had parked. Two reporters were shouting over each other, microphones in hand, their faces red with the effort of being first. Behind them, a crowd had gathered, not to watch the sky, but to watch the street. Phones were raised, recording the empty pavement where Aurora had landed. The concrete was still dark with the stain of blood and dust.

Ethan turned a corner, his heart hammering against his ribs. He needed to be inside. He needed the four walls of his apartment to close around him, to separate him from the public gaze.

The elevator ride up to the fourth floor was silent. The doors slid open, and he stepped out into the hallway, the smell of stale carpet cleaner and industrial cleaner sharp in his nose. He walked to his door, fumbling for his keys. His hands were shaking so badly that he dropped them twice. The second time, a key clattered against the floor, rolling under the radiator. He froze, listening for any sound from the other apartments. There was nothing. Just the hum of the building’s ventilation system.

He bent down, his joints stiff and aching, and retrieved the key. It felt heavy in his palm, a small, cold metal anchor to reality.

He unlocked the door and stepped inside, locking it behind him with a finality that felt like a prayer. The apartment was quiet. The light from the window was pale and diffuse, casting long shadows across the floor. The coffee maker was still on, the water boiled away long ago, leaving the pot dry and steaming.

Ethan stood in the center of the living room, staring at the spot where the couch usually faced the television. He was alone. He was safe. And he was terrified.

He looked at his hands again. They were clean. They were pale. They were his.

The memory of Aurora’s face, turned away from him, her eyes glazed with pain, replayed itself in his mind with cruel clarity. He had seen the blood. He had seen the crack in the concrete. He had seen the medic’s confusion.

He had been close. He had been so close that he could have reached out and touched her.

And he had walked away.

The realization settled in his stomach, cold and heavy. He hadn’t run because he was guilty. He had walked away because he was afraid. Afraid of what he was. Afraid of what he had done. Or what he hadn’t done.

He sat down on the edge of the bed, the mattress dipping under his weight. He didn’t move. He didn’t breathe. He just waited for the world to stop spinning, for the silence to break, for someone to knock on the door and tell him it was over.

But the silence remained. And in the quiet, the only sound was the ticking of the clock on the counter, counting out the seconds that were now his alone to bear.

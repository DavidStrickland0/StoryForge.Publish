The brick was cold against Ethan’s back, a solid, unyielding reality that anchored him to the earth. He pressed his forehead against it, breathing in short, sharp bursts that tasted of dust and exhaust. The vibration in his teeth had faded, replaced by a dull, throbbing ache in his temples. He closed his eyes, trying to push the image of Aurora falling out of his mind, but it only sharpened. The way her arms had flailed. The way the concrete had cracked. The silence that followed.

He opened his eyes. The street was quiet now, the initial shock having given way to a low-level hum of anxiety. People were moving again, but their steps were heavier, their conversations muffled and hurried. A few of them glanced at him, their eyes wide with a mixture of fear and curiosity, before they looked away, unwilling to acknowledge the civilian standing amidst the wreckage of a god.

Ethan pushed himself off the wall. His legs felt unsteady, as if the ground might shift beneath him again. He adjusted his jacket, the fabric feeling rough and coarse against his skin. He needed to move. He needed to leave this place, this intersection of chaos and awe, before his heart rate slowed enough for the fear to take root.

He began to walk. The route home was familiar, a path of concrete and glass that he had traveled a hundred times before. But today, it felt alien. The buildings loomed taller, the streets wider, the air thinner. Every shadow seemed to hold a weight, every sound a potential threat.

As he passed the venue, the scale of the incident became undeniable. News vans had already pulled up to the curb, their satellite dishes extending into the sky like mechanical flowers. Reporters stood in clusters, their microphones thrust toward the crowd, their voices rising in a cacophony of questions and speculation. Cameras swiveled, capturing every angle, every detail, turning the tragedy into content, into spectacle.

Ethan slowed his pace, drawn by the sheer volume of attention. A reporter, a woman with sharp features and a serious expression, was speaking directly into her camera. Her voice was clear, projected by the microphone, carrying over the murmurs of the crowd.

"We are learning now that Aurora’s powers appear to have failed completely during her descent. Medical personnel are on the scene, but the cause remains unknown. Questions are already being raised about the safety of the event, and whether there were any external factors that could have contributed to this unprecedented event."

*Unprecedented.* The word hung in the air, heavy and final. It was the same word Ethan had used in his head, a private, secret word he had never spoken aloud. *Unprecedented.* Something that had never happened before. Something that shouldn't have been possible.

He looked at the crowd. They were watching the reporters, their phones out, recording, sharing, amplifying. They were witnessing the fall of a hero, and they were consuming it. They were hungry for an explanation, for a villain, for a mistake. They were not looking for a man in a grey henley who had been standing three feet away, whose presence might have been the cause, and who was now walking away, hiding in the anonymity of the ordinary.

Ethan turned away from the news van. He didn't want to be part of this. He didn't want to be part of the story. He wanted to be invisible, unnoticed, safe. He walked faster, his steps becoming a jog, his breath coming in quick, shallow gasps. He kept his head down, his eyes fixed on the pavement, on the cracks, on the solid, unyielding reality of the world.

The street was emptying, the morning rush having resumed its flow. People were moving with purpose, with direction, with a certainty that Ethan no longer possessed. He felt like a ghost, moving through a world that had forgotten him, that had moved on, that had already decided what the story was.

He reached his corner, the familiar intersection where the bakery met the bank. He stopped, leaning against the brick wall again, his hands clasping his knees. He looked at his hands. They were clean. They were pale. They were his.

And for the first time in his life, he was unsure if that was a comfort or a curse.

The ambulance did not wail.

That was the first thing Ethan noticed, a detail that cut through the fog of adrenaline with the sharpness of a blade. He had expected the high-pitched shriek, the urgent, rhythmic pulse that accompanied every emergency he had ever seen on the news or in the distance. He had braced himself for the sound, for the chaos of sirens cutting through the morning air.

Instead, there was only a low, heavy hum. The vehicle pulled away from the curb with a smooth, hydraulic grace, its white shell unmarked by the frantic energy of the scene it had just left behind. It moved with the same deliberate, controlled speed as any other city vehicle, sliding into the flow of traffic that had begun to reassert itself around the blockade.

Ethan watched it go. He stood on the corner, his back still pressed against the cool brick of the building, his hands hanging loosely at his sides. The tremor in his fingers had subsided, replaced by a numbness that felt more permanent, a cold that seemed to have settled into his bones.

The crowd behind him was a different kind of noise.

It was no longer a collective gasp, a shared moment of shock. It had fragmented into a thousand individual voices, a cacophony of speculation, outrage, and morbid curiosity. Phones were raised, not to record the event—that had already happened—but to record the aftermath. To capture the empty space in the pavement where a hero had just broken her body. To catch the faces of the medics, the confused expressions of the onlookers, the blurred motion of the ambulance disappearing around the bend.

A woman in a bright yellow coat stepped forward, her phone extended on a selfie stick, her voice carrying over the din.

"Is this live? Can you see the spot? The concrete is actually cracked. Look at that. Who would do this? Who would let this happen?"

She was speaking to no one and everyone, her eyes wide with a mixture of fear and excitement. Ethan looked at her, really looked at her, and felt a strange detachment. She was part of the machinery of the event, a gear in the machine that turned tragedy into content, into discussion, into something that could be consumed and discarded.

He turned his head away from the crowd. He looked down the street, toward the venue. The massive structure still loomed in the distance, a cathedral of glass and steel, its facade reflecting the pale morning sky. The stage lights were still on, casting long, sharp shadows across the empty plaza. The digital screens that had once displayed Aurora’s face were now cycling through a generic news ticker, red letters scrolling in a steady, indifferent rhythm.

*BREAKING: AURORA DOWN IN PUBLIC. CAUSE UNKNOWN. INVESTIGATION UNDERWAY.*

The words blurred together. Ethan blinked, his eyes aching. He took a step away from the wall, then another. His legs felt heavy, as if the ground had gained weight overnight. He walked toward the crosswalk, his gaze fixed on the pedestrian signal. The light was red. He stopped, waiting for it to change, though he had no intention of crossing. He just needed to stand still, to let the world move around him without participating.

When the light turned green, he did not move. He watched the traffic flow past, a stream of cars and buses, each one carrying its own passengers, its own secrets, its own small dramas. None of them knew what had happened three blocks away. None of them knew that the air had tasted of ozone and fear, that the ground had cracked under the weight of a falling god.

Ethan checked his watch. It was later than he thought. He had been standing there for ten minutes, maybe fifteen. Time had become unreliable, stretching and contracting around the event, refusing to settle into a normal rhythm.

He turned and began the walk home. The street was quiet now, the initial rush of onlookers having dispersed or moved further down the block to get a better angle on the ambulance’s departure. The air was cooler, carrying the scent of wet pavement and exhaust. He walked with his hands in his pockets, his shoulders hunched against the chill, his mind a blank, echoing space.

He did not look back. He did not need to. He knew, with a certainty that felt heavier than the guilt, that the story had already begun to move without him. He was no longer a witness. He was a variable, an unaccounted for element in a equation that was rapidly being solved by others.

He reached the corner of his street. The building stood there, familiar and solid, a box of brick and glass that had kept him safe for years. He looked at the door, then at the street, then at the sky. The sky was clear, blue and empty, offering no explanation, no apology, no comfort.

Ethan pulled his key from his pocket. The metal was cold against his skin. He turned and walked toward the entrance, his steps measured, deliberate, hiding the fact that his heart was still hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird.

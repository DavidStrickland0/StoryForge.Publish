Ethan did not wait for the ambulance.

He had already turned his back on the scene, his body moving with a mechanical precision that felt alien to his own limbs. The air behind him was thick with the scent of antiseptic and the low, persistent hum of the vehicle’s engine, but he did not look. To look would be to acknowledge the reality of the broken woman in the street, to admit that the geometry of the world had shifted in a way he could not reverse.

Instead, he walked.

The street outside the venue was a chaotic patchwork of stopped traffic and frantic movement, but Ethan moved through it with the singular, dull focus of a man following a route he had memorized years ago. He kept his hands in his pockets, his fingers curled around the rough fabric of his jeans, grounding himself in the tactile reality of the material. The crowd behind him had not dispersed; if anything, it had swelled. He could hear the distant roar of it, a wall of sound that seemed to vibrate in his molars, a collective intake of breath that never released.

He passed a group of reporters who had set up a makeshift perimeter, their microphones jutting out like the antennae of strange, metallic insects. One of them, a woman with a blazer that cost more than Ethan’s monthly rent, was speaking into a lens with the brisk authority of someone delivering a weather report on a minor inconvenience.

"...unprecedented incident in the history of the city. Authorities are currently investigating the cause, but initial reports suggest a catastrophic failure of her bio-electric stabilizers. We have reached out to the Aurora Foundation for comment, but as of this hour, they have declined to speak—"

Ethan walked past them without slowing. The words washed over him, familiar and yet meaningless. *Catastrophic failure.* *Bio-electric stabilizers.* Technical terms for a miracle that had stopped working. He felt a strange, detached amusement at the phrase. *Stabilizers.* As if her power were a machine with a fuse box, something that could be tripped, reset, or blown.

The world was reacting with the specific, panicked energy of a society that had built its safety net around a single, unbreakable thread, only to find that thread snapped in the middle of the plaza. He could feel the shift in the air, a subtle tension that replaced the previous awe. People were watching the sky with a new, wary suspicion, as if the blue expanse above them had become a hostile element rather than a canvas for display.

He turned the corner onto his own street. The noise of the venue faded behind him, replaced by the mundane sounds of the neighborhood: a dog barking, the distant screech of a bus tire, the rhythmic thump of a bass line from a passing car. The transition was abrupt, a hard cut from the epic scale of the plaza to the intimate, manageable scale of his own life.

The building stood at the end of the block, familiar and solid. The brick was red, the windows dark and reflective. It was a place of routines, of quiet mornings and solitary evenings, a box he had built for himself to keep the world at a manageable distance. Now, that distance felt less like safety and more like a cage.

Ethan stopped in front of the entrance. His reflection stared back at him from the glass doors, pale and thin, a ghost of the man he had been only an hour ago. He did not move. He stood there, breathing in the cool morning air, feeling the weight of the secret settle into his bones, heavy and cold.

He turned the key in the lock.

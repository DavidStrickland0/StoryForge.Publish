The brick wall was cold through his jacket. Ethan pressed his forehead against it, letting the rough texture ground him. The city noise that had felt like a roar just minutes ago was now a distant, muffled hum, a background static to the ringing in his ears. His hands were still shaking, though he forced his fingers to uncurl, one by one, until his palms lay flat against the pavement.

He checked his phone. The screen was cracked, a spiderweb of fractures radiating from the corner where it had hit his hip when he’d stumbled through the crowd. The display still worked, but the touch sensor was unresponsive on the left side. He navigated with the right thumb, his movements deliberate and slow.

The Lens docked beside the street. He didn’t take it out. He didn’t need directions. He knew this part of the city.

The walk was a blur of motion and sound. People pointed at the barriers. People recorded the empty space where Aurora had landed. People shouted, their voices thin and reedy in the morning air. Ethan kept his head down. He walked faster as he moved away from the venue, his pace shifting from a deliberate stroll to a steady, purposeful walk. He kept his eyes on the pavement, counting the cracks in the concrete as a metronome for his breathing. *One, two, three. One, two, three.*

The street was quiet now that the immediate spectacle had faded. A news van was parked outside a coffee shop, its satellite dish tilted toward the sky. A reporter was setting up a camera, her movements brisk and efficient. She didn’t see him. Or if she did, she didn’t care. He was just another pedestrian, another civilian moving through the city’s business.

Ethan turned the corner onto his street. The buildings here were older, their facades covered in ivy that had started to brown with the change in season. He could hear the hum of the ventilation system from his own building before he could see the entrance. The sound was a comforting, mechanical drone. It meant the world was still turning. It meant the machines were still working.

He reached his door. The key card took two attempts before the reader accepted it. The light turned green. He pushed the door open and stepped inside.

The lobby was empty. The doorman, Ray, was at his desk, his face illuminated by the glow of his tablet. He looked up as Ethan entered. His eyes flicked to the door behind Ethan, then back to Ethan’s face. There was a question in his gaze, a silent inquiry that Ethan didn’t have the energy to answer.

"Morning, Ray," Ethan said. His voice sounded hollow, distant, as if it were coming from the other end of a long tunnel.

Ray nodded, a professional gesture that contained more weight than words could carry. He looked back at his tablet. The transaction was complete. Ethan was back. The world was safe.

Ethan walked to the elevator, his steps heavy on the marble floor. The doors closed, cutting off the view of the lobby. The descent was quick, a drop of three floors that felt longer than it should have. When the doors opened onto his apartment, the air was still. It smelled of lavender detergent and the faint, metallic tang of the ventilation system.

He locked the door behind him. The click of the latch was sharp and final in the quiet corridor. He stood there for a moment, listening to the hum of the refrigerator, the tick of the clock, the distant rumble of a truck passing on the street outside.

It was a list of sounds that used to comfort him, a soundtrack to a life that made sense. Now, every noise seemed to carry the echo of the night before.

He looked at the Lens dock on the table. The device sat there, inert and black, a tiny window to a world he was supposed to navigate. He had left it disconnected. For the first time in months, he had walked home by feel, by the rough texture of the pavement under his shoes and the smell of the rain that had started to fall near the park.

It had been a small victory, a reclamation of the mundane.

But now, the mundane felt like a trap.

The brick wall was cold against Ethan’s lower back. The city hummed around him, a distant, muffled roar that felt disconnected from the frantic beating of his own heart. He stayed there for a long time, counting his breaths until the trembling in his fingers subsided into a dull, persistent ache. The street was quiet now, the onlookers having dispersed or retreated into their vehicles, leaving only the faint scent of exhaust and damp concrete.

Ethan straightened up. His legs felt heavy, leaden, as if the blood had drained from his extremities. He walked, not with the deliberate pace he had used to leave the venue, but with a shuffling, uneven gait. He didn’t look at the phones of the few remaining pedestrians. He didn’t look at the sky. He kept his eyes fixed on the pavement, navigating the few blocks to his building by the tactile memory of the curb cuts and the rhythm of his own footsteps.

The elevator ride was silent. The digital display climbed slowly, each number a small, mechanical confirmation that he was still moving, still alive. When the doors slid open to his floor, the hallway was exactly as it had been that morning: sterile, quiet, and safe.

He unlocked his apartment, the key fumbling in the lock for a second before turning. Inside, the air was still, holding the faint, familiar scent of lavender detergent and old coffee. It was a smell that usually grounded him, but today it felt suffocating, a physical weight pressing against his lungs.

Ethan didn’t turn on the lights. He stood in the dim entryway, his coat still on, his shoes on. He looked at the living room, the desk, the sofa. Everything was in its place. The world outside had broken, had fallen, had bled, but here, inside these four walls, the geometry of the room remained intact.

He walked to the kitchen. The refrigerator hummed, a low, steady vibration that seemed to anchor him to reality. He opened the door, the light spilling out onto his face. Inside, the jar of pickles, the half-onion, the mustard. The mundane items stared back at him, indifferent to the catastrophe that had just unfolded.

He took a glass from the cupboard. His hand was steady now, but the motion felt mechanical. He filled it with water from the tap, the sound loud and rushing in the quiet apartment. He drank it in long, thirsty gulps, the cold water shocking his system, clearing the haze of panic.

He set the glass down on the counter. His hand shook as he touched the rim.

He looked up, not at the window, but at the small, rectangular screen of the Lens dock on the table. It was black, inert, a tiny window to a world he was supposed to navigate. But right now, it was just a black rectangle.

Ethan reached out and touched it. The surface was cool and smooth. He pulled his hand back as if burned.

He needed to know. He needed to see. But he was afraid of what he would see. He was afraid that if he turned it on, the sound of the crowd, the scream of the sirens, the image of the woman in the white suit lying in the dust, would come rushing back into his head.

He left the dock alone.

Instead, he walked to the bathroom and turned on the tap. He washed his hands, scrubbing them with soap until the skin turned red and raw. He dried them with a towel that had been hanging over the back of the chair, the same towel he had used that morning.

He looked at his reflection in the mirror. The man looking back at him was pale, his eyes wide and dark-rimmed. He looked like a ghost.

Ethan turned off the tap. The silence of the apartment rushed back in, filling the space where the water had been. He stood there, listening to the hum of the refrigerator, the tick of the clock, the distant rumble of a truck passing on the street.

He was home. He was safe.

And for the first time in his life, he wasn’t sure if that was a comfort or a curse.

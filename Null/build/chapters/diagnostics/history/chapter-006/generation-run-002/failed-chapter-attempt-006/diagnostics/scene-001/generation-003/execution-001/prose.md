The elevator doors slid open with a soft hydraulic sigh, releasing Ethan into the sterile quiet of the lobby. The air conditioning hummed, a low, constant vibration that felt more solid than the screaming he had left behind. He did not go to his apartment immediately. Instead, he walked to the far wall, where a small mirror was mounted next to the mailboxes.

He stared at his reflection. His hair was disheveled, a few strands sticking to his forehead. His jacket was buttoned to the top, though he had not checked the fasteners in the crowd. His hands were empty. He looked like a man who had been waiting for a bus, not a man who had just watched a god break on the pavement.

He pulled his hands out of his pockets and rubbed his face. The skin was dry, the stubble rough against his palms. He felt the faint, ghostly tremor still moving through his muscles, a residual echo of the adrenaline spike. He pressed his thumb into his own eye socket, hard, until the pain sharpened his focus. The tremor stopped.

Ethan moved to the elevators. The doors closed, sealing him in a small box of polished steel and dim light. The descent took four seconds. When the doors opened onto his floor, the corridor was dark and empty. The fluorescent lights above the mailboxes flickered once before settling into a steady, buzzing glow.

He walked to his door, the keycard in his hand. The plastic was warm from his grip. He held it against the reader. The light turned green. The lock clicked.

The apartment smelled of lavender detergent and cold air. It was the same smell as always, a scent he had associated with safety and routine. Now, it felt like evidence. Proof that he had been here, that he had lived a life where the ceiling did not collapse and the ground did not crack.

He shut the door and leaned his forehead against it. The wood was cool. He stayed there for a long moment, listening to the silence. There was no bass from the speakers, no roar of the crowd. Just the muffled sound of the street outside, distant and muted by the double-glazed windows.

He peeled off his jacket and hung it on the back of the kitchen chair. The fabric was damp with sweat. He looked at the chair, at the way the jacket hung limply, taking on the shape of the man who had worn it. He then went to the sink.

He turned the tap on. The water was cold, running clear over the ceramic basin. He held his hands under the stream. He scrubbed. He used the soap, the small bar of lavender-scented soap that sat in the dish soap holder. He worked the lather into his palms, his fingers, the backs of his hands. He scrubbed the creases of his knuckles, the tips of his fingers, the space between his nails.

He did not know why he was doing it. His hands were clean. They had been in his pockets. They had not touched the pavement, the blood, or the woman. But the sensation of the water and the friction of the soap felt necessary. It was a ritual of purification, a way to wash away something that was not physical.

He washed for a full minute. The water turned slightly cloudy with soap suds. He rinsed, the cold water stinging his skin. He turned off the tap and shook the water from his hands. The droplets fell into the sink, disappearing into the drain.

He dried his hands on the towel hanging from the rack. The towel was soft and white. He looked at his hands again. They were red from the cold, the veins prominent under the skin. They were his hands. They were mortal hands.

He turned away from the sink and walked to the living area. The Lens dock was on the table, the small blue indicator light glowing steadily. It was waiting. It was connected to the internet, to the world, to the news.

Ethan looked at the device. He did not touch it. Not yet. He stood in the center of the room, his hands clasped behind his back, and waited for the silence to fill the space again. The hum of the refrigerator was the only sound. The world was still turning, but for a moment, it felt very far away.

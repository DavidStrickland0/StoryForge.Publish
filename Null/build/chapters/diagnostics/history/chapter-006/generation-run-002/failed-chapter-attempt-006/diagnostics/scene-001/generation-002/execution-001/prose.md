The elevator ride up to the third floor felt shorter than usual, or perhaps Ethan’s perception of time had simply compressed. The doors slid open with that soft, hydraulic sigh, and he stepped into the hallway. The carpet was the same beige, the lighting the same sterile white. Ray, the doorman, was already at his post, a tablet resting on his lap. He looked up. His eyes moved to Ethan’s face, then down to his hands, which were still trembling slightly at his sides.

Ray didn’t say anything. He didn’t ask if Ethan was okay, or if he had seen anything. He just nodded, a small, professional gesture that felt heavier than any question. Ethan nodded back, the motion automatic, and stepped out into the street.

The air outside was still cool, but the vibration in the air was gone. The street was quiet, save for the distant wail of a siren that seemed to be moving away, not toward the venue. A few people lingered on the sidewalk, their phones still raised, recording the empty space where the crowd had been. They looked at Ethan as he passed, but their eyes were glazed, focused on the screen rather than the man walking by. They didn’t know who he was. They didn’t know what he had done.

Ethan walked until the sound of the city faded into a dull hum, until the world felt solid again. He stopped on a corner, leaning against a brick wall, his breath coming in short, shallow gasps. He looked at his hands. They were clean. They were pale. They were his.

And for the first time in his life, he was unsure if that was a comfort or a curse.

He turned and walked toward the building. The walk home was short, a blur of wet pavement and grey brick. When he reached his apartment, he didn’t go in immediately. He stood in the hallway, listening to the silence of the corridor. It was a specific silence, the kind that exists in buildings where everyone is asleep or at work. It was a safe silence.

He unlocked his door and stepped inside, locking it behind him. The click of the latch was sharp and final.

The apartment was exactly as he had left it. The coffee maker was still on the counter, a thin film of dried grounds clinging to the rim of the carafe. The blue mug was still on the shelf, holding two pens. The drawer was still slightly crooked.

Ethan walked to the kitchen. He didn’t turn on the lights. The ambient glow from the streetlamp outside the window was enough to see by. He moved to the sink and turned on the tap. The water ran cold, clear, and steady. He plunged his hands into the stream, the shock of the temperature biting into his skin. He washed them for a long time, scrubbing at the palms, the backs of his hands, the spaces between his fingers. He washed them until the skin was red and raw, until the feeling of the concrete beneath Aurora’s body, the wetness of the blood, the vibration of the fall, was scrubbed away.

He turned off the tap. The water dripped from his fingertips onto the stainless steel sink.

He stood there, staring at his hands. They were his. They were flesh. They were mortal.

He dried his hands on a towel, his movements slow and deliberate. He hung the towel back on the hook, smoothing it out with a straightening motion. Then he looked at the drawer.

It was still crooked.

Ethan pulled it out. It caught halfway, as it always did. He lifted the front, just as Ben had taught him. The runner cleared the damaged section with a soft click. He pushed the drawer closed. It sat flush with the cabinet face.

He pulled it open again. It moved smoothly.

He closed it.

He opened it.

He closed it.

The small, mechanical success of the drawer felt like a victory. It was a small thing, a trivial thing, but it was a thing that worked. It was a thing that he controlled.

He walked to the living room and sat on the edge of the sofa. The cushions creaked under his weight. He looked at the Lens dock on the table. The device sat there, inert and black.

He reached out and touched the dock. It was cool to the touch.

He pulled his hand back.

He didn’t pick it up. He didn’t connect it. He just sat there, in the quiet of his apartment, with the drawer fixed and his hands clean, and waited for the world to stop spinning.

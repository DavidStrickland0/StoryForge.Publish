The brick wall was cold against Ethan’s lower back, the grit of the mortar pressing into his shirt. He didn’t move. He couldn’t move. The corner was quiet now, the distant roar of the crowd reduced to a dull, rhythmic thrumming that seemed to vibrate in his molars. The street was empty, save for a stray cat watching him from atop a parked car with a judgment that felt entirely too sharp.

Ethan looked at his hands again. They were still clean. He turned them over, staring at the palms, the lifelines, the faint dust on his fingertips. He flexed his fingers. They worked. They were just hands. Human hands.

But the thought that had taken root in his chest, the one that had driven him away from the medic’s gaze, refused to let go. *What did that say about him?*

He hadn’t touched her. He hadn’t spoken to her. He had stood three feet away, a spectator, a body in the stream. And yet, as the memory of her face turned toward the stage—away from him, away from the crowd, away from the world that had built her—played back in his mind, the geometry of it felt wrong. It felt like a causal link.

He remembered the bar. Elena, or whatever her name was, had been fine. Then the man had hit him, and the strength had just... The strength had just left him. Like a light switch flipped off.

Was that what had happened here?

The thought was absurd. He was a civilian. He was a nobody. He didn’t have powers. He didn’t have skills. He had a job, a lease, and a drawer that stuck if he didn’t tighten the screw.

But the memory of the woman in the bar, the way she had looked at him with that sudden, bewildered fear when her invulnerability failed, echoed in his head. It was the same fear he saw in the medic’s eyes when he checked Aurora’s pulse.

Ethan closed his eyes. The cool air against his neck felt like a lie. It was too calm. The world outside this corner was in chaos, and he was the only person who might have been connected to the anomaly, and he was leaning against a wall, breathing shallowly, waiting for the world to decide if he was a threat or a victim.

He needed to go home.

The thought arrived with a clarity that cut through the fog. He had to get back to his apartment. He had to lock the door. He had to sit in his chair and not move, not speak, not exist, until the next news cycle passed. If he ran, they would chase. If he explained, they would doubt. If he stayed, they might find him.

But going home was the only option that didn’t involve them.

He pushed off the wall. His legs were still trembling, but the motion steadied him. He straightened his jacket, brushing the dust from his lapels. He looked down the street. The venue was a block away, a dark shape against the morning sky. He turned in the other direction, toward the residential streets, toward the safety of his own four walls.

He walked.

He didn’t run. Running was what guilty people did. He walked with the steady, deliberate pace of a man who had nowhere else to be. The air tasted of ozone and regret.

He passed the park, the bakery, the coffee shop where he bought his Tuesday lattes. The world was waking up, the shops opening, the people emerging. They didn’t know. They didn’t know that the woman who had been a symbol of invincibility was currently lying in a puddle of her own blood, unable to fly, unable to heal, unable to save herself.

And they didn’t know that the man walking past the bakery, looking at the ground, had been standing three feet away when it happened.

Ethan kept walking. The street stretched out before him, empty and indifferent. He was alone. He was safe. And for the first time in his life, he was terrified of the distance between himself and the rest of the world.

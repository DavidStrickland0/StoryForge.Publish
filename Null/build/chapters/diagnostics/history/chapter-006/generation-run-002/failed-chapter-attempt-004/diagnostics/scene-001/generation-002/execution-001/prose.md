The brick wall was cool against Ethan’s lower back. It was a solid, unyielding reality that felt more trustworthy than the air he was breathing. He kept his eyes fixed on his hands, which were still trembling. The tremor had moved from his fingers to his forearms, a persistent vibration that seemed to resonate in his bones. He flexed his left hand, then his right, as if expecting to see a change, a glow, or a sign of the anomaly that had just ruptured the world’s rules. There was nothing. Just pale skin, dirt under the fingernails, and the faint, ghostly imprint of the denim from his pocket.

He tried to remember the last time he had felt this physically present. It was a dangerous exercise. His mind kept drifting back to the stall, the sudden absence of lift, the wet thud that had traveled up through the soles of his shoes. He had been so close. He had been there. The logic of it defied every principle of safety he had ever internalized. If he was the cause, the implication was terrifying. If he was not the cause, he was a witness to a miracle that had gone wrong, and the guilt of his inaction was a heavier weight than the guilt of action.

A car door slammed somewhere down the street. The sound was sharp, mundane, and utterly disconnected from the horror he had just witnessed. He flinched, his head snapping toward the noise. A sedan, a dull grey thing with a dent in the rear bumper, was pulling away from the curb. Its taillights blinked twice before it merged into the flow of traffic.

Ethan watched it go. He felt a surge of irrational envy for the driver. The driver was going home, or to work, or to dinner. The driver had a destination that did not involve the possibility of breaking the world. The driver did not have to wonder if his presence was a weapon.

He turned his attention back to his hands. The trembling had subsided slightly, replaced by a dull ache in his joints. He was tired. It was a deep, marrow-level exhaustion that sleep would not fix. He needed to be somewhere quiet. He needed to be somewhere where no one was watching, where no one was recording, where no one was looking for a culprit.

He straightened up, his knees popping. The air was still cool, but it no longer felt crisp. It felt thin, as if the oxygen had been diluted. He pushed off the wall and began to walk. He did not look at the phones held aloft by the bystanders on the opposite corner. He did not look at the news vans beginning to park near the venue entrance. He walked with his head down, his hands in his pockets, his steps measured and deliberate.

He was going home. He would close the curtains. He would sit in the dark and try to understand how a man who could barely afford a new pair of shoes could also be the reason a god fell. The question was too large for his mind to hold, so he set it aside, leaving it in the hollow space in his chest where the fear lived. He walked until the street behind him was empty, and the world ahead of him was just a long, silent path back to his apartment.

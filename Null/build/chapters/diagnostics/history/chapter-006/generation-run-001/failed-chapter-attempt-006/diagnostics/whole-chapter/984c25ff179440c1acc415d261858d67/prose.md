The air outside the venue was different. It was thinner, colder, and tasted of burnt rubber and panic.

Ethan emerged from the crowd’s throat, that narrow, jagged exit where the press of bodies thinned into the sterile grey of the city street. He expected the chaos to follow him, to drag him back into the maelstrom of screaming and shattered glass. Instead, the street offered a cold, mechanical silence.

Three news vans had already pulled up to the curb, their satellite dishes unfolding like metallic flowers. Technicians in high-visibility vests scurried between vehicles, coiling cables, shouting into radios. The cameras were already spinning, their red lights blinking in the early morning light, capturing the scene from every angle. They were framing the story.

Ethan kept his head down. He moved with the deliberate, slow pace of a man who knows that running looks like guilt, but standing still looks like an accomplice. He walked past a cluster of onlookers who had gathered behind the police tape. They were no longer screaming; they were recording. Their phones were held up like shields, capturing the aftermath for an audience that didn’t exist yet.

He stopped at the corner, leaning against the brick wall of a closed bakery. The vibration in his teeth had faded, replaced by a hollow ache in his chest. He looked at his hands again. They were still clean. They were still pale.

Across the street, the ambulance was being prepped.

It was a large, white vehicle, stark against the dark asphalt. Two paramedics were loading a stretcher. Ethan couldn’t see Aurora clearly, but he could see the shape of her, the way her body was constrained by straps. The medic from earlier, the one with the beard, was checking a monitor on the side of the ambulance. He looked exhausted. His shoulders were slumped, the professional urgency drained out of him, leaving only a weary, human confusion.

Ethan watched as the ambulance’s doors slid shut. The engine roared to life, a deep, guttural sound that vibrated through the pavement. The vehicle lurched forward, tires screeching slightly on the wet road, and then it sped away, disappearing into the flow of traffic.

Ethan stood there for a long time. He watched the empty spot where the ambulance had been. He watched the police tape being tightened. He watched the news crews setting up their lights.

He didn’t know if she was alive. He didn’t know if she was in pain. He didn’t know if she would ever fly again.

He turned and walked home.

The walk took forty minutes. It was a journey through a city that was waking up to a new reality. People on the sidewalk stopped to look at him, then looked away. They saw a man in a grey jacket, unremarkable, tired. They didn’t see the weight he was carrying.

As he walked, the news started. It began as a murmur in the coffee shop he passed, then a louder voice from a radio in a parked car. By the time he reached his building, the story was everywhere.

*“…Aurora, the city’s most celebrated superhero, has fallen from the sky during a public appearance. Witnesses report that her powers failed without warning. She is currently at St. Jude’s Hospital, where doctors are treating her for multiple fractures and internal bleeding…”*

Ethan stopped on the sidewalk. The radio voice was calm, factual, detached. It was the voice of a world that was trying to make sense of the senseless.

*“…Authorities are investigating whether this was an act of sabotage or a malfunction in her suit. The cause remains unknown…”*

Unknown.

The word echoed in his mind. *Unknown.*

He knew the cause. He had been standing there. He had felt the air thicken around her, felt the light die. He had seen the blood. He knew that his presence, his mere existence in that space, had stripped her of her divinity.

But he couldn’t say that. He couldn’t explain it. If he did, they would lock him up. They would study him. They would dissect him.

He unlocked his apartment door and stepped inside. The smell of lavender detergent and old coffee hit him immediately. It was a smell of safety. Of normalcy.

He closed the door and leaned against it, sliding down until he was sitting on the floor. The apartment was quiet. The refrigerator hummed. The clock ticked.

He looked at the Lens dock on the table. It was black. Inert.

He looked at the blue mug on the shelf. It was chipped.

He looked at his hands.

He was safe. He was alone. And he was the most dangerous man in the city.

The ambulance moved through the city like a white shark in dark water.

Aurora lay on the stretcher, strapped down with a precision that felt like a shackle. Her head was in a cervical collar, her arm in a splint. The pain was a white-hot spike in her ribs, a dull throb in her skull. She tried to move her fingers, but they were numb, distant.

She was dead. That was the only logical conclusion. The fall had killed her. The impact had shattered her bones. She was floating in a void of pain, waiting for the final darkness.

But then, the ambulance turned a corner.

The pain didn’t fade. But something else did. The weight. The heaviness in her limbs, the feeling of being made of glass, of being fragile and breakable—it lifted.

It was subtle at first. A tingling in her fingertips. A warmth in her chest.

Then, the air around her thickened.

Aurora’s eyes snapped open. She gasped, a sharp, ragged sound. The paramedic beside her looked up, his face pale.

“Easy,” he said, his voice trembling. “Don’t move.”

Aurora tried to sit up. The straps held her down, but her body responded with a strength that made the metal buckle. She felt the energy flood back into her, a river of gold and white light surging through her veins. Her powers were back.

She could fly. She could heal. She could break the world.

But her ribs still hurt. Her arm was still broken. The blood was still drying on her skin.

She looked at the paramedic, her eyes wide with a terror that had nothing to do with pain.

“Why?” she whispered.

The paramedic didn’t answer. He couldn’t. He was staring at her, at the light that was beginning to glow from her skin, at the way the air around her was warping.

He was afraid.

Aurora closed her eyes. She was alive. She was powerful. And she was broken.

And she didn’t know why.

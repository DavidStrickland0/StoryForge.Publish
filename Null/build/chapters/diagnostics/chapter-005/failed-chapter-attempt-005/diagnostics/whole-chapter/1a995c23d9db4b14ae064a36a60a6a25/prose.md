The silence that followed Aurora’s smile was not empty; it was pressurized. It was the held breath of thousands of people, a collective inhalation that sucked the oxygen out of the air around Ethan. He stood frozen, his hands still buried deep in the pockets of his jacket, his knuckles white against the inner lining. The stage was a monolith of light, and she was the apex of it, a figure of white and gold suspended in the center of the digital void.

Ethan’s heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic, irregular rhythm that seemed to echo the low-frequency hum of the crowd. He waited for the music to swell, for the announcer’s voice to crackle through the speakers, for the spectacle to begin. But the moment stretched, thin and taut as a wire.

Then, she moved.

It wasn’t the graceful, sweeping arc he had seen in the advertisements. It was a dive. A sudden, violent drop from her high-altitude perch. The air pressure changed instantly, a sharp pop in his ears that made his vision blur. A shadow, vast and cold, swept over him.

Ethan didn’t look up. He couldn’t. His body was locked in a primal paralysis, a freeze response so profound that his muscles had simply ceased to function. He felt the displacement of air, a heavy, warm gust that ruffled his hair and pressed against his chest, pushing him back on his heels. The sound was a roar, a tearing of the atmosphere, a sonic boom that hit him in the gut and stole his breath.

He felt her pass.

It was a sensation of absolute wrongness. For a split second, the air around him felt dead. The ambient noise of the crowd vanished, replaced by a ringing silence in his head, a void where sound should have been. And then, the sound returned, but it was different. It was heavier. Thicker.

And then, the scream started.

It didn’t come from the crowd. It came from above. A high-pitched, strangled sound of terror, followed by a crash that shook the concrete beneath his feet.

Ethan turned.

Aurora was falling.

She was not flying. She was plummeting, a blur of white and gold tumbling end over end, her limbs flailing wildly as if she were trying to swim in air that had turned to stone. Her face, visible even from this distance, was a mask of shock, her mouth open in a silent cry.

Time seemed to slow. Ethan watched her trajectory, a straight, unbroken line toward the ground. It was a trajectory that ended in the crowd. It ended *here*.

He tried to move. He tried to look away, to duck, to do anything that might save her or save himself. But his legs were lead. His mind was screaming, but his body was a statue. He watched her hit the barrier.

The impact was not a thud. It was a wet, heavy crack, the sound of a tree snapping under its own weight. The steel and wire barriers, designed to hold back thousands of tons of human weight, buckled and crumpled like paper. Aurora crashed through, her body twisting in the air as she broke through the final line of defense.

She landed in a tangle of bent metal and broken glass, a few feet in front of him.

The crowd erupted. It was a wave of panic, a solid wall of bodies surging forward, screaming, pushing, trampling. People fell over one another, scrambling to get away, or perhaps to get closer, the instinct of the mob overriding the logic of safety.

Ethan stood still.

He stared at the figure lying in the wreckage. Aurora was still. Her suit, the pristine white and gold of the advertisements, was torn and stained. There was a dark spot spreading across her chest, a deep crimson that was rapidly darkening in the morning light. Her arm was bent at an angle that made Ethan’s stomach lurch, the bone clearly visible through the torn fabric of her sleeve.

She was bleeding.

She was *broken*.

Ethan’s brain refused to process the image. He knew, with a cold, clinical certainty, that this was impossible. Supers did not bleed. Supers did not break. They were the apex, the invulnerable, the gods who walked among men. They did not suffer the consequences of gravity or physics.

But she was there. She was human. She was flesh and bone, and she was dying.

A wave of nausea rolled through him, powerful and visceral. He felt the ground beneath his feet tremble with the footsteps of the panicking crowd. Someone pushed him, a hard, clumsy shove, and he stumbled forward, his feet catching on a piece of twisted wire. He fell to his knees, his hands hitting the concrete, and the impact jolted his system, forcing him to look directly at her.

Aurora’s eyes were open. They were wide, dilated, and fixed on the sky. They were not looking at him. They were looking at the distance, at the void, at the place where her power had been.

Her hand twitched. A spasm, a final, futile attempt to reach for something that wasn’t there.

Ethan looked at his own hands. They were shaking, violently, uncontrollably. He looked at the blood on his knees, a smear of red that had come from the shattered glass near her body. He looked at the crowd, a sea of faces turning toward the tragedy, their expressions a mix of horror and fascination.

And then he felt it.

The same feeling he had felt in the bar. The same dead, heavy silence in the air. The same sense of wrongness.

It was coming from him.

The realization hit him with the force of a physical blow. It wasn’t a device. It wasn’t a drug. It wasn’t a glitch in the system.

It was him.

The air around him was a null zone. A field of negation. His presence was the vacuum that had swallowed her power, the void that had dropped her from the sky.

Ethan stared at the blood on his hands. He stared at the broken woman in front of him. He stared at the crowd, at the security guards who were now breaking through the panic, their faces grim, their hands reaching for their weapons.

He wanted to scream. He wanted to run. He wanted to vanish.

But he stayed.

He knelt there in the dust and the blood, his hands trembling, his mind shattering, as the first sirens wailed in the distance, a sound that seemed to come from a world he had just been exiled from. He was the cause. He was the accident. He was the thing that had broken the god.

And he was alone.

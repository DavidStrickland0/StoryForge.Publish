The silence that followed the roar was not empty; it was pressurized. Ethan felt the air in his lungs turn to stone. His hands were still in his pockets, his fingers curled tight against his own thigh, a physical anchor to keep him from doing something stupid, like running.

Aurora moved.

It was a slow, deliberate descent, a feather falling through honey. The crowd’s cheer shifted from a roar to a sustained, high-pitched hum, a sound that vibrated in the marrow of Ethan’s bones. She was no longer a distant point of light. She was a presence, a woman suspended in the air, her white suit catching the morning sun until she looked like she was made of it.

She began to fly.

It wasn’t the aggressive, streaking flight he had seen in the ads. This was a greeting. She drifted over the heads of the front rows, her arms relaxed at her sides, her face turned toward the crowd with that same calm, detached serenity he had seen on the screen. She was close now. Close enough that Ethan could see the individual strands of her hair, the slight flush in her cheeks, the way her chest rose and fell with a rhythmic, effortless grace.

He watched her pass directly overhead.

The air above him shifted. It wasn’t a draft, exactly. It was a change in density, a subtle thinning of the atmosphere that he felt on his skin like a sudden drop in temperature. The hair on his arms stood up, not from cold, but from a static charge that had nothing to do with electricity.

Ethan tried to look away. He wanted to look at the ground, at his shoes, at anything other than the woman floating ten feet above him. He wanted to be invisible. He wanted to be small.

But then the hum changed.

It was a low, discordant note, a vibration that seemed to come from the ground itself, or perhaps from inside Ethan’s own chest. The crowd’s cheer stuttered, a collective intake of breath that sounded like a gasp.

Aurora’s movement stopped.

She did not stop gracefully. She froze. Her arms, which had been relaxed, jerked outward, losing their balance. Her head tilted, her eyes widening, the serenity cracking to reveal something raw and terrified.

For a second, she hung there, defying gravity by sheer force of will.

Then, she dropped.

It wasn’t a fall in the way a bird falls when its wings fail. It was a crash. A sudden, violent release of tension. She plummeted from the sky, a white streak of light turning into a blur of motion.

Ethan didn’t think. He didn’t calculate the distance, or the speed, or the angle of impact. His body simply reacted with the primal, terrified instinct of a prey animal watching a predator strike.

He saw her hit the ground.

It was not a soft landing. It was a hard, final impact. Her body struck the concrete barrier at the edge of the crowd, not the stage, but the barrier separating the ordinary from the extraordinary. The sound was a sickening crunch, a noise that was far too loud, far too human, for a woman who was supposed to be invincible.

The crowd didn’t cheer. They didn’t gasp. They went silent.

A heavy, suffocating silence descended on the venue, broken only by the sound of a woman crying out. It was a short, sharp sound, a yelp of pain that cut through the quiet like a knife.

Ethan stood frozen. His feet were planted on the ground, but he felt like he was standing on ice. His hands were still in his pockets, his fingers trembling so violently that he could feel the fabric of his jacket shudder against his skin.

He looked down.

Aurora was on the ground. She was lying on her side, her white suit torn at the shoulder, her hair spread out around her head like a halo of pale gold. She was bleeding.

The blood was bright red, vivid and shocking against the white fabric. It pooled on the concrete, spreading in a dark, irregular shape. Her arm was bent at an angle that made Ethan’s stomach turn, the bone protruding through the skin with a terrible, mechanical precision.

She was trying to sit up. Her hands, which should have been strong enough to crush steel, were shaking. She pressed them to her chest, her fingers splayed, as if she were trying to hold herself together.

Ethan wanted to move. He wanted to run to her, to check her, to help her. But his legs wouldn’t move. His mind was a blank white space, a void where thought should have been.

He was the cause.

He didn’t know how he knew it. He didn’t have the proof, the evidence, the scientific explanation. But he knew it with the same certainty he knew his own name, his own heartbeat, the fact that the air was cold and the day was bright.

He had been standing there. He had been watching her. He had been *present*.

And she had fallen.

A murmur started in the crowd. It was a low, uncertain sound, a question without an answer. *What happened? Why did she fall?*

Ethan looked at his hands. They were pale, the knuckles white from gripping his own thigh. He looked at the blood on the ground. He looked at Aurora’s face, which was twisted in pain and confusion.

He didn’t understand. He didn’t understand how his presence could have broken her. He didn’t understand how his fear, his anxiety, his very existence in that space could have stripped away her power.

But he knew he was the only one who wasn’t cheering. He was the only one who wasn’t shouting. He was the only one who was still.

A woman next to him, a young girl with a phone in her hand, lowered the device. She looked at Ethan, then at Aurora, then back at Ethan. Her face was pale, her eyes wide with fear.

"Is she..." the girl started, her voice trembling.

Ethan didn’t answer. He couldn’t. His throat was tight, his breath shallow. He felt like he was drowning in air.

Security guards began to move through the crowd, their voices sharp and commanding. "Move! Move! Get back!"

The people around Ethan started to shatter, a wave of bodies pushing outward, away from the center of the chaos. Ethan was pushed, jostled, bumped. He didn’t resist. He let them push him, letting the current carry him back, away from the blood, away from the broken body, away from the woman who had fallen because he was watching.

He stumbled backward, his feet finding a rhythm on the concrete, his hands still in his pockets, still trembling.

He didn’t look back.

He couldn’t.

If he looked back, he would have to see her. He would have to see the blood. He would have to see the way her arm was bent, the way her face was contorted in pain.

He had to keep walking. He had to keep moving. He had to get out of there, into the street, into the world, away from the place where a god had fallen and a man had been left standing in the wreckage.

The crowd parted around him, a sea of faces turning toward the stage, toward the ambulance that was already rushing in, its lights flashing, its sirens wailing.

Ethan walked.

He walked with his head down, his eyes fixed on the ground, on the concrete, on the cracks in the pavement, on the small, insignificant details of a world that had suddenly become terrifyingly, painfully real.

He didn’t know where he was going. He didn’t know how long he would walk. He didn’t know if he would ever stop.

He just knew that he was alone.

And for the first time in his life, the solitude didn’t feel like safety. It felt like a prison.

The beer was warm, a fact that usually warranted a complaint to the bartender, but tonight Ethan didn’t have the energy to make a scene. He sat in the corner booth of The Rusty Anchor, a mid-tier dive that catered to the kind of people who liked to watch the world happen to other people. The neon sign outside buzzed with a low, electrical hum that seemed to vibrate in his teeth, a constant reminder that he was just another face in the crowd, another ordinary human being in a city that had long since decided ordinary was a defect.

He watched the room with the detached curiosity of a man who had learned, through years of subtle social conditioning, that he was merely background noise. To his left, a woman with skin that shimmered like polished chrome laughed at something a man said, the sound metallic and sharp. To his right, a group of college students huddled around a table, their voices low and reverent, discussing the latest heroic feat like a religious text. Ethan took a sip of his beer. It tasted of stale hops and regret. He set the glass down with a careful click, the sound small and insignificant in the din of the bar.

It was at the table three booths down that his attention snagged, not because of anything extraordinary, but because of the sheer, unapologetic weight of it.

The woman sat with her back to the wall, commanding the space with an ease that suggested she had never once been told to move. She was dressed in business attire that cost more than Ethan’s monthly rent, tailored to accentuate a physique that hinted at strength without ever quite confirming it. Her hair was pulled back in a severe bun, not a strand out of place. She was speaking, her voice low but carrying with a clarity that cut through the ambient noise of the bar. She was speaking to the man beside her, a man who looked like he had been designed in a factory to be agreeable.

He was lean, polished, wearing a suit that fit him with the ease of a second skin. He leaned in, his eyes wide, his posture a perfect study in subservience.

"It’s just that the quarterly reports are a disaster," the woman said, her tone not angry, but flatly declarative. "Like explaining quantum mechanics to a golden retriever. I keep having to rewrite the entire strategy deck myself because it’s so... tedious."

The man nodded, his smile a practiced, placating expression. "Your vision is always so much more... precise, Ms. Sterling. I always struggle to keep up with the pace of your intellect. If I could only process the data as efficiently as you do, I wouldn’t need to worry about the details."

The woman tilted her head, her eyes scanning him with a look of mild, condescending amusement. "That’s what you’re paid for, Marcus. To handle the details. I handle the outcomes."

Ethan watched the interaction with a familiar, dull ache in his chest. It was a dynamic he saw everywhere, in different flavors and packaging. Supers didn’t just hold power; they wielded it with a casual indifference that normal people were expected to admire. It wasn’t just about strength or speed. It was about the social architecture of the world, which had been quietly rebuilt to accommodate their superiority. Normal people were the infrastructure, the support staff, the audience. And Marcus, with his eager nodding and hollow compliments, was a perfect example of a man who had decided that his best chance at survival was to be a mirror.

Ethan turned back to his beer. The foam had begun to collapse, the bubbles popping in slow, lazy clusters. He felt a strange, heavy irritation settle in his stomach, a tension in his shoulders that had nothing to do with the bar’s stiff booth and everything to do with the way the woman had dismissed the man’s competence as a natural, inevitable state of being.

She was still talking, her voice rising slightly in volume, her hand gesturing dismissively at the air. "I don’t understand why we even keep the legacy accounts. They’re a drag on the portfolio. Pure liability."

Marcus leaned forward, his voice soft, almost soothing. "You’re right, of course. You always see the bigger picture. I just... I worry about the transition. For the staff."

The woman’s gaze sharpened, a flicker of something cold moving behind her eyes. "Staff. Yes. People who are too slow to adapt. That’s what we do with them, Marcus. We streamline."

Ethan’s hand tightened on the neck of his beer bottle. The glass creaked, a faint, plastic-like sound that was entirely too loud in the sudden quiet that seemed to gather around his table. He looked away, his jaw working against a surge of frustration that felt disproportionate, childish. He was just a guy with a warm beer and a bad back. He had no business caring about the employment practices of a woman who probably had a personal jet and a legal team that could make a government agency look like a charity.

He stood up, the legs of the booth scraping against the floor with a harsh shriek. The sound drew a few glances, including the sharp, assessing look of the woman in the tailored suit. She didn’t look away; she just watched him with the same detached curiosity one might give to a stray dog that had wandered into a luxury lobby.

Ethan ignored her. He grabbed his jacket from the back of the booth, the fabric brushing against his neck, and turned toward the exit. He didn’t look back. He didn’t want to see the satisfaction in her eyes, or the confusion on Marcus’s face. He just wanted to be out of there, out of the smell of stale beer and the heavy, suffocating weight of a world that had decided he was less than.

The air in the bar was thick with the scent of citrus cleaner and expensive perfume, a cloying mixture that stuck to the back of his throat. As he moved toward the door, passing the table with the chrome-skinned woman, he felt a subtle shift in the atmosphere. It was hard to describe, a slight drop in pressure, a momentary stillness in the air that made the hairs on his arms stand up.

The woman at the table stopped talking. She looked up, her eyes fixing on Ethan with a sudden, intense focus.

Marcus, too, looked up, his face falling into an expression of sudden, alert concern.

Ethan’s steps faltered. He didn’t turn back, but his shoulders tensed, his muscles coiling with a sudden, instinctive need to protect himself from a threat he couldn’t name. The air around him felt charged, electric, as if the world itself had held its breath.

He reached for the handle of the door, his hand trembling slightly.

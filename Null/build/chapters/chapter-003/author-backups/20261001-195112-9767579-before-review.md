The key card took two attempts before the reader accepted it. Ethan held it against the panel until the light turned green, pushed through the door, and waited for the deadbolt to engage behind him. Somewhere down the corridor a television was playing loudly enough to carry through a neighbor's door. He could hear an audience laughing, followed by a man's indignant reply and another round of laughter.

He stood there longer than he needed to, with his keys still in his hand.

His apartment smelled faintly of lavender detergent and the coffee grounds he'd left in the machine that morning. A towel hung over the back of the kitchen chair. The strip of light beneath the cupboards illuminated an unpaid utility bill, a screwdriver, and the shopping bag he'd been meaning to fold and put away. He'd left all of it exactly where it was before going to the bar.

Ethan hung up his jacket and eased off his shoes, working one heel against the other. The right sock was damp. He'd stepped into a puddle on the walk home while watching the Lens directions instead of the pavement, which seemed a reasonable conclusion to the evening. He peeled off both socks and carried them to the laundry basket.

In the bathroom he washed his hands, then bent to splash water over his face. When he straightened, the man in the mirror looked tired and mildly annoyed. He checked the front of his grey henley for anything he'd brought home from the bar. Finding nothing, he dried his face and went to see what there was to eat.

The refrigerator held mustard, a jar of pickles, and half an onion in a container too large for it. He'd meant to stop for beer and groceries. The Lens had taken him away from the disturbance by a route that avoided both, and he'd followed it without thinking beyond the next turn.

There was pasta in the cupboard. He put water on to boil and drank a glass from the tap while it heated. The water had the usual chemical aftertaste. He'd once looked into a filter, discovered the cost of the replacement cartridges, and decided he could live with the municipal supply.

Behind the saucepan sat Mara's blue mug.

She'd bought it at a street market during the first summer they were together, back before the supers started showing up. The woman selling pottery had claimed the irregular rim was intentional. Mara had accepted that explanation until she'd tried drinking from it and spilled coffee down the front of a white blouse. Afterward she'd used it to hold pens.

Ethan turned it slightly on the shelf so the chip faced the wall. There were still two pens inside, neither of which worked. He knew because he'd tried both when the utility company sent him a form that somehow required an actual signature.

He ought to throw the pens away. The mug could stay; he liked the color, and there was enough room in the cupboard.

Mara had liked this apartment when he'd first moved in. At the time, having a place where they could spend an evening without negotiating somebody else's kitchen or tolerating somebody else's music had seemed luxurious. She'd brought over a plant, a spare charger, and the conviction that he owned the wrong towels. Gradually there had been more things of hers around, although she'd kept her own place and most of her life in it.

They'd talked about finding somewhere together. A proper bedroom, a table they could leave standing, perhaps a balcony big enough to sit outside on warm evenings. She'd sent him listings while he was at work, usually accompanied by complaints about what landlords considered a second bedroom.

He'd opened every listing. Sometimes he'd worked out the commute or calculated their share of the rent. He'd thought that showed he was taking it seriously.

Mara had eventually asked him to arrange a viewing.

He remembered that conversation more clearly than several of the arguments that followed. They'd been eating takeaway at this counter, and she'd waited until he'd finished explaining why the next month would be a better time to start looking.

“You keep saying you want this,” she'd said. “I'm trying to work out when you're going to do something about it.”

He'd had reasons. His lease still had months to run. Work was uncertain. The neighborhoods they could afford were farther out than either of them wanted. Each reason had seemed sensible when he said it aloud, and he'd managed to produce another whenever she solved the previous one.

She'd stopped sending listings before they stopped seeing each other.

The water began to hiss against the lid. Ethan tipped in the pasta and reduced the heat, watching it settle beneath the surface. He could admit, alone in his kitchen, that the lease had been an excuse. He'd liked having Mara there. He'd also liked knowing when she'd go home, leaving him free to let the dishes wait or spend an entire evening doing something she found incomprehensibly dull. He'd wanted the advantages of a shared life while keeping his own arrangements available whenever they suited him.

At the time he'd called that being careful.

They still exchanged birthday messages. Last year she'd sent him a photograph of a balcony crowded with pots and asked whether he'd managed to kill the plant she'd left behind. He'd sent back a photograph of its replacement, taken from an angle that concealed the label.

She'd recognized it immediately.

Thinking of that made him smile. He hoped the balcony was hers and that she was happy with whoever helped pay for it. He'd resisted checking the other photographs on her profile to find out.

While the pasta cooked, Ethan brought up his messages in the Lens. A promotional offer occupied the top of the list, followed by a work reminder and a delivery notification for something he'd already collected. He dismissed those and searched for Ben.

The conversation opened where he'd left it.

*Your repair finally died. You owe me a beer.*

Below the words was a photograph of the kitchen drawer hanging crookedly from its runners. Six months old. There was no reply.

Ethan glanced at the drawer, then opened it to get a fork. It caught halfway. He lifted the front with his free hand and pulled until the runner cleared the damaged section.

Ben had repaired it during a visit when he'd come over to watch a match. They'd discovered the problem while looking for a bottle opener, and Ben had spent the entire first half on the floor with Ethan's screwdriver and a strip of cardboard cut from a cereal box. He'd refused to wait until morning for the hardware store to open.

“Try that.”

Ethan had pulled the drawer out. It moved smoothly.

“How long's that supposed to last?”

“Depends how you treat it. Show a little respect.”

It had lasted nearly two years. Ben would have considered that vindication, particularly since the repair had cost Ethan most of his cereal.

They'd known each other since Ethan's first job. Ben had been the person who invited him for a drink after a bad shift and actually waited when Ethan said he needed to finish something. Over the years they'd helped each other move, argued about games neither had enough time to play, and developed the comfortable habit of sharing a room without having to fill every silence. Ben could arrive with food and take over the chair without asking whether Ethan was entertaining.

Ethan missed that more than he liked admitting. Arranging to see people had become a matter of checking calendars and finding somewhere everyone could afford. Ben had simply knocked.

Then he'd taken a job outside the city. The pay was better, the accommodation was included, and he'd said he would finally be able to get ahead. For the first few weeks he'd sent photographs: a narrow bed, a battered kettle, the view from a window overlooking a parking area. He'd complained about the food and asked whether Ethan wanted to come out for a weekend once he had a proper place.

The messages had become shorter and less frequent. Ethan had assumed he was busy. He'd been busy himself, and a reply three days late had seemed perfectly reasonable until there stopped being replies at all.

He'd called. He'd tried the older address and asked the two people he still knew from their workplace whether they'd heard anything. Neither had. His questions had led to sympathetic noises and promises to let him know.

For a while he'd searched Ben's name whenever he saw a report about an accident in that part of the country. Eventually he'd stopped doing that every day.

The pasta needed draining. Ethan put the glass down and carried the pot to the sink, taking care with the steam. He left a little cooking water in the bottom, added oil and the last of a packet of dried herbs, and stirred until the pasta stopped sticking together. Half went into a bowl. He put the rest in a container for tomorrow.

Back at the counter, Ben's conversation still occupied the edge of his vision.

Ethan started a message asking whether he was all right. He'd asked that already, twice, in almost the same words. He erased it and tried again.

*Had a shit evening. Could use someone to complain to. Call me when you can.*

He read it once and sent it before he could decide it sounded needy.

There was nothing to watch for afterward. He held the charging dock up to his eye and queued the Lens to eject. The faint tug made him blink; then the display disappeared and the small blue indicator on the dock lit steadily. He set it beside the utility bill and carried his bowl into the living area.

The room was still arranged for daytime, with the desk against the wall and the narrow sofa beneath the window. Ethan sat sideways on the sofa, bowl balanced on one knee, and looked at the blank screen opposite him. He could have put something on. He'd had enough of other people's voices for tonight.

Without the Lens or television, the bar came back to him in pieces. Elena's voice carrying across the room. The confidence with which FireMan had reached for her. David hitting him and getting a result Ethan would have dismissed as impossible if someone had described it yesterday.

He put the bowl on the low table. He'd been frightened enough to leave, and he was still pleased he'd made it home in one piece. Remembering the woman at the table made that pleasure uncomfortable. He wondered whether he ought to give a statement, then tried to imagine what useful information he could supply beyond what everyone in the room had seen.

He didn't know why the man's skills had failed. He'd heard claims about devices and drugs that could do things to supers, usually from people trying to sell something or attract an audience. Perhaps one of those claims had finally turned out to be true. Perhaps the explanation was something he hadn't heard of. He could produce possibilities all night and still know exactly as much as he did now.

According to everything he'd been told, skills were permanent. They attached themselves to particular organs or systems, which explained why some people got useful strength and others got an indestructible part of the body that was unlikely ever to face much danger. You couldn't switch them off to suit yourself. FireMan's sudden vulnerability would presumably be somebody's problem to explain tomorrow.

Like everyone else, Ethan had hoped he'd emerge, or bloom, or whatever people were calling it that week. When the supers first started showing up, he'd paid attention to every unusual ache or brief improvement in his eyesight. He'd once spent an afternoon convinced he could hear through walls before discovering that the neighbors had moved their speakers.

Depending on whose figures you believed, perhaps one person in three had some kind of skill. That included enough useless variations to make the number considerably less impressive. Elastic body hair had been a favorite example for a while. Ethan had wondered how the owner discovered it and hoped the answer involved a comb.

He hadn't identified anything in himself. His body continued to require sleep, food, and occasional persuasion to get out of bed. Mara would have enjoyed pointing out that he'd approached the search for extraordinary ability with roughly the same commitment he'd brought to finding a larger apartment.

He finished eating and washed the bowl, the pot, and the fork. The drawer caught again when he put the fork away. This time he took the screwdriver from beside the dock and crouched to examine the runner. A screw had worked loose near the back; tightening it improved the movement enough that he could close the drawer without lifting it.

He opened and shut it twice to make sure, then returned the screwdriver to its place.

Tomorrow he would buy groceries. He added beer to the paper list beneath the utility bill, along with onions and something that would qualify as a meal without requiring a cupboard search. Then he checked his alarm at the desk and keyed the room for sleep.

The sofa withdrew and the bed unfolded into the cleared space. He waited for the mechanism to finish before moving the low table aside. There was barely enough clearance when he forgot, and he'd already paid once to have a leg replaced.

After brushing his teeth, Ethan changed out of the henley and pulled back the covers. The apartment was cool now that he'd stopped moving. He settled onto his side, drawing the duvet over his shoulder, and watched the light from a passing vehicle travel across the blinds.

He had work in the morning, lunch waiting in the refrigerator, and a drawer that ought to survive another week. He pictured Ben reading the message somewhere, finding a quiet place to call, opening with a complaint about the cost of the beer Ethan thought he owed him.

For once Ethan had given him something to answer.

He rolled over and left the dock where it was.

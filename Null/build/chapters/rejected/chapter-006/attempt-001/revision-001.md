The crowd was a living thing, a dense, breathing organism that swallowed him whole. The air here was thick, recycled and warm, smelling of stale coffee, cheap cologne, and the metallic tang of electricity from the massive speakers lining the perimeter. It pressed against Ethan’s skin, a physical weight that made his shoulders slump further. He moved with the flow, his feet finding a rhythm on the concrete, his hands shoved deep into the pockets of his jacket to keep them from trembling.

To his left, a woman in a hoodie the color of electric blue was holding a phone up with both hands, recording the stage. To his right, a man in a worn leather jacket muttered something under his breath, his eyes fixed on the sky with an intensity that bordered on religious fervor. They were all watching. They were all waiting. The energy was a low-frequency hum that vibrated in Ethan’s teeth, a constant, anxious thrum that had nothing to do with the music and everything to do with the sheer, crushing magnitude of the anticipation.

He stopped when the current of bodies slowed, pushed forward by someone’s elbow, pulled back by someone else’s shoulder. He was standing now, or rather, he was being held in place by the collective mass of the crowd. He looked up.

The stage was a monolith of light and steel, raised high above the heads of the thousands. It was an architectural marvel, a cathedral to human achievement, designed to make the ordinary feel small and the extraordinary feel infinite. And there, suspended in the center of it, was Aurora.

She was a speck, a distant point of white and gold against the darkened sky. She wasn’t moving, not yet. She was just there, a figure of impossible grace, framed by the massive digital screens that flanked the stage. The screens were displaying her face, a close-up that filled the entire side of the venue. Her eyes, wide and bright, looked out over the sea of people with a calm, detached serenity. She smiled, and the crowd erupted. The sound was a physical blow, a wave of noise that hit Ethan in the chest and rattled his ribs.

He felt a strange, hollow ache in his stomach. It was a mix of awe and anxiety, a knot of emotions he couldn’t quite untangle. He wanted to be part of this, to feel the surge of power that seemed to flow from her to the people around him. But he was just a body in the stream, invisible, unremarkable, and utterly alone. He looked down at his hands, pale and trembling in the pocket of his grey henley. He was a civilian. He was a threat level zero. He was nothing.

The hum of the crowd rose again, a crescendo of voices that blurred into a single, deafening roar. Ethan closed his eyes for a second, breathing in the dust and the anticipation, feeling the weight of the moment settle over him like a heavy cloak. He was here. He was present. And for the first time in days, the isolation of his apartment felt like a distant memory, replaced by the overwhelming, terrifying reality of being part of something much larger than himself.

Then, a shift.

It was subtle at first, a ripple in the air that Ethan felt in his gut before he saw it. The low thrum of the crowd’s excitement stuttered, replaced by a sudden, collective hold of breath. The noise didn’t fade; it vanished, sucked out of the air as if the world had hit a mute button.

Ethan opened his eyes.

Aurora had moved. She was descending, a slow, graceful spiral that should have been the highlight of the event. But as she passed directly overhead, the light that had framed her, the force that had held her aloft, flickered. It didn’t fade like a dying bulb; it snapped.

For a heartbeat, she hung in the air, her body angled downward, arms outstretched as if reaching for a current that was no longer there. Then, the physics of the world, indifferent and absolute, took hold.

She fell.

It was a free fall, silent and terrifyingly fast. The crowd did not scream; they gasped. The sound was a sharp, collective hiss, like steam escaping a valve. Ethan’s body reacted before his mind could process the geometry of the event. He turned his head, his gaze tracking the plummeting figure. She was coming down straight toward him.

The impact was a thud. A wet, heavy, bone-deep thud that vibrated through the soles of his shoes and up his legs.

Dust puffed out from the pavement where she had landed. The concrete cracked, spiderwebbing outward in a radial pattern. Aurora lay in the center of the fracture, her body crumpled in a way that defied the invulnerability she had always represented. Her white suit was torn at the shoulder, the fabric darkened by a spreading bloom of red.

Ethan stood frozen. His hands were still in his pockets. His heart had stopped, replaced by a hollow, ringing vacuum in his ears. He looked at the woman on the ground. She was not moving. Her face was turned away from him, toward the stage, and her skin, which had always been the pale, unblemished canvas of a superhero, was now flushed with the pinkish-white pallor of shock. Blood pooled beneath her head, mixing with the dust, creating a slurry that looked obscenely real.

A whisper moved through the crowd. It started as a single voice, a question, a denial. Then it swelled. *Is she dead? Did she fall? Why did she fall?*

Ethan tried to move. His legs refused. He was paralyzed by a sensation colder than fear. It was the sudden, crushing weight of proximity. He was three feet away. He could see the fine details of her injury, the way the blood was beading on her cheek, the way her chest did not rise.

Then, a sound cut through the whispers. A siren. The distant, muffled wail of a city emergency gave way to a high-pitched, urgent shriek that seemed to come from right beside him.

The crowd parted. It happened in a sudden, fluid rush, a path clearing through the dense mass of bodies. Ethan was shoved, jostled, but he did not move. He could not move. He was a statue in a world that had suddenly lost its center of gravity.

Through the gap in the crowd, two figures in high-visibility yellow vests rushed in. They were paramedics, their faces tight with professional urgency. One of them, a man with a thick beard and a red cross on his chest, dropped to his knees beside Aurora.

"Check her pulse," the man shouted, his voice cracking. "Get the trauma kit. Now."

Ethan watched as the medic’s hands hovered over Aurora’s neck. The man’s face changed. The urgency was replaced by a flicker of confusion, a micro-expression of disbelief. He pressed two fingers against her throat. He frowned. He pressed them again, harder.

"She’s breathing," the medic said, his voice tight. "But barely. Get the suction."

The second medic, a woman with a headset, was already scanning the area with a handheld device. She looked up, her eyes widening. "Readings are off the charts, but... they’re flatlining. No bio-electric field. No energy signature. She’s just... flesh."

*Flesh.*

The word hung in the air, heavy and final. It was the same word Ethan had used in his head, a private, secret word he had never spoken aloud. *Flesh.* Mortal. Breakable.

The medic reached for Aurora’s arm, intending to splint it. As his hand closed around her wrist, Aurora’s fingers twitched.

It was a small movement. A spasm. But it was enough.

Aurora’s head turned. Her eyes, half-lidded and glazed with pain, focused on the medic. Her lips moved. No sound came out at first. Then, a rasp, thin and broken.

"Help."

The word was so small, so human, that it seemed to shatter the last remaining illusion of her divinity. She was a woman in pain, lying in a puddle of her own blood, unable to fly, unable to heal, unable to protect herself.

Ethan felt a surge of nausea. Guilt sat in his chest, a sharp, stinging pain, but beneath it lay terror. Pure, unadulterated terror. If she could fall, if she could break, if she could die... then what did that say about him? What did it say about the world?

The medic was already working, applying a pressure bandage to her head, his movements quick and precise. "Stay with me," he said, his voice soothing, professional. "You’re going to be okay. We’ve got you."

Aurora tried to speak again, but this time, a groan escaped her lips. Her eyes rolled back into her head, and she went limp.

The medic looked up, scanning the crowd. His eyes swept over the faces, searching for a culprit, for a threat, for an explanation. For a moment, his gaze lingered on Ethan.

Ethan looked away. He couldn’t meet the man’s eyes. He couldn’t look at the woman on the ground. He pulled his hands out of his pockets, his fingers trembling violently. He pushed forward, forcing his legs to move, stepping over the cracks in the pavement, stepping into the gap left by the receding crowd.

He walked away.

He did not run. Running would draw attention. Running would mean he was guilty. He walked with a steady, deliberate pace, his eyes fixed on the floor, on the concrete, on the cracks that Aurora had made. He moved through the parting sea of bodies, the whispers following him like a trail of dust.

He reached the edge of the crowd. The security barriers were still there, a wall of steel and wire. He paused, looking back. The medics were surrounding Aurora, their yellow vests a bright, ugly spot of color against the grey pavement. The crowd was closing in again, a wall of faces, of phones, of lenses.

Ethan turned and walked toward the exit.

The air outside was cool, carrying the scent of ozone and wet pavement. The street was empty, the morning rush having paused to watch the spectacle. A few people stood on the sidewalk, their phones out, recording the scene. They looked at him as he passed, but they didn’t stop him. They didn’t know who he was. They didn’t know what he had done.

He walked until the sound of the crowd faded, until the vibration in his teeth stopped, until the world felt solid again. He stopped on a corner, leaning against a brick wall, his breath coming in short, shallow gasps.

He looked at his hands. They were clean. They were pale. They were his.

And for the first time in his life, he was unsure if that was a comfort or a curse.

The air above the crowd shifted. It was a subtle change first, a displacement that made the hair on Ethan’s arms stand up before the sound arrived. The low-frequency hum of the crowd’s anticipation spiked into a sharp, collective intake of breath.

Ethan looked up.

Aurora was descending.

She poured herself into the space between the stage and the sea of heads. She was a streak of white and gold, moving with a liquid grace that defied the physics Ethan had spent his entire life learning. To him, flight was a concept, a thing that happened in videos and news reports. To see it now, close enough to feel the downdraft of her movement, was like watching a bird dive into water. There was no sound of thrusters, no mechanical whine. Just the soft whoosh of displaced air and the sheer, impossible weightlessness of her motion.

The crowd surged. People pushed forward, driven by a desperate, magnetic pull. Phone cameras rose, lenses tracking her. The air smelled of sweat, expensive perfume, and the sharp ozone scent of excitement. Ethan felt the vibration in his teeth again, that strange resonance that seemed to come from the ground itself, or perhaps from the collective nervous system of the thousands of people packed around him.

He was a grey henley, a slumped posture, a pair of trembling hands buried deep in his jacket pockets. He was invisible. And yet, as Aurora drifted lower, her gaze sweeping across the crowd, he felt a strange, prickling heat at the base of his neck. It was the feeling of being seen, even though he knew with absolute certainty she was looking at the mass, the energy, the spectacle. She wasn't looking at him. She couldn't be looking at him. He was just another head in the crowd, another unit of consumption for her performance.

She hovered ten feet above the heads of the people in the front row. Her feet dangled, toes pointed, as if she were walking on air. The light from the stage caught the edge of her silhouette, turning her into a figure of pure intent. Ethan held his breath. His heart was hammering against his ribs, a frantic, erratic drumbeat that felt dangerously loud in the sudden quiet of the moment. He wanted to look away. His instinct, the same cautious, self-preservation instinct that had driven him to leave the bar days ago, screamed at him to turn, to retreat, to find a spot against the wall and wait for this to be over. But his feet were planted. He had come here to see this. He had come here to prove that the world still made sense, that the rules were intact, that a man like him could stand in the shadow of a god and simply exist.

Aurora smiled. It was a small, private thing, barely a twitch of the lips, but it reached his eyes. The crowd roared. The sound hit him in the chest, a physical weight of noise.

And then, she dropped.

It was a correction. One moment she was suspended in that perfect, weightless orbit; the next, gravity remembered her name.

There was no warning. No flicker of light, no sound of engines cutting out. She simply ceased to be light. Her body jerked, a violent, unnatural twitch as if an invisible hand had yanked her ankles. Her arms flailed, a desperate, human gesture of panic that looked grotesque on a woman accustomed to the sky.

Ethan’s brain refused to process the image. He saw her fall. He saw the ten feet of air between her and the crowd vanish in a fraction of a second. He saw the people below her, the ones who had been cheering, turn their faces upward in horror. He saw the back of a man’s head, the top of a woman’s ponytail, the glint of a phone screen.

The impact was a dull, wet thud.

It was a sound Ethan had heard in movies, in dreams, in the nightmares he didn’t talk about. It was the sound of a body hitting pavement, of bone meeting concrete. It was a sound that belonged to the world of cars, of accidents, of fragile things breaking. It did not belong to the world of Aurora.

The crowd screamed. It was a different kind of scream than before. It was terror.

Ethan didn’t move. His legs felt heavy, rooted to the spot. His hands were still in his pockets, his fingers digging into the fabric of his jeans, tearing the material. He stared at the spot on the ground where Aurora had been. The dust was settling. A few strands of white hair floated down, drifting through the air like snow.

He looked at the man in front of him. The man was staring at the ground, his mouth open, his eyes wide and unfocused. He didn’t look at Ethan. No one looked at Ethan. They were all looking at the body.

Aurora lay on her side. Her left arm was bent at an angle that made Ethan’s stomach lurch. Her face was pale, her eyes closed. There was blood on her cheek, dark and bright against her skin. She was bleeding.

*She is bleeding.*

The thought echoed in his mind, hollow and empty. Supers didn't bleed. Supers didn't break. Supers were permanent, organ-locked, untouchable. This was a glitch. This was a mistake. This was the bar incident, replayed, but worse. Infinitely worse.

He tried to move. His legs refused to obey. He tried to speak, to say something, to ask if she was okay, to call for help, but his throat was tight, sealed shut. He was frozen, paralyzed by a fear so profound it felt like a physical weight pressing down on his chest. He was the cause. He knew it with a certainty that bypassed logic and reason. He had been here. He had been watching. And she had fallen.

The crowd began to part, a slow, hesitant wave of bodies moving away from the center of the chaos. People were backing into each other, shoving, pushing. The air was thick with panic. Someone was crying. Someone was vomiting. The smell of fear was pungent, metallic, overwhelming.

Ethan stood there, rigid in the storm. He watched the body of the woman he had admired since he was a teenager. He watched the blood spread across the grey concrete. He watched the dust settle. He watched the world break.

He didn't know why he had come here. He didn't know why he had stayed. He didn't know why his presence had turned a god into a victim. He only knew that he was there, and she was broken, and the silence in his head was louder than the screaming crowd.

The first paramedics arrived with the speed of a strike force. They came from the periphery, cutting through the crowd with a practiced, brutal efficiency. They wore white coats and carried red bags. They moved with a purpose that contrasted sharply with the chaos around them.

Ethan watched them approach. He didn't move. He couldn't. He was afraid that if he moved, if he stepped forward, if he spoke, he would shatter whatever fragile reality was holding them together. He was afraid that if he touched her, he would kill her. He was afraid that if he looked away, she would disappear.

The lead paramedic, a woman with sharp eyes and a no-nonsense demeanor, knelt beside Aurora. She checked her pulse. She checked her breathing. She looked at the broken arm. She looked at the blood.

"Get the trauma kit," she said. Her voice was calm, clipped. "We have a major trauma. Bleeding from the face. Possible compound fracture in the left arm. She's unconscious. No response to stimuli."

Another paramedic, a man with a thick beard, held a flashlight to Aurora’s eye. "Pupils are fixed and dilated. She's not healing."

The lead paramedic frowned. "What?"

"She's not healing," the bearded man repeated, his voice tight. "Look at the fracture. It's not knitting. It's just... broken."

The lead paramedic looked up, scanning the crowd. Her eyes were sharp, calculating. She was looking for a threat. She was looking for a weapon. She was looking for the cause.

Ethan felt her gaze. He felt it like a physical touch, a cold hand on the back of his neck. He wanted to run. He wanted to sink into the ground and disappear. He wanted to be anywhere but here, anywhere but in the line of sight of the woman who was trying to save the life of the woman who had just fallen because of him.

"Get her on the board," the lead paramedic said. Her voice was hard now. "We need to move her. Now."

They worked quickly. They stabilized her neck with a collar. They splinted the broken arm. They pressed a cloth against the bleeding on her face. They moved her onto the stretcher with a speed that bordered on violence.

Ethan watched every move. He watched the way the cloth turned red. He watched the way her body twitched, a faint, involuntary spasm as they lifted her. He watched the way her eyes remained closed, her face a mask of pain and unconsciousness.

She was ordinary. For the first time in his life, he was seeing a super as she was. A body. A fragile, broken, bleeding body.

He felt a wave of nausea roll through him. He bent over, his hands gripping his own knees, his head hanging low. He was sick. He was terrified. He was guilty.

The paramedics lifted the stretcher. They moved through the crowd, parting the sea of bodies with ease. The crowd parted for them, a river of fear and awe.

Ethan straightened up. His legs were shaking, but he was standing. He was still there. He was still in the crowd.

He watched the stretcher disappear into the ambulance. He watched the doors close. He watched the ambulance pull away, sirens wailing, lights flashing.

He stood there, alone in the center of the crowd, surrounded by people who were looking at their phones, who were recording, who were talking, who were living. He was the only one who knew the truth. He was the only one who knew that the world had changed. He was the only one who knew that the rules were broken.

He looked at his hands. They were still trembling. He pulled them out of his pockets. They were clean. They were empty. They were ordinary.

He turned and walked away. He didn't look back. He didn't look at the ambulance. He didn't look at the crowd. He just walked, moving through the people, moving through the noise, moving through the world that had just broken, and he didn't know where he was going, or why, or what he was. He just knew that he was here, and he was alone, and the silence in his head was no longer loud. It was empty.

And in that emptiness, he heard the sirens fading into the distance, a sound that sounded like a warning.

The roar of the crowd did not fade; it swelled, a tidal wave of sound that seemed to press against Ethan’s eardrums, forcing his heart to beat in a frantic, syncopated rhythm against his ribs. He stood rooted, his feet planted on the cold concrete, his hands still buried deep in the pockets of his jacket. The air around him was thick with the heat of bodies and the electric hum of anticipation. Above him, suspended in the center of the massive digital screens, Aurora began to move.

It was a slow descent at first, a graceful lowering that defied the gravity which should have been crushing her weight. She drifted downward, her white and gold form a stark contrast against the darkened sky and the steel monolith of the stage. The crowd’s cheers shifted from a sustained roar to a chattering, expectant buzz. People around him reached out to point, their fingers trembling in the air as if they could physically guide her trajectory. Ethan watched, his throat dry, his eyes fixed on the figure that was slowly, impossibly, becoming human.

She was descending toward the center of the venue, directly above the densest cluster of the crowd. Ethan felt a pull, a magnetic gravity that had nothing to do with physics and everything to do with the sheer, overwhelming presence of the woman above him. He wanted to look away. He wanted to step back, to increase the distance, to return to the safe, sterile isolation of his apartment where the world was predictable and the gods were distant. But his legs refused to move. He was anchored by a fear he couldn’t name, a suspicion that if he moved, he would break the spell, or worse, that he was part of the mechanism that was about to fail.

Aurora’s smile faded on the massive screens, replaced by an expression of confusion. Her descent stuttered. For a fraction of a second, she hung motionless in the air, her arms outstretched, her body rigid. The crowd’s cheering faltered, a collective intake of breath that sucked the oxygen from the venue.

Then, the sound changed.

A sharp, high-pitched whine cut through the noise, like a wire snapping under extreme tension. Ethan felt a vibration in the ground beneath his feet, a subtle tremor that traveled up through his socks and into his bones. He looked up. Aurora was falling.

A plummet. Her body, which had been a symbol of effortless grace, became a projectile. She tumbled, her limbs flailing in a desperate, uncoordinated attempt to regain control that she no longer possessed. The distance between her and the pavement shrank in a heartbeat.

Ethan watched her hit the ground.

The impact was silent in the chaos of the screaming crowd, but the visual was violent. She struck the concrete with a force that sent a shockwave of dust and debris into the air. Her body crumpled, a grotesque inversion of the grace she had displayed moments before. There was no spark of energy, no flash of light to cushion the blow. Just the brutal, undeniable reality of a human body hitting a hard surface at terminal velocity.

Ethan’s breath hitched in his throat. He stared at the figure lying in the center of the pavement, surrounded by a rapidly growing circle of terrified, gaping spectators. The crowd was recoiling, a wave of bodies pushing backward, creating a vacuum around the fallen hero. Ethan was on the edge of this vacuum, his feet frozen in place. He could see the blood. It was dark, pooling beneath her head, spreading across the grey concrete in a stark, ugly pattern. Her legs were bent at angles that made his stomach lurch, a clear indication of fractures that no amount of superhuman resilience could ignore.

Panic erupted. A primal, animalistic fear rippled through the assembly. People were screaming, some in shock, others in terror. A few brave or foolish souls began to push forward, trying to reach her, but the crowd pressed them back. The air was thick with the smell of ozone and fear, a metallic tang that coated Ethan’s tongue.

He wanted to move. Every instinct in his body screamed at him to run, to flee the venue, to disappear into the streets and leave this nightmare behind. But he was paralyzed. He was watching the woman he had admired for years, the symbol of a power he would never possess, lying broken and bleeding on the ground.

The realization hit him with the force of the fall itself. The world he had known, the hierarchy he had accepted, had just shattered. The super had failed. The invulnerable had broken. It was a glitch, a failure, a moment of inexplicable chaos that defied every rule he had ever understood.

He looked down at his hands. They were still in his pockets. He pulled them out, his fingers trembling so violently he could barely feel them. They looked normal. Pale, unremarkable, human. But they felt like they had been dipped in ice water, a chill that radiated outward, spreading through his arms and up his neck.

Sirens began to wail in the distance, a rising, urgent sound that cut through the chaos. The first emergency vehicles were already turning the corner, their lights flashing red and blue, painting the venue in a strobing, disorienting glow. Paramedics and security personnel rushed toward the center of the crowd, pushing people aside with brusque, authoritative commands.

"Clear the area!" one shouted, his voice amplified by a megaphone. "Everyone back! Give us space!"

The crowd parted, a sea of faces turning back toward Ethan. He could feel their eyes on him, or perhaps it was just the collective anxiety that had made him part of the perimeter. He didn’t move. He couldn’t. He was watching the paramedics reach Aurora.

One of them knelt beside her, checking for a pulse. His face was grim, his movements efficient but hurried. He looked up at the massive screens, which were still displaying Aurora’s face, frozen in that moment of confusion before the fall. The disconnect between the image on the screen and the reality on the ground was jarring. The screen showed a goddess; the ground showed a victim.

"BP is dropping," the paramedic called out, his voice tight. "Get the trauma kit. Now."

Another responder arrived, carrying a heavy, black case. They opened it, pulling out bandages, splints, and medical equipment. Ethan watched them work, his mind racing. He had seen this before, in the news, in the videos. Supers didn’t bleed. Supers didn’t break. They regenerated, or they were invulnerable, or they simply didn’t care about the physical consequences of their actions. But this woman was bleeding. Her leg was twisted. She was unconscious, her face pale and slack.

She was human.

And she was dying.

Ethan’s chest tightened. He felt a surge of horror so acute it was almost physical, a pain behind his eyes that made him want to curl in on himself. He had been there. He had been watching. The thought was absurd, ridiculous, impossible. And yet, the evidence was right in front of him. The bar incident. The fall. The pattern was clear, even if the mechanism was unknown.

He wanted to speak. He wanted to step forward, to raise his hand, to say, *I was there. I saw it happen.* But the words died in his throat. What would he say? *I don’t know why it happened?* *I don’t know how it happened?* He would be laughed at, or worse, ignored. He would be a witness. He would be a problem.

And he was afraid. Not of the consequences, but of the truth. If he spoke, he would have to explain. And he had no explanation. He was a man who hid in his apartment, who ate pasta, who fixed drawers with screwdrivers. He was not a villain. He was not a hero. He was just a man who had stumbled into a world he couldn’t understand and watched something he couldn’t explain.

The paramedics were struggling. They tried to move her, but her body was heavy, unresponsive. One of them checked her leg, wincing as he touched the fracture. "We need a stretcher," he said. "And I need to know if she’s regenerating. I don’t see it."

"No visible regeneration," the other replied. "She’s just... broken."

The words hung in the air, heavy with implication. *Just broken.* Like a piece of glass. Like a human.

Ethan watched them load her onto the stretcher. The movement was careful, deliberate, but it was clear that they were treating her with the same protocol they would use for any other trauma victim. They were not expecting her to heal. They were not expecting her to fly. They were just trying to save a life.

The crowd was still pressing in, their faces a mix of horror and fascination. Phones were out, recording, capturing the moment. The image of the fallen hero, the blood on the concrete, the frantic efforts of the paramedics. It was a scene that would be replayed a thousand times, analyzed, dissected, debated. And at the center of it all, invisible, unremarkable, was Ethan Vale.

He looked down at his hands again. They were still trembling. He clenched them into fists, forcing them to stop, forcing himself to be still. He was part of this. He was part of the accident. And as the ambulance doors closed, as the sirens wailed and the vehicle sped away, carrying the broken hero with it, Ethan realized that his life had just changed.

He was no longer just a man in the crowd.

He stood there, in the fading light of the venue, the smell of blood and ozone still clinging to the air. The crowd was beginning to disperse, the initial shock giving way to a dull, buzzing anxiety. People were talking, pointing, speculating. But Ethan was silent. He was alone in his own head, trapped in the moment of the fall, the moment of the break, the moment he had become something he didn’t understand.

He turned and walked away, his steps slow and deliberate, his hands still trembling in his pockets. He didn’t look back. He didn’t need to. He knew where he was going. He was going home. To his apartment. To the silence. To the box he had built for himself.

But as he walked, he could feel the weight of the secret settling on his shoulders, a burden heavier than any super’s power. He had broken a god. And now, he had to live with the knowledge that he was the one who had done it.

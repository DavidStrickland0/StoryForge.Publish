The heavy oak door hissed shut behind Ethan, sealing the bar’s low hum and the smell of spilled wine back inside. Outside, the night air was cool and dry, a sharp contrast to the stifling heat of the room. Ethan paused on the sidewalk, his hand still gripping the lapels of his jacket, his breath coming in short, controlled bursts. He had made it out. That was the important part. He was free.

He didn't look back at the window. He didn't need to. He knew what was happening in there. He knew how these things ended. He simply walked, his steps quickening until they became a brisk pace, then a run, merging into the stream of pedestrians who had the good sense to keep to the center of the pavement. The city lights blurred into streaks of neon and gold around him, the only thing separating him from the memory of the super’s thumb digging into Elena’s jaw.

The adrenaline began to fade, replaced by a creeping, cold dread that settled in his stomach. He had seen the super’s hands. He had seen the way the man had moved, effortless and terrifying. And he had seen the moment the man’s grip faltered, the subtle shift in his posture that suggested something had gone wrong. Ethan had assumed it was a trick, a momentary lapse in concentration, or perhaps a reaction to the woman’s struggle. He had not considered the alternative. He had not considered that the man might have been affected by something else entirely.

Elena remained seated, her hand still trembling around the stem of her wine glass. The liquid inside sloshed gently, a small, mocking ripple. She stared at the man’s receding figure, at the way his broad shoulders filled the frame of the open door, until the darkness swallowed him. Only when the last trace of his silhouette had vanished into the night did she allow her fingers to unclench.

She looked down at her hand. The tremor had stopped, replaced by a dull, throbbing ache in her knuckles. She flexed her fingers slowly, watching the joints move, testing the reality of her own limbs. Her head throbbed, a deep, rhythmic pounding behind her eyes that made the neon signs outside seem to swim. A faint, hot sting burned along her scalp where the super’s grip had been, and her neck felt stiff, locked in a position it hadn’t been in for hours. She was intact. She was breathing. But the pain was a constant, low-level hum in her bones, a reminder of the force that had just been applied to her body.

Across the table, David was still standing. He looked as though he had been struck by a physical blow, his face pale and slick with sweat. His eyes were wide, fixed on the closed door, his mouth slightly open as if he were trying to form a word that didn’t exist. He looked like a man who had just witnessed a ghost.

Elena turned her gaze to him. Her expression was hard, her eyes sharp and assessing. She looked dangerous.

"David," she said. Her voice was low, steady, and utterly devoid of the warmth that had been present in her conversation with the super moments before.

David blinked, his gaze snapping to her. The sound of her name seemed to snap him back to the present, to the reality of the table, the glass, and the woman who sat across from it. He straightened his posture, his hands clenching and unclenching at his sides.

"Ms. Vance," he whispered, his voice rough.

"Sit down," she commanded.

David hesitated for a fraction of a second, his eyes darting to the closed door again, then back to her. He lowered himself into his chair, the leather creaking softly under his weight. He sat rigid, his hands resting on the table, his knuckles white.

Elena reached for her wine glass. She took a slow sip, the cool liquid sliding down her throat, grounding her. She set the glass down with a precise click against the wooden surface.

"Did you see that?" she asked. Her tone was conversational, almost casual, but her eyes were locked on David’s face, searching for the crack in his composure.

David swallowed hard. "I... I saw him leave, Ms. Vance."

"No," she said, a thin smile playing on her lips. "You saw what happened. You saw how he looked when he realized he couldn't break me."

David’s jaw tightened. He looked away, his eyes fixing on a point on the table somewhere between them. "It was... unexpected."

"Unexpected," she repeated, tasting the word. "Is that what you call it? A super, the kind who can crush a car with his bare hands, suddenly deciding to walk away because a woman in a cocktail dress told him to stop?"

She leaned forward, her elbows on the table, her chin resting on her interlaced fingers. "He didn't walk away because I was brave, David. He walked away because something was wrong. Something was very, very wrong."

David’s eyes flickered to her, a flicker of fear in his dark pupils. "Ms. Vance, I don't understand."

"You don't need to understand," she said, her voice dropping to a whisper that carried more weight than a shout. "You need to remember. And you need to be very, very careful about what you tell anyone about tonight. About me. About this table. About the fact that I was sitting here, drinking my wine, and talking to a god who suddenly decided to become a man."

She sat back, her posture perfect, her face a mask of serene confidence. But her eyes, dark and intelligent, remained fixed on David, demanding his compliance, demanding his silence.

David nodded, his throat working as he swallowed. "Yes, Ms. Vance. I will be careful."

"Good," she said. She picked up her wine glass again, raising it in a small, silent toast to the empty air. "Now. Let’s talk about what we are going to do about the fact that I am still sitting here, and he is still out there. And that I am not entirely sure that he will not come back."

David looked at her, his face a mixture of relief and dread. He had survived the encounter. He had kept his job. But he had also seen the edge of a blade he could not see, a power he could not name. And he knew, with a cold certainty that settled in his stomach, that Elena Vance was the only thing standing between him and the unknown.

He reached for the check, his hand steady now, his face a mask of professional indifference. "I’ll take care of the bill, Ms. Vance. We can discuss the next steps in your office tomorrow."

Elena studied him for a long moment, her eyes unreadable. Then she gave a small, sharp nod.

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

The silence in the bar was heavy, punctuated only by the low hum of the refrigerator behind the counter and the faint clink of ice settling in a glass. At the far end of the room, near the restrooms, the large man stood perfectly still. His shoulders were hunched, and his hands were tucked deep into the pockets of his jacket, fingers twitching as if trying to find an object that wasn’t there. He kept looking down at his palms, flexing them, then releasing them into fists. The motion was repetitive, mechanical.

He had tried to lift a chair, just to test the weight, just to feel the familiar resistance of steel yielding to his grip. Instead, the leg had slipped, and the chair had crashed to the floor, splintering under his own clumsy weight. The sound had been sharp and final, a small, ordinary failure that echoed in his chest more loudly than any shout he could have made. Now, the patrons of The Gilded Cage moved around him like water around a stone. No one looked at him directly. No one spoke to him. The bartender had wiped down the counter with aggressive efficiency, avoiding eye contact, and the few couples at the tables had shifted their conversation to a volume that was almost a whisper, their eyes fixed on their drinks as if the liquid held answers to the sudden, inexplicable shift in the room’s atmosphere.

The man ran a hand over his face, feeling the coarse grit of stubble against his palm. The sensation was mundane, irritating, and utterly real. He had expected the world to continue, expected the strength to be a constant, a bedrock he could stand on even when he was tired or bored or angry. Instead, he felt hollowed out, a vessel with the contents suddenly drained. He opened his mouth, perhaps to call out, perhaps to demand an explanation from the universe, but no sound came. The air in the room felt thinner, less supportive, as if the very atmosphere had withdrawn its recognition of his presence.

He took a step toward the door, his movements stiff and deliberate. The floorboards creaked beneath his weight, a sound that felt exaggeratedly loud in the quiet. He paused, looking back toward the table where the executive and her companion were sitting. They were still, both of them, their faces turned slightly away, as if the air between their table and his position was contaminated. He couldn't read their expressions, couldn't tell if they were afraid, confused, or simply done with him. The uncertainty was worse than the fear. He turned away, his back to them, and walked toward the exit. His steps were heavy, each one a small concession to the gravity that had suddenly reclaimed its hold on him.

The door to the bar swung open with a squeak, and the cool night air rushed in, carrying the smell of rain and distant traffic. He stepped out into the street, the neon lights of the sign reflecting off the wet pavement, blurring the world into streaks of red and blue. He stood there for a moment, breathing in the cold, trying to find the strength to keep walking, but it felt like a memory, something he had once had but had now lost.

He walked down the block, his pace slow and deliberate. The street was empty, the few remaining cars passing by with a hiss of tires on wet asphalt. He stopped under the flickering light of a streetlamp, lifting his hand to examine it. The skin looked normal. The bones felt normal. He clenched his fist, feeling the tendons pull tight against the muscle. It was solid. It was real.

He waited.

Ten seconds passed. Then twenty.

A sudden, sharp heat bloomed in his chest, spreading outward through his arms and legs like liquid fire. It was a familiar sensation, a rush of energy that hummed in his marrow. He flexed his fingers, and the air around his hand shimmered with a faint, heat-haze distortion. The weight in his limbs vanished, replaced by a coiled, explosive tension. He was back. The strength was back. The invulnerability was back.

He looked at his hand, then at the dark entrance of The Gilded Cage. The mystery of the last hour remained, a cold knot in his stomach that the returning power did not dissolve. He had lost it. He had found it. The cause was unknown, the mechanism invisible. But the power was his again, and the world had returned to its proper, dangerous order.

He turned and walked away, his stride long and confident, the pavement cracking slightly under the force of his steps as he disappeared into the night.

Ethan was still walking when the realization hit him. He had passed the super. He had been close enough to touch him. And the super had lost his power.

He stopped in the middle of the sidewalk, his heart hammering against his ribs. The pedestrians around him flowed past, oblivious to the young man standing frozen in the neon glow. He looked back toward the bar, then at his own hands. They were trembling, but not from fear. They were trembling from a sudden, electric surge of something else.

He had caused it.

The thought was absurd, impossible, and yet it felt undeniably true. He had no power. He had no device. He had no explanation. But the timing was too precise, the coincidence too perfect. He had walked past the super, and the super had fallen.

Ethan turned and walked back toward the bar. He had to know. He had to see. He had to understand what had just happened.

The door to The Gilded Cage opened, and he stepped inside. The air was thick with tension, the patrons still wary, the bartender still avoiding eye contact. But the table where Elena and David had been sitting was empty.

The chair was still on the floor, splintered and broken. The wine glass was still there, a single drop of red liquid clinging to its rim. And the super was gone.

Ethan stood in the center of the room, his mind racing. He had done this. He had changed the outcome. He had saved her.

And he had no idea how.

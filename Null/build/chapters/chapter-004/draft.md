The morning light in his apartment was the color of weak tea.

Ethan stood in the center of the living room, still in his grey henley and socks, staring at the ceiling. The silence of the small space felt heavy, pressing against his eardrums. He could hear the hum of the refrigerator, the tick of the clock on the counter, and the distant, muffled rumble of a truck passing on the street outside. It was a list of sounds that used to comfort him, a soundtrack to a life that made sense. Now, every noise seemed to carry the echo of the night before.

He looked at the Lens dock on the table. The device sat there, inert and black, a tiny window to a world he was supposed to navigate. He had left it disconnected. For the first time in months, he had walked home by feel, by the rough texture of the pavement under his shoes and the smell of the rain that had started to fall near the park. It had been a small victory, a reclamation of the mundane.

But now, the mundane felt like a trap.

He walked to the window and pulled the curtain back. The street was waking up. People were walking their dogs, checking their phones, buying coffee from the kiosk on the corner. Everything was moving with the steady, predictable rhythm of a city that had decided to ignore the fact that its laws were often suggestions.

Ethan’s hand moved to his pocket, a reflex he couldn’t shake. He didn’t have the Lens. He didn’t need it. He knew where he was going.

He had been going to this event for weeks. Aurora’s public appearance. The kind of thing that used to make his heart race with a mixture of awe and a quiet, lonely longing. He had admired her since he was a teenager, watching her fly across the sky in the ads, a symbol of a power he would never possess. Now, she was just a woman in a room full of other people who had the same power.

He looked at his reflection in the glass. He looked tired. His eyes were shadowed, his posture slumped. He looked like a man who had seen something he couldn’t explain and had decided, for the sake of his sanity, to pretend it hadn’t happened.

He turned away from the window.

He could stay here. He could stay in the apartment, close the curtains, and let the day pass him by. He could tell himself that the bar incident was a fluke, a bad day for a super, a temporary glitch in the system. He could tell himself that the world was safe, that the rules still applied, that he was just a civilian trying to get through his shift.

But the thought made his stomach turn.

If he stayed here, he was admitting that the world was broken. He was admitting that the only thing that had saved the woman in the bar—Elena, though he never learned her name—was a random, unexplainable failure in a man who should have been invincible.

He didn’t want to believe that.

He wanted to believe that the world was still the one he had grown up in, where strength was a commodity, where status was a hierarchy, and where he, Ethan Vale, was safely on the bottom, observing from a distance that kept him alive.

He needed to see.

He needed to stand in a room full of people who had power, who had status, who had the ability to crush him with a thought, and see if any of it felt different. He needed to see if the air tasted the same, if the ground felt solid under his feet, if the world was as safe as he had been taught to believe.

He walked to the closet and pulled out his jacket. He hung it over his arm. He didn’t need the Lens. He didn’t need the map. He just needed to put his feet on the street and walk toward the venue.

He looked back at the apartment one last time. It was quiet. It was safe. It was a box he had built for himself, a place where he could hide from the fact that he was just a person in a world of gods.

He didn’t want to hide anymore.

He locked the door behind him, the click of the latch echoing in the hallway, and stepped out into the morning.

The hallway smelled of stale carpet cleaner and the faint, metallic tang of the ventilation system. Ethan clicked the deadbolt, the sound sharp and final in the quiet corridor. He paused, listening for the specific creak of his neighbor’s floorboards or the muffled thump of bass from the unit above. He heard nothing. Just the hum of the building’s life support, keeping the air recycled and the world outside at a manageable distance.

He stepped into the elevator, the doors sliding shut with a soft hydraulic sigh. The descent was quick, a drop of three floors that felt longer than it should have. When the doors opened onto the lobby, the morning light was already pouring through the glass facade, washing the sterile white marble floors in a pale glow. The doorman, a broad-shouldered man with a badge that read *Security: Level 4*, looked up from his tablet.

"Morning, Mr. Vale."

"Morning, Ray."

Ray’s eyes flicked to Ethan’s face, then down to his hands, empty and relaxed. There was a micro-expression there, a slight tightening around the eyes that Ethan had learned to read over the years. It was the look of someone calculating the threat level of a civilian who was about to walk out into a world that didn’t care about his safety. Ray didn’t say anything. He just nodded, a professional gesture that contained more weight than words could carry.

Ethan stepped out onto the sidewalk. The air was cool, carrying the scent of ozone and wet pavement from the night before’s rain. The street was already bustling with the early morning rush, a river of people flowing in the same direction. He kept his head down, moving with the current, his hand brushing the rough fabric of his jacket sleeve.

The venue was three blocks away. It was a massive structure of glass and steel, designed to look less like a stadium and more like a cathedral to human achievement. The crowd had already begun to form, a dense wall of bodies pressing against the security barriers. Ethan weaved through the throng, feeling the heat and humidity of the crowd against his skin. He could feel the vibrations of the bass from the sound system, a low, thrumming pulse that seemed to come from the ground itself.

He passed a group of teenagers, their phones out, recording the sky. Above them, a streak of blue and gold cut through the clouds, leaving a trail of condensation that evaporated in the morning sun. The crowd erupted in a cheer, a sound so loud it felt physical, a wall of noise that pushed against his eardrums. Ethan watched the figure recede into the distance, a tiny point of light against the vastness of the sky.

He felt a pang of something he couldn’t name. Envy, perhaps. Or just a deep, resonant sadness. He was watching a god from the ground, and the distance between them felt insurmountable. He adjusted his jacket, pulling the collar up against the chill, and continued walking. The barriers were thick, reinforced with steel and wire, designed to keep the ordinary from the extraordinary. Ethan paused, looking at the crowd pressing against the other side. They were cheering, waving, screaming. They were alive with a kind of energy that he felt excluded from.

He took a breath, the air tasting of dust and anticipation. Then he stepped forward, joining the flow of people moving toward the entrance. The crowd parted around him, a sea of faces turning toward the stage, toward the sky. Ethan was just another body in the stream, invisible, unremarkable, and utterly alone.

The crowd was a living thing, a dense, breathing organism that swallowed him whole. The air here was thick, recycled and warm, smelling of stale coffee, cheap cologne, and the metallic tang of electricity from the massive speakers lining the perimeter. It pressed against Ethan’s skin, a physical weight that made his shoulders slump further. He moved with the flow, his feet finding a rhythm on the concrete, his hands shoved deep into the pockets of his jacket to keep them from trembling.

To his left, a woman in a hoodie the color of electric blue was holding a phone up with both hands, recording the stage. To his right, a man in a worn leather jacket muttered something under his breath, his eyes fixed on the sky with an intensity that bordered on religious fervor. They were all watching. They were all waiting. The energy was a low-frequency hum that vibrated in Ethan’s teeth, a constant, anxious thrum that had nothing to do with the music and everything to do with the sheer, crushing magnitude of the anticipation.

He stopped when the current of bodies slowed, pushed forward by someone’s elbow, pulled back by someone else’s shoulder. He was standing now, or rather, he was being held in place by the collective mass of the crowd. He looked up.

The stage was a monolith of light and steel, raised high above the heads of the thousands. It was an architectural marvel, a cathedral to human achievement, designed to make the ordinary feel small and the extraordinary feel infinite. And there, suspended in the center of it, was Aurora.

She was a speck, a distant point of white and gold against the darkened sky. She wasn’t moving, not yet. She was just there, a figure of impossible grace, framed by the massive digital screens that flanked the stage. The screens were displaying her face, a close-up that filled the entire side of the venue. Her eyes, wide and bright, looked out over the sea of people with a calm, detached serenity. She smiled, and the crowd erupted. The sound was a physical blow, a wave of noise that hit Ethan in the chest and rattled his ribs.

He felt a strange, hollow ache in his stomach. It was a mix of awe and anxiety, a knot of emotions he couldn’t quite untangle. He wanted to be part of this, to feel the surge of power that seemed to flow from her to the people around him. But he was just a body in the stream, invisible, unremarkable, and utterly alone. He looked down at his hands, pale and trembling in the pocket of his grey henley. He was a civilian. He was a threat level zero. He was nothing.

The hum of the crowd rose again, a crescendo of voices that blurred into a single, deafening roar. Ethan closed his eyes for a second, breathing in the dust and the anticipation, feeling the weight of the moment settle over him like a heavy cloak. He was here. He was present. And for the first time in days, the isolation of his apartment felt like a distant memory, replaced by the overwhelming, terrifying reality of being part of something much larger than himself.

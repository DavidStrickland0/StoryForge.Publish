Ethan kept walking. The pavement was uneven, a minor nuisance that demanded his full attention to avoid a stumble. His hands had stopped shaking, the adrenaline slowly bleeding out of his muscles, leaving behind a hollow, buzzing emptiness in his chest. He passed a row of parked cars, their paint reflecting the neon glow of the bar’s sign. The Gilded Cage. A name that seemed appropriate for the kind of people who frequented it.

He didn’t look back immediately. Instead, he focused on the rhythm of his steps, forcing his mind to disengage from the visceral memory of the table, the stiletto, the sudden, violent shift in the air. His heart rate was still elevated, a frantic drumbeat that didn’t match the calm of the empty street. He needed to breathe. He inhaled through his nose, counting to four, then exhaled through his mouth, counting to seven. The ritual was a trick he’d learned years ago, a way to ground himself when the world felt like it was tilting off its axis. It worked, or at least, it kept the panic from taking over.

A few yards down, a puddle from a recent rain sat in a pothole. He stepped around it, his sneakers squeaking on the wet concrete. The street was quiet, save for the distant wail of a siren and the low hum of traffic on the main avenue. No one had seen him. No one had called the police. The bar incident had become a private thing, a secret shared only by the people inside that window.

Ethan slowed his pace. He wasn’t running anymore. He was just a man walking home on a Tuesday night, trying to ignore the fact that he had just witnessed a crime that, in any other context, would have resulted in a body count. Or at least, a very broken woman. But here, in the city, things were different. The laws existed, but they were suggestions for the powerful, and the powerful were everywhere. Supers walked the streets, flew over the crowds, and occasionally, if they felt like it, broke the rules without consequence.

He stopped in front of a laundromat that was closed for the night. The glass door was fogged up, but he could see the silhouette of the man inside, still standing near the table. The super was motionless, his head tilted to one side as if listening to a sound only he could hear. His posture was stiff, his shoulders hunched, a physical manifestation of his confusion. He had lost his strength, his invulnerability, and with them, the certainty that made him untouchable. He was just a man now, standing in a bar, dealing with the sudden, terrifying reality of his own fragility.

Ethan felt a strange, cold knot in his stomach. He had expected fear, anger, or perhaps a desperate attempt to regain control. Instead, he saw only bewilderment. The super was trying to understand what had happened, just as Ethan was trying to understand why he had been the one to walk past the table. The lack of accountability was palpable. There was no one to blame, no one to punish. The super had lost his powers, and he would simply wait for them to return, or perhaps he would leave, embarrassed and shaken, and go home. The woman who had been his victim was gone, safe in the distance, her trauma left to fester in the dark.

Ethan turned away from the window. He didn’t want to see the man’s face, didn’t want to see the confusion etched into his features. He wanted to forget the weight of that moment, the way the air had changed, the way the world had shifted on its axis. He pulled his jacket tighter, the wool rough against his neck, and continued down the sidewalk. The street stretched out before him, empty and quiet, a long, dark path leading home. He had a cup of tea to make, a bath to run, and a life to resume. He was going to exist, ordinary and unremarkable, just like he always had.

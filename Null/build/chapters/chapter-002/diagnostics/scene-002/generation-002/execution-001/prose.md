The glass of The Gilded Cage acted as a one-way mirror, darkening the interior into a murky silhouette against the neon spill of the streetlights. Ethan kept walking, his pace dictated by the need to keep his legs moving rather than by any sense of urgency. The air outside was colder than it had been inside, carrying the metallic tang of recent rain and the faint, oily scent of exhaust. He counted his steps. One, two, three. He focused on the pavement, on the way the wet brick reflected the amber glow of the streetlamps, on the rhythmic thud of his own sneakers against the concrete. It was a grounding technique he’d picked up from a self-help article he’d read three years ago and mostly forgotten. It helped now.

Through the window, the figure of the super remained. The man stood near the table where the commotion had been, his posture rigid. He was not moving with the fluid, predatory grace Ethan had seen just minutes ago. Instead, he shifted his weight from one foot to the other, a subtle, unconscious compensation for the sudden absence of the superhuman strength that had defined his presence. The super ran a hand through his hair, the motion slow and deliberate, as if he were trying to process the fact that his fingers were still attached to his hand. He looked down at them, turning them over, flexing the joints. It was a mundane gesture, the kind anyone might make while waiting for a drink, but in this context, it carried a heavy weight of confusion.

Ethan felt a pull to look back, a magnetic draw that tugged at the base of his skull. He wanted to see if the man was reaching for his phone, or perhaps looking for a weapon that would no longer be effective. He wanted to know if the super was trying to punch a glass, or lift a chair, testing the boundaries of his own new, ordinary limitations. But Ethan did not look. He knew, with a visceral certainty that felt more like instinct than logic, that his presence was not what had caused the change. He was just a guy walking home, with shaking hands and a heart that still felt too large for his chest. The man inside was dealing with a crisis of identity that had nothing to do with the stranger passing by on the sidewalk.

The super’s status in this city was a known quantity. People like him were celebrities, strategic assets, and, in the eyes of the law, largely untouchable. They could break bones and shatter glass and walk away with a public apology and a tab for the damages. But right now, he was just a man in a bar who couldn’t lift his coffee cup without making a sound. The disconnect was stark. The man was likely feeling the same disorientation Ethan felt, a sudden, jarring shift in the physics of the world. He was powerless in a setting where his power had been his primary currency. The frustration would be palpable, a low-grade hum that vibrated through the glass and into the street.

Ethan reached the corner, where the streetlight cast long, distorted shadows across the intersection. He paused, leaning against the brick wall of the adjacent building. The texture of the bricks was rough and gritty under his palms, a tangible reality that contrasted sharply with the ethereal, untouchable nature of the man inside the bar. He took a deep breath, the cold air filling his lungs and settling into the ache in his chest. He was still here. He was still himself. And the super was still inside, grappling with the silence of his own diminished body.

The scene had ended, but the echo of it remained, a static charge in the air. Ethan pushed off the wall and continued walking, leaving the darkened window behind. The super was still there, a fixed point in the periphery of Ethan’s vision as he turned the corner. The mystery of the power loss hung in the air, an unexplained variable in an equation Ethan didn’t have the numbers to solve. He would go home, make his tea, and try to sleep, carrying the weight of an event he had witnessed but not caused, a ghost of a power he didn’t possess.

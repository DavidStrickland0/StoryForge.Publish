The silence in the void was heavy, a physical weight that pressed against Caelen’s eardrums even though he had no ears to hear with. He stood in the center of the shifting landscape, his bare feet sinking slightly into the translucent ground. It looked like frozen water, or perhaps glass, but it yielded to his step with the softness of moss. The air tasted of nothing. That was the most unsettling part. On Earth, the air had always carried something: the metallic tang of blood, the sulfurous bite of cordite, the stale sweat of the transport ship, or the cloying sweetness of the mother’s kitchen, a memory that now lingered in the periphery of his mind like a stain. Here, there was only absence.

Caelen looked down at his hands. They were his hands, or the memory of them. The skin was smooth, the knuckles unswollen, the calluses from the rifle stock and the weight of the M4 gone. He flexed his fingers, expecting the familiar ache in his wrist, the one that never fully healed after a long night of holding a line. There was no pain. There was no fatigue. He moved his arm, and it moved with a fluidity that felt alien, as if the joints were made of oiled steel rather than bone and tendon. He was a new machine, built from the blueprint of the last one, but stripped of the wear and tear.

He tried to remember the taste of the coffee he had drunk before the deployment. It was there, but it was distant, like a flavor described by someone else. He could remember the bitterness, the acidity, the way it burned the back of his throat, but he could not taste it. He was a curator of his own past, walking through a museum of sensations that had been sealed off from him by the glass of death.

This was wrong. Caelen did not like being wrong. He had spent eighteen years building a life around the certainty of cause and effect. Pull the trigger, the bullet flies. Eat the food, the hunger fades. Bleed, the pain arrives. Everything had a price, and he had been good at paying it. He had been good at taking what he needed and leaving the rest for others to scramble over. He had been selfish, yes, but it was a practical selfishness, a survival strategy refined in the crucible of his upbringing and sharpened in the barracks. It had kept him alive. It had kept him ahead.

And then he had thrown himself in front of a bullet.

The memory of the courtyard surfaced, unbidden and sharp. The woman’s face, pale and terrified, the dead infant wrapped in cloth in her arms. Miller’s eyes, hard and flat, the rifle raised. The sound of the burst, a sharp crack that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere. The impact, a hot shove that drove him into the woman, the way her body had gone rigid against his, the way his own body had shattered.

He had not thought about it. That was the part that bothered him most. He had not calculated the odds. He had not weighed his life against hers. He had simply moved. It was an instinct, a reflex, as automatic as breathing or blinking. And for the first time in his life, Caelen found that he did not know why he had done it. He had not been the hero. He had not been the saint. He had been Caelen, the selfish, ambitious, frightened marine who wanted to be seen, who wanted to matter, who wanted to be more than just a body in a uniform. And in that split second, when the world had stopped and the geometry of the situation demanded a choice, he had chosen the woman.

Why?

He poked at the memory, trying to find the logic, the rational justification. There was none. It was a void within the void, a hole in the middle of his understanding where the reason should have been. He had always been a man of reasons. He had reasons for joining the Marines, reasons for his temper, reasons for his ambition. To find a reason for his death was to find a reason for his life, and the more he looked, the less he saw.

The landscape around him shifted. The frozen glass ground rippled, and the translucent fragments that had been drifting in the air began to coalesce. They were not random anymore. They were forming shapes, patterns, structures. Caelen watched as a fragment of his childhood bedroom drifted closer, the red glow of his old video game console flickering in the distance. It faded, replaced by a fragment of the training center, the smell of chlorine and sweat hanging in the air.

Then, the shapes stopped drifting. They froze.

In the center of the void, where the air had been empty, a light appeared. It was not a light in the way he understood light. It did not shine, or cast shadows, or warm the skin. It was a presence, a density in the nothingness. It was a box, or a frame, or a window, but it had no edges, no corners. It was a space that was clearly defined by what it was not.

Caelen stepped forward. He did not know why he moved, but his feet carried him. The ground did not yield under his weight this time. It was solid, or perhaps he was solid enough to make it so. He stopped before the presence, and he saw.

It was a display. A list.

Two lines of text floated in the air, glowing with a faint, neutral white. They were simple, unadorned, devoid of any moral judgment or explanatory text. They were just facts, presented with the cold clarity of a computer interface.

**Dominion: Level 1**

**Sacrifice: Level 1**

Caelen stared at them. The words were in a language he understood, but the concepts were foreign. Dominion. Sacrifice. He knew the words. He had used them, or heard them used, in the context of theology, of philosophy, of the abstract debates that his squadmates sometimes engaged in during downtime. But here, they were not abstract. They were metrics. They were scores.

He looked at the first line. Dominion. Level 1.

He thought of the time he had taken Davis’s spot in the mess hall, leaving the private to wait while he ate first. He thought of the way he had manipulated Miller’s trust to get a better assignment. He thought of the small, petty cruelties he had inflicted, the times he had used his strength, his knowledge, his position to gain an advantage. He had always believed those actions were neutral, part of the game, part of the cost of survival. But here, they were counted. They were quantified. They were Level 1.

He looked at the second line. Sacrifice. Level 1.

He thought of the courtyard. The woman. The infant. The bullet. The single act, the one unplanned, irrational, uncalculated act of his life. And here, too, it was counted. It was quantified. It was Level 1.

The equality of the numbers struck him with a force that felt like a physical blow. One. One. They were the same. His selfishness and his selflessness were balanced. His greed and his generosity were equal.

He felt a surge of anger, hot and sudden. This was unfair. He had not earned this. He had not chosen this. He had been a Marine, a soldier, a man who did what he was told and took what he could get. He had not signed up for this. He had not asked for a scorecard. He had not wanted to be measured.

But the display did not change. It did not respond to his anger. It did not explain itself. It simply was. A neutral presence in a void of nothing, offering him a truth he had no framework to understand.

Caelen reached out, his hand hovering in the air before the light. He could not touch it. He could not grab it. He could only look. He looked at the numbers, at the cold, unblinking facts of his existence, and he felt the weight of his own soul pressing down on him, a weight he had never known until now.

He did not know what it meant. He did not know how to use it. He did not know if it was a reward or a punishment. He only knew that it was there, and that it was his, and that it would follow him wherever he went.

He stood there, in the center of the void, surrounded by the ghosts of his life, watching the two lines of light pulse with a slow, steady rhythm. He was waiting. He was waiting for something. He did not know what, but he knew that the waiting had changed. It was no longer just a pause between life and death. It was a preparation. A calibration. A settling into a new shape.

The landscape around him began to shift again, the fragments of his memories drifting back into a slow, lazy rotation. The red glow of his childhood, the chlorine smell of the training center, the metallic tang of blood. They were still there, but they were smaller now. Distant.

Caelen took a breath, though he did not need to. He felt a strange sense of clarity, a sharpness of mind that he had not possessed before. He was not afraid. He was not sad. He was aware. He was aware of the numbers, of the balance, of the void. He was aware of the fact that he was here, and that he was not alone in the way he had always been alone.

The darkness did not arrive all at once. It came in layers, like the closing of a heavy curtain in a theater where the audience had stopped watching.

Caelen lay on his back, the dust of the courtyard settling into the creases of his uniform, but the weight of his body had vanished. The terrible, screaming heat in his chest was receding, replaced by a dull, distant ache, like a bruise on a limb he could no longer feel. He tried to lift a hand to brush the grit from his face, but his fingers did not move. In fact, he could not feel his fingers. He could not feel the ground beneath him, or the sky above, or the trembling breath of the woman crouched nearby.

He tried to speak, to ask Davis for a medic, to tell Miller he was done, but no sound came from his throat. The silence was absolute and total, a vacuum that pressed against his eardrums until the concept of sound itself seemed irrelevant.

Then, the gray haze began to shift.

It was not a visual change, exactly. It was more like the way a dream alters when one begins to wake. The flat, featureless gray around him started to ripple, pulling in fragments of light and texture from somewhere deep within his own mind. The dust of the courtyard dissolved, replaced by the smell of gun oil and the metallic tang of blood, but without the pain. The sharp, jagged edges of his last memory—the rifle clattering on the brick, the mother’s sobbing—softened into a muted, distant echo.

Caelen found himself standing.

He looked down. His hands were there, solid and real, but they were not the hands of a Marine. They were smooth, unmarked, devoid of the calluses and scars that had defined his body for eighteen years. He flexed his fingers, and the motion felt effortless, as if he were moving through water.

He looked up.

There was no sky. There was no floor. There was only a shifting, translucent landscape made of memory. To his left, a fragment of a room from his childhood hung in the air: his mother’s kitchen, the yellow wallpaper peeling, the smell of burnt toast lingering in the stillness. To his right, a piece of the base, the barracks room where he and Davis had argued over the radio frequency, the words hanging in the air like trapped insects.

He reached out to touch the kitchen wall. His hand passed through it. The fragment shuddered, the smell of burnt toast intensifying for a second before fading, and the image drifted away like smoke.

Caelen stood in the center of this drifting gallery, his heart—or the part of him that still functioned as a heart—beating with a strange, rhythmic calm. The panic he had expected to feel, the terror of dying, the rage at Miller, the guilt for his own selfishness, it was all there, but it was distant. It was a story he was watching from a great height, the characters small and far away.

He remembered the moment before the shot. He remembered the geometry of the courtyard, the line of fire, the mother’s face. It was so clear, yet so far away. He could see the logic of it, the tactical necessity, the cold calculation that had driven him to be efficient, to be seen, to be important. And he could see the moment he had broken that logic. He had thrown himself into the path of the bullet. It had been a stupid act. A useless act. He had been eighteen, and he had been afraid, and he had wanted to be human.

The memory of that act did not bring him pride. It brought him a strange, hollow clarity. He had not been a hero. He had been a man who had finally decided to stop being a machine. And now, the machine was broken.

He looked at his hands again. They were clean. They were perfect. They were not his.

He took a step forward, and the landscape around him shifted. The fragment of the barracks room drifted closer, the faces of his squadmates blurred and indistinct. He could hear their voices, but they were muffled, as if he were listening to them through a thick wall. Davis was talking about a promotion. Miller was barking orders. The sounds were flat, stripped of their emotional weight.

Caelen felt a profound sense of detachment. He was no longer in the courtyard. He was no longer in his body. He was somewhere else, in a space that had no name and no rules. The pain in his chest was gone, replaced by a lightness that felt like freedom.

He stood in the void, surrounded by the ghosts of his own life, and waited.

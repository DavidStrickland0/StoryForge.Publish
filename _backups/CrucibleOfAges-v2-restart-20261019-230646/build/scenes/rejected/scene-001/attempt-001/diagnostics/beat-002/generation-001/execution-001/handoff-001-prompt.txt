The silence didn’t hold. It snapped like a dry twig under a boot, shattered by the high-pitched whine of a projectile slicing through the air.

My head snapped up, eyes tracking the sound, but the source was already gone, swallowed by the smoke and the chaos of the street. I didn’t think. Thinking was a luxury for people with more than three rounds in their magazine and a support team that wasn’t currently trying to save their own skins. My body moved on muscle memory, a reflex drilled into me at Parris Island and sharpened in the field. I dropped, hitting the dirt with a heavy thud that vibrated through my chest plate.

I was flat on my back, staring up at a sky choked with grey dust and the distant, indifferent sun. The woman was three meters to my left. I could see the hem of her torn shawl, the dust coating her hands as she pressed them over her ears. She was frozen. Not hiding, not running, just frozen, a statue made of flesh and terror.

The whine got louder. The whine got closer.

I had three options. I could stay where I was, exposed on the open street, and hope the round missed. I could scramble for the trench, a distance that felt like a mile in the heat of the moment, and hope I got there before the impact. Or I could do the stupid, stupid thing that my training screamed at me to avoid and my gut, that primitive, animal part of me that still remembered what it felt like to be a child, suggested.

I rolled.

It wasn’t a tactical maneuver. It was a desperate, ugly scramble, my boots churning up the dirt and broken glass. I moved toward the debris pile where the woman was crouched, my rifle clutched tight against my chest, the three rounds in the magazine feeling like a promise I couldn’t keep. I didn’t look at her. I couldn’t afford to. I focused on the space between her and the open street, the gap where the threat was coming from.

I hit the pile of rubble and collapsed over her.

It was a clumsy thing, my helmet striking the concrete, my shoulder taking the brunt of the impact. I was on my back, my legs tangled with hers, my rifle pointing at the sky. I was a shield. A human, clumsy, expensive shield.

The whine peaked.

Then the world exploded.

The impact was not a sound. It was a force. A physical blow that hit me in the center of my back, driving the air from my lungs and slamming me into the dirt. The concrete beside my head disintegrated, shards of stone and rebar spraying outward in a shower of deadly debris. Dust billowed up, thick and choking, filling my mouth, my nose, my eyes.

I coughed, a dry, hacking spasm that tore at my throat. My vision was blurred, red-rimmed and distorted. I tried to move, to check the woman, to see if she was alive, but my body wouldn’t listen. My muscles were locked in a rigid spasm, my nerves firing off warning signals that drowned out everything else.

The pain was intense.

It started as a dull ache in my shoulder, then bloomed into a searing, white-hot fire that raced down my arm and into my chest. I gasped, the air scraping against my raw throat. I was alive. I was still alive. The thought was absurd, a joke told in a language I didn’t understand.

I shifted, wincing as the movement pulled at the injury. My hand found the woman’s arm. It was cold, limp, but it moved. She was breathing. I could feel the faint, ragged rise and fall of her chest against my side.

I lay there, my head throbbing, my body screaming in protest, and I listened.

The whine was gone. The explosion was gone. The silence returned, but this time it was different. It was heavy, oppressive, a weight that pressed down on my chest. The dust settled, slowly, coating everything in a fine, grey powder.

I checked my rifle. The magazine was still in place. I had three rounds. I had a broken shoulder, a concussion, and a civilian tangled in my limbs. I was useless. I was a liability. I was exactly what Vance had been looking at when he had turned away from me.

I tried to push myself up, but my arm gave way. I fell back against the rubble, my breathing shallow and labored. I closed my eyes, not out of defeat, but out of necessity. I needed to think. I needed to process. I needed to understand why I had done what I had done.

I had no answer.

The only thing I knew for certain was that I was still here. And I was still alone.

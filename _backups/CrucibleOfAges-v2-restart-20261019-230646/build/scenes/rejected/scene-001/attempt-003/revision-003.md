The mud in the ditch was thick enough to swallow a boot up to the ankle, and it smelled like a mix of wet earth and old blood. I kept my weight on the balls of my feet, trying to stay light, though gravity had other plans. To my left, a kid named Miller was shaking. His whole body rattled, making his rifle stock click against his cheek. He was eighteen, maybe nineteen. He had the kind of face that hadn’t yet decided whether to grow into it or be eaten by it.

"Easy, Miller," I said, my voice low and steady. It was a good voice. I used it to calm down guys who were about to do something stupid, like run forward or throw up in their helmets. "You’re safe here. I’ve got the sector."

That was a lie, of course. I had the sector because I had positioned myself so that his body was the primary barrier between me and the open ground. He was a fleshy shield, a human sandbag that happened to know how to aim a weapon. In the field, efficiency was the only religion that mattered. If you could use a man’s fear to keep him quiet and his body to keep you alive, you were a good soldier. If you wasted that potential on sympathy, you were a liability.

I checked my magazine. Fourteen rounds. Enough for a few more exchanges. I looked past Miller’s shoulder, over the ridge where the enemy had been pushing for the last hour. They were heavy infantry, armored in a way that suggested they had better supply lines and less concern for the individuals wearing the plates. We were light infantry, fast and agile, but we were also expendable. That was the trade-off. We got the glory of the first strike; they got the comfort of the rear guard.

"Miller," I said again, tapping the butt of my rifle against his shoulder. It was a firm tap, a reminder. "When I move, you cover me. Do you understand? You don’t have to hit anything. Just make noise. Just make them look at you."

He nodded, his eyes wide and wet. He wanted to believe me. He wanted to believe that I was a leader, a protector, a guy who had his back covered. I let him have that. It was easier to control a man who felt safe than one who felt afraid. Fear made men unpredictable. Safety made them obedient.

I waited for the command. It came from the radio, a burst of static and then the voice of Sergeant Kael. "Hold position. Enemy advance stalling. Prepare for rotation."

Rotation. That meant we were moving. That meant the next wave of men would take our place in the ditch, and we would move forward into the open. I looked at Miller. He looked at me. In that look, I saw the transaction. I was the one with the plan. I was the one with the experience. I was the one who had survived the last three rotations. He was the one who was about to find out what it felt like to be on the other side of the line.

"Good job, Miller," I said, and I meant it in the way that a mechanic means it when he tells a driver his car is in good shape. It was a compliment to the utility, not the soul.

I started to crawl out of the ditch, my movements practiced and silent. I didn’t look back. I knew he would follow. I knew he would look at the open ground and feel the pull of the enemy’s guns, and I knew he would hesitate. That hesitation was what I needed. It would keep him behind me, in my shadow, where he was useful.

As I reached the lip of the ditch, I heard the first shot. It was a crack, sharp and final, and then the second, and the third. The air filled with the smell of cordite and the sound of dirt being kicked up by high-velocity rounds. I didn’t flinch. I had expected it. I had planned for it.

I turned to look at Miller. He was still in the ditch, his face pale, his hands frozen on his rifle. He wasn’t shooting. He wasn’t covering me. He was just standing there, paralyzed by the sound, by the sight of the enemy, by the sudden realization that his safety was an illusion.

"Move, Miller!" I shouted, my voice cutting through the chaos. "Get up! Now!"

He didn’t move. He was staring at the ground, at the mud, at the dirt that was flying around him. He was thinking. He was thinking about his mother, or his girlfriend, or the fact that he was going to die. All of those thoughts were slow, and in combat, slow meant dead.

I grabbed the back of his vest and hauled him forward. He stumbled, his boots slipping in the mud, and I felt the weight of him against my back. He was heavy. He was dead weight. I dragged him toward the next cover, a pile of rubble that was barely enough to protect us.

We slid down the slope, the mud sucking at our legs, and I let go of him. He collapsed into the dirt, gasping, his eyes rolling back in his head. He was unconscious. Or close to it.

I checked my weapon. I was still functional. I was still alive. I looked at Miller. He was a mess. His helmet was askew, his face was covered in mud, and his eyes were wide and empty. He was useless now. He was a burden.

But he was also a witness. And witnesses were valuable.

I stood up, brushing the mud from my uniform. I was clean. I was ready. I was the one who had made the move. I was the one who had taken the risk. I was the one who had survived.

And I was the one who was about to go back out there and do it again.

I stepped over Miller’s body without looking down. The mud was deep, sucking at my boots with a wet, tearing sound that was far too loud in the sudden quiet. My rifle was already up, the stock pressed hard against my shoulder, my finger resting on the trigger guard. The enemy had stopped firing, which was worse than if they hadn’t. Silence meant they were reloading, or they were watching, or they were coming from a direction I hadn’t checked. I checked the left flank first, then the right. Nothing but broken concrete and the gray, dusty sky above.

I took three steps into the open ground. Then four. My boots crunched on debris, and a single round pinged off the rebar sticking out of a shattered column near me. I ducked, flattening myself against the base of a collapsed wall. The concrete was warm from the sun, even now, in the middle of this hell. I felt the heat of it through the fabric of my uniform, a strange, intimate contact with the destroyed world.

"Contact!" I screamed, my voice cracking on the second word. I fired three rounds into the shadow of the alleyway where the ping had come from. No answer. Just the echo of my own shots bouncing off the brick and stone. I was alone. The radio was dead. The command post was a memory. The squad was a ghost.

I moved. I didn’t run; running was for people who didn’t know how to die. I sprinted in short bursts, head down, eyes scanning. A man in a vest burst out of a doorway twenty meters to my right. He was holding a pistol, his face twisted in a snarl of rage and exhaustion. He raised the gun. I raised mine.

We fired at the same time.

I felt the impact in my shoulder, a heavy, blunt shove that spun me sideways. My vision blurred. The man across the street was already falling, his pistol clattering into the dirt. I didn’t care. I didn’t care about the blood soaking through my uniform, or the way my left arm had gone numb, dead weight against my side. I rolled behind the overturned trash can, gasping, my breath coming in ragged hitches. The pain was a white-hot spike, but it was distant, like it was happening to someone else.

I checked my magazine. The magazine was empty. I ejected it, racked the slide, and inserted a fresh one. My hands were shaking, but they were steady enough. That was the trick. You let your hands shake, but you didn’t let them drop. You kept your eyes on the target. You kept your mind on the next step.

I stood up, leaning heavily on the trash can for support. The air smelled of cordite and wet dust. I could see the main street stretching out ahead of me, a gray ribbon of destruction. There were bodies on the ground, some of them ours, some of them theirs. It was hard to tell the difference at this distance. The insignia on the uniforms were covered in mud and blood.

I took a step forward. Then another. The pain in my shoulder was becoming a throbbing ache, a constant reminder that I was made of meat and bone. I ignored it. Ignoring pain was a skill, just like ignoring fear. It was easier to ignore pain. Fear was a noise that got into your head, whispering that you were small, that you were fragile, that you were going to die. Pain was just information. It told you what was broken so you could fix it, or work around it.

I reached the corner of the building where I had taken the shot. The man was still there, slumped against the wall. I kicked his pistol away from his hand. He didn’t move. I checked his pulse. He had no pulse. I felt a strange sense of satisfaction, a cold, clean feeling in the pit of my stomach. He had been a threat. I had removed the threat. That was the job.

I continued down the street. The buildings on either side were gutted, their interiors exposed to the sky. I could see the debris of lives lived here, books scattered on the floors, clothes hanging from racks, a child’s shoe in the middle of the road. It was a graveyard of the mundane. I moved through it with a grim respect for the efficiency of death.

I heard a sound. A low, rumbling engine. I stopped, pressing my back against a wall. The sound was getting closer. A truck. A military truck. I waited, holding my breath, until the engine faded into the distance. I let out a slow breath, my heart pounding in my ears.

I was alive. I was still the most dangerous thing in this street. I was the one who had made the move, the one who had taken the risk, the one who had survived. The others were either dead or broken. I was whole. I was functional. I was ready.

I started walking again, my pace steady, my rifle ready. The sun was beginning to set, casting long shadows across the rubble. The light was orange, painting the destroyed city in a false warmth. I walked into the shadows, moving with the quiet confidence of a man who knows exactly what he is worth.

The street was quiet in a way that felt wrong.

I had learned in the last two years that silence in a combat zone was just the sound of the enemy checking their sights. It was the breath before the scream. I kept my rifle up, the stock pressed into my collarbone, my left arm hanging dead and heavy by my side. The blood had dried in thick, rust-colored patches on my sleeve, making the fabric stiff and cold against my skin. Every time I shifted my weight, the wound pulsed with a dull, rhythmic ache. I ignored it. Pain was just data. It told you where you were broken. I had time to fix the broken parts later. Right now, I just needed to keep moving.

I scanned the upper floors of the buildings on either side. Broken windows stared back at me like empty eye sockets. I checked the debris piles, the overturned cars, the shadows cast by the crumbling facades. Nothing moved. The sun was lower now, slicing through the gaps in the rubble and painting the dust in long, golden streaks. It looked almost beautiful, in a desolate, post-apocalyptic sort of way. If I hadn’t been bleeding out and walking through a graveyard of buildings, I might have even appreciated the lighting.

Then I heard it.

It was a small sound, easily lost in the ambient hum of the city settling into night. A cough. Wet, ragged, and terrified. It came from the mouth of a collapsed building to my left, where a concrete slab had caved in, creating a narrow, dark alleyway. I stopped walking. I raised my rifle, the barrel steady despite the tremor in my legs. I didn’t peek. Peeking got you killed. I waited.

The cough came again. Then a whimper.

I moved to the corner, keeping my body low, my profile tight. I leaned out just enough to see into the alley. My eyes adjusted to the gloom.

There was a woman.

She was sitting on the ground, her back pressed against the shattered brick wall. She was small, wrapped in a torn dress that had seen better days, her arms wrapped tightly around a child who was curled up in her lap. The child was silent, which was worse than crying. The woman was looking at me. Her eyes were wide, rimmed with red, filled with a terror that was so raw it looked like it was eating her from the inside out.

I lowered the rifle slightly. I didn’t put it away. Not yet. In this game, you kept your weapon up until you were sure the threat was gone. And right now, the threat wasn’t a guy with a gun. The threat was the fact that I was a Marine, and she was a civilian, and in my experience, that combination rarely ended well for either of us.

"Hey," I said. My voice sounded rough, scraped raw by the dust and the adrenaline. "Can you hear me?"

She flinched. The child stirred, a small, whimpering sound escaping their lips.

"I'm not going to hurt you," I said. It was a lie. I might not have wanted to hurt her, but if I had a reason, I would. That was the job. That was the training. I was a Marine. I did what I was told, or what I had to do to survive. "Is there anyone else with you?"

She shook her head. Her eyes moved past me, scanning the street behind me. She was looking for backup. For more Marines. For the next threat. I could see the calculation in her face. She was trying to figure out if I was the problem or the solution.

I stepped into the alley. The ground was covered in broken glass and ash. I kept my distance, giving her space. "What happened here?" I asked.

She didn’t answer. She just pulled the child closer, her arms tightening until her knuckles turned white.

I sighed. I had no time for this. I had a radio that didn't work, a squad that didn't exist, and a wound that was slowly turning my left side into a map of pain. I needed to get out of this city. I needed to find a checkpoint, a safe house, something. I couldn’t stay here. I couldn’t be responsible for this.

"Look," I said, keeping my voice steady. "I’m leaving. You should too. Take the child and go."

I turned to leave. I took two steps back toward the main street.

And that was when the sound came.

It wasn’t a gun. It was a mechanical whine, high-pitched and rising. A drone. Or a rocket. Something airborne, moving fast, coming from behind the building I had just stepped out of.

My head snapped around. My eyes tracked the sound. A streak of fire, thin and fast, was cutting through the air. It was aimed at the alley. At her.

Time didn’t slow down. That was a myth. Time just got loud. The world went sharp and bright and very, very real.

I didn’t think. Thinking takes time, and time was a luxury I didn’t have. My body moved before my brain could catch up. I was a Marine. I was a weapon. And right now, there was a target in my crosshairs.

I didn’t have a chance to aim. I didn’t have a chance to calculate the trajectory. I just had a body, and a threat, and a choice.

I threw myself forward.

I didn’t think about the woman. I didn’t think about the child. I thought about the fire. I thought about the hole it was going to make. I thought about the fact that if I stayed where I was, I would be dead in two seconds. And if I moved, I might be dead in one.

I landed on my back, my rifle still in my right hand, my left arm splayed out to the side. I was between her and the threat. I was the obstacle. I was the wall.

The fire hit the ground behind me. The explosion was a wall of heat and noise. It punched the air out of my lungs. The world turned white, then black, then a swirling blur of dust and debris.

I felt a jolt in my shoulder, a sharp, biting pain that shot down my arm. The blast wave had caught me. I was still alive. My heart was hammering against my ribs, a frantic, bird-like flutter. I was still breathing.

I lay there for a moment, staring up at the broken sky. The dust was settling. The silence was back.

I sat up slowly. My body ached in places I didn’t know I had. My left arm was dead again, the nerves screaming in a low, constant hum. I looked down.

The woman was still there. She was unharmed. The child was unharmed.

She was looking at me. Her eyes were wide. Her mouth was open. She wasn’t screaming. She wasn’t crying. She was just staring.

I looked at her. I looked at the crater in the ground behind me. I looked at my rifle.

I didn’t know why I had done it. It was just a reaction. A reflex. A way to keep the fire away from the thing that was close to me.

But I was alive. And she was alive.

I stood up. My legs were shaking. I gripped my rifle tighter. I needed to move. I needed to keep moving.

I looked at the woman one last time. I didn’t say anything. There was nothing to say.

I turned and walked away.

The street was quiet again. The sun was setting, casting long shadows across the rubble. I walked into the shadows, my pace steady, my rifle ready. The world was broken. But I was still standing. And that was enough.

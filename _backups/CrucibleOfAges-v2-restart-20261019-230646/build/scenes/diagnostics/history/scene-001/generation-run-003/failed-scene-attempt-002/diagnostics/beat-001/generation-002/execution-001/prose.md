The mud on the south bank of the riverbank was slick with rain and the churn of boots, and the air tasted of cordite and wet dust. Kaelen crouched behind a splintered wooden fence, the metal of his rifle cold against his cheek, his fingers wrapped around the trigger guard with the automatic tension of a man who had spent three years learning to kill. Across the open field, a cluster of buildings burned, their smoke rising in thick, oily columns that obscured the sky. The enemy had pushed through the eastern perimeter, and his squad was pinned down, bleeding out, shouting orders that dissolved into the roar of automatic fire.

Kaelen checked his magazine. Fourteen rounds left. He counted them by feel, the plastic casing clicking against the metal, a small, satisfying ritual that grounded him in the immediate reality of survival. He didn’t look at the bodies of his fellow Marines lying in the ditch; they were already history, already data points in a war that had long since stopped being about ideals and become a calculation of logistics. He was good at logistics. He was good at finding the angles that others missed, the weaknesses in the armor, the moments of hesitation where a man could be turned into a resource.

A spotter on the ridge had called out a movement in the tree line. Kaelen adjusted his aim, the crosshairs drifting through the wet leaves. He saw a figure, a soldier, stumbling with a bad leg. The man was trying to crawl toward a makeshift shelter, his hands dragging in the mud. Kaelen could have shot him. It would have been easy. It would have been correct. The figure was a threat, a potential witness, a variable in the chaos. But Kaelen didn’t shoot. Instead, he watched the man’s movements, noting the way his weight shifted, the limp in his gait. It was a data point. A weakness. If the man could be captured, interrogated, turned against his own unit, the value would be immense. Kaelen’s mind cataloged the opportunity, the potential leverage, the concrete benefit of holding a piece of the enemy’s intelligence. He was already calculating the trade.

The firefight intensified. A burst of heavy machine-gun fire tore through the air, shredding the fence behind him. Splinters of wood flew into his face, stinging his skin. He flinched, then forced himself to remain still. Fear was a luxury he couldn’t afford. Fear was for men who didn’t understand the mechanics of death. He understood them. He had seen enough of them to know that survival was a discipline, a series of choices made in the split second before the instinct to run took over. He had made those choices before. He had made them well.

A radio crackled in his earpiece. "Kaelen, we need you to move to the secondary objective. Get those civilians out of the sector. They’re in the way."

Kaelen ignored the order. The secondary objective was a distraction. The real game was the enemy’s retreat. If he could cut off their escape route, he could gain access to their supply cache. The thought was sharp, clear, and entirely his own. He didn’t care about the civilians. They were obstacles, irrelevant variables in a larger equation. They were weak, they were slow, and they were getting in his way.

He shifted his position, moving along the fence line, his boots silent on the mud. He needed a better angle. He needed to see the enemy’s command post. He scanned the tree line, his eyes tracking the movement of the soldiers who were beginning to regroup. There, a cluster of them, huddled behind a fallen tree. They were confused, disoriented. They were vulnerable. Kaelen smiled, a cold, thin expression that didn’t reach his eyes. He had the high ground. He had the time. He had the will.

He raised his rifle, the barrel steady in his hands. He didn’t need to hesitate. He didn’t need to think. He just needed to pull the trigger. But before he could, a sound cut through the noise of the battle. A scream. High, thin, and terrified. It came from the edge of the tree line, from the direction of the burning buildings. Kaelen’s head snapped toward the sound.

He saw her. A woman, small and frail, clutching a child in her arms. She was running, stumbling, her eyes wide with panic. She was in the open, exposed to the fire. She was in the way.

Kaelen’s finger tightened on the trigger. She was an obstacle. She was a liability. If he let her die, the enemy would lose a witness. If he saved her, he would be wasting time, wasting energy, wasting his own life for a stranger. The logic was sound. It was the logic of a Marine. It was the logic of a man who had survived this long.

But the scream again, a sharp, piercing cry that cut through the haze of smoke and fear. Kaelen watched her stumble, watched her fall to her knees in the mud. The child in her arms was crying, a thin, reedy sound. Kaelen felt a flicker of something in his chest, a tightness that had no place in the battlefield. He looked at the enemy soldiers regrouping behind the tree. He looked at the woman, who was now looking at him, her eyes wide with terror.

He didn’t move. He didn’t shoot. He just watched, his rifle lowered, his mind racing. The enemy was closing in. The woman was in danger. The choice was simple. It was a calculation. It was a decision. And for the first time in his life, Kaelen didn’t know which side of the equation he was on.

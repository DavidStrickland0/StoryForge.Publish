The mud in the trench was a thick, sucking paste that had nothing to do with the rain and everything to do with the boots of the three men who had run in front of me and never stopped running. I wiped the slime from my visor with the back of a gloved hand, leaving a grey smear that offered zero optical clarity but did a decent job of letting me know exactly how much I hated this specific Tuesday.

"Keep your head down, rookie," a voice crackled in my earpiece. Sergeant Miller. Or "The Wall," as the men in our squad had dubbed him after the time he’d personally shoveled a crater to create a firing step because the map was wrong. "We’re pinned. I don’t care if you’re itching to go hero. You stay low."

I adjusted my grip on the rifle, my knuckles whitening against the polymer. I wasn’t itching to go hero. I was itching to go home. There is a distinct, tactical difference between the two, though in the heat of a firefight, the distinction often gets lost on people who are still counting their paychecks. I had eighteen years of life on this rock, and I had spent the last two of them in this uniform because it was the only thing that had ever made me feel like I occupied space in a room. It gave me a rank, a call sign, and a reason to be angry at the universe in a way that felt productive.

A burst of automatic fire chewed into the dirt inches from my face. The concussion pressed against my eardrums, a physical thud that reminded me of the fragility of the bone beneath the Kevlar. I flinched. Not because I was scared—fear is a useful chemical, a cocktail of adrenaline and cortisol that sharpens the senses and overrides the limbic system’s desire to vomit—but because the flinch was a waste of energy. I corrected my posture, lowering my center of gravity, bracing my feet against the muddy floor of the trench.

"Who’s flanking?" I asked. My voice was steady. I practiced my voice in the mirror every morning until it sounded like a man who had already decided he was going to survive this. It was a lie, of course. I was terrified. But I was also efficient.

"Don’t know," Miller replied. "Probably the heavy squad from the north ridge. Stay in cover. We’re waiting for the smoke."

I looked at the man to my left. The man to my left was Davis. Davis had been in the unit for six months. He was young, skinny, and he chewed his thumb with a rhythmic, compulsive motion that I found deeply irritating. He was shaking, a fine tremor that ran through his rifle and up his arms. He was a liability. In a vacuum, in a theoretical war where soldiers were interchangeable units of firepower, Davis was a defect. He reacted two seconds slower than the standard. He flinched at the sound of his own heartbeat.

I shifted my weight, angling my body slightly away from him. Not enough for him to notice, but enough that if I had to step over him to grab a better firing position, I wouldn’t have to touch him. It was a small, selfish calculation, the kind that defined my existence. I didn’t hate Davis. I didn’t love him. I simply valued my own survival more than his comfort, and I was currently calculating that my survival might require me to be the one taking the shot, not the one standing next to the guy who was going to hesitate.

"Miller," I said, keeping my eyes on the tree line where the enemy had vanished. "How many of us are actually effective?"

"Three," Miller said, his tone flat. "Me, you, and the guy behind the sandbag who hasn’t fired a single round yet. Why?"

"I’m just asking. Because if we’re holding this line, I’d prefer to know I’m not covering for someone who’s going to freeze."

There was a silence in the comms that lasted for a heartbeat. Then Miller’s voice came back, dry and devoid of sympathy. "You’re covering for everyone, son. That’s the job. You like it when it’s easy, so here’s some easy. Keep your head down. And for God’s sake, stop judging the squad. You’re eighteen. You think you’re the main character. You’re a support asset. Don’t forget it."

He was right. I was a support asset. I was a tool for violence, calibrated and oiled, designed to do a specific job with minimal waste. I had joined the Marines because I wanted status, because I wanted to be the guy people looked at when the door opened. I wanted to be the one who decided who lived and who died, because being in control was the only way to stop the world from feeling like it was trying to swallow me whole. And right now, in this muddy hole, I felt more in control than I had in years. I knew where the enemy was. I knew where my friends were. I knew exactly what I was willing to do to stay alive, and exactly what I was willing to let others do if it served my purpose.

I checked the magazine. The magazine was full. I checked the chamber. The chamber was locked. I took a breath, feeling the cold air burn in my lungs, and waited for the smoke to rise. The rain began to fall again, a fine mist that blurred the world beyond the trench. I watched the drops hit the mud, creating tiny, fleeting craters that disappeared as quickly as they formed. Everything here was temporary. The fear, the anger, the pride. It would all wash away, eventually.

"Smoke is up," Miller announced.

I moved. I didn’t run; running was for people who didn’t have a plan. I flowed, my body moving with the fluidity of someone who had rehearsed this motion a thousand times in a simulator that didn’t bleed. I cleared the sandbag, my boots finding the slick mud, and I raised my rifle. The smoke was thick, a grey wall that obscured the tree line. I couldn’t see them. I didn’t need to. I could hear them. The crunch of boots on gravel, the heavy breathing of men who were afraid.

I adjusted my aim, the red dot hovering in the grey mist. I was ready. I was sharp. I was the best of the best, or so I told myself every time I looked in the mirror. I was the one who would make it out. I was the one who would remember this day, and the others would be forgotten. That was the deal I had made with myself, years ago. I would take what I needed. I would use what was available. And I would leave the rest to rot.

I waited for the first shot to break the silence. I calculated the distance. I adjusted for the wind. I was a machine, precise and cold and utterly convinced of my own right to be the one standing on the other side of this moment. The rain soaked through my uniform, cold and heavy, but I didn’t feel it. I only felt the hum of the rifle in my hands, the steady beat of my own heart, and the absolute, unwavering certainty that I was the protagonist of this story.

Then the first shot fired.

The treeline held its breath.

I kept my eye on the gap where the enemy sniper had been, the crosshair resting on a patch of dry grass that smelled of crushed weeds and old rain. My finger lay on the trigger guard, relaxed but ready, waiting for the world to make sense. It wasn’t making sense. The radio crackled with static and fragments of commands that no longer applied to my sector. I could hear the distinct, rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* of small arms fire from somewhere to the east, a sound that felt distant, like a movie playing in another room.

"Miller!" I shouted, my voice flat, devoid of the panic I saw in their faces. "Squad is breaking. Fall back to the treeline. Now."

Sergeant Miller didn’t look at me. He was staring at the dirt, his jaw working as if he were chewing on the orders he’d received. His knuckles were white on his rifle, and he was shaking, a fine tremor that ran through his arms and into the weapon. He was a good soldier. He was also useless.

"Vance," I said, turning my head just enough to see the private. He was still on his knees, his helmet hanging off the side of his head, revealing a face that looked like it had been washed out by fear. He looked like a child who had forgotten how to breathe. "Stay down. Don’t move until I tell you to."

He nodded, a jerky, mechanical motion. He wasn’t listening. He was looking at me with the desperate hope of a man waiting for a miracle. I gave him the only thing I had left: indifference.

I turned back to the treeline.

The breakdown wasn’t sudden. It was a slow erosion, a leaking of structure into the mud. One by one, the sounds of cohesion stopped. The coordinated volleys became sporadic pops. The commands became shouts, then screams, then silence. I watched Sergeant Miller finally lift his head. He looked at me, and for a second, I saw the calculation in his eyes. He was trying to figure out if I was a threat or a savior. Then he saw the fear in my eyes, or maybe the lack of it, and he turned away.

He pulled his rifle from his shoulder, checked the chamber, and began to walk. Not run. He walked instead of running. He moved toward the tree line with the slow, deliberate gait of a man who had already decided he was going to die, or maybe just wanted to be somewhere else when he did. He disappeared into the brush, his uniform blending into the green and brown chaos of the forest floor.

"Miller is gone," Diaz said. His voice was thin, reedy. He was standing up, his rifle held loosely by his side. He looked at O’Malley, who was still arguing with the air. "Sergeant is gone."

O’Malley stopped pointing. He looked at Miller’s empty space, then at me. His face was a mask of confusion, the anger drained out of it, leaving behind a hollow, gray fatigue. "We hold the line," he whispered, as if repeating the words might make them true.

"There is no line," I said.

The words hung in the air, heavy and final. I watched Diaz’s shoulders slump. He looked at his rifle, then at the ground, and then he sat down. He didn’t stand up again. He just sat there, his back against the sandbags, his head in his hands. O’Malley looked at me, then at Vance, who was still kneeling, still waiting for a command that wasn’t coming. O’Malley’s hand drifted to his holster, then dropped. He turned and walked away, his boots crunching on the gravel, disappearing into the same brush where Miller had vanished.

I was alone.

Not alone in the way of a ghost, invisible and harmless. I was alone in the way of a sniper, a single point of contact between two worlds that wanted to destroy each other. The sandbags were still there, damp and heavy. The sky was still blue. The enemy was still out there, beyond the trees, waiting for me to make a mistake.

I checked my magazine again. The magazine was full. I checked my watch. 14:03.

I looked at Vance. He was watching me, his eyes wide, his lips moving silently. I looked at the treeline. I looked at the sky.

The system was gone. The rank, the chain of command, the institutional support, the shared purpose—it had all evaporated in the last ten minutes. What was left was just me, a gun, and the knowledge that I was the only one here who knew how to keep breathing.

I didn’t feel triumphant. I didn’t feel brave. I felt light. The weight of responsibility for other people’s lives was gone, and in its place was the simple, cold clarity of survival. I was no longer a Marine. I was a problem solver. And the problem was the enemy in the treeline.

I adjusted my position, shifting my weight to find a better angle. The sandbags were damp, the wet clay sticking to my boots. I ignored it. I focused on the gap in the trees, on the subtle shifts in the shadows, on the faint rustle of leaves that might be wind, or might be a boot.

I was ready.

The silence stretched, thin and taut. I could hear my own heartbeat, a steady drum in my ears. I could hear the distant sound of fire, the crackle of branches, the whisper of the wind. And I could hear Vance, breathing rapidly, his chest heaving under his flak jacket.

He was a liability. A dead weight. A reason for me to be here.

I looked at him, then back at the treeline.

I didn’t tell him to stay down. I didn’t tell him to run. I didn’t tell him anything.

I just waited.

The enemy sniper was still out there. I knew it because I had seen the muzzle flash, heard the crack of the rifle, and felt the bullet pass through the air inches from my ear. He was good. He was patient. He was waiting for me to move, to make a mistake, to show my hand.

I would not move.

I would not make a mistake.

I would wait.

The minutes ticked by. The sun moved slightly across the sky, casting long shadows across the sandbags. The light changed, turning the dust motes into gold. I watched them dance in the air, suspended in the stillness.

Vance was still kneeling. His eyes were fixed on me, pleading for an answer, for a command, for anything that would tell him he was not alone.

I looked at him, and I felt a strange, cold detachment. I was not his leader. I was not his savior. I was just a man with a gun, standing between him and the enemy.

And I was the only one who knew what that meant.

I checked my magazine one last time.

The container was full.

I was ready.

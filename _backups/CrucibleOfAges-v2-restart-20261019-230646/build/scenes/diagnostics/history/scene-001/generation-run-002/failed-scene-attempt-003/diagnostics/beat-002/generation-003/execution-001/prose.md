The argument between Diaz and O’Malley reached a crescendo that sounded less like a tactical debate and more like two men trying to break each other’s necks through a helmet. Diaz’s finger was in O’Malley’s face, tapping the side of the man’s head as if checking for a pulse, while O’Malley sputtered, spittle flying, his eyes wide and wet with a panic he was too proud to name. Miller shouted something over the din, a command that dissolved into the air before it could land, swallowed by the rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* of distant artillery that had started to chew the tree line to the left.

I kept my eye on the branch I’d hit three shots ago. It was still there, twitching slightly in the wind. I liked that branch. It was a problem I could solve. It was a thing I could make stop moving. The men around me were problems I couldn’t solve because they refused to be solved. They wanted structure. They wanted someone to tell them the world made sense, that if they just did the thing in the manual, the thing would work. I had stopped believing in the manual about six years ago, back when I was still trying to fit into the mold they were trying to pour me into.

Vance made a sound. It was a wet, gurgling exhale that vibrated in his chest. He was still on his knees, his helmet hanging off one ear, his face the color of old dishwater. He looked at me. Then he looked at Miller. Then he looked at his hands, as if expecting them to explain the sudden absence of a future.

"Vance," I said. My voice was flat. It wasn’t a shout. It was a statement of fact. "Stop looking at Miller. He’s not giving an order."

"He’s giving an order!" Vance hissed, his voice cracking. "He said hold the line!"

"Miller is having a stroke," I corrected him. "He’s not giving an order. He’s screaming. There’s a difference. You know the difference, right? You’re a smart boy. You just have a bad stomach."

Vance blinked, the terror in his eyes warring with a confused, desperate hope for any anchor he could grab. He didn’t argue. He just stared at me, waiting for the next sentence to be the one that saved him. I gave him silence.

I turned my head slightly, just enough to watch the gap in the sandbags where the treeline was exposed. The enemy shadows were still there. They were moving with a slowness that suggested they were either very brave or very stupid. Probably both. I watched one of them step out, pausing to check a map. The map was useless. He knew it was useless. But the act of looking at it was a ritual. It was a way to pretend that the chaos had geometry.

I felt a strange, cold satisfaction in my chest. It wasn’t pride. It wasn’t courage. It was the quiet, mechanical hum of a engine running on empty. I was the only one in this trench who wasn’t trying to convince myself that we were going to win. We weren’t going to win. We were going to survive, or we weren’t. And survival was a calculation I could do. Winning was a story I didn’t have to buy.

Miller stopped shouting. That was worse. He stood there, his rifle lowered, his mouth open, breathing in short, sharp gasps. He looked at Diaz, who was still pointing, and O’Malley, who had finally lowered his hand and was now pressing it against his forehead. The three of them formed a triangle of incompetence, a geometric shape I had seen a thousand times in the classroom, a thousand times on the drill field. A shape that always collapsed under the weight of reality.

I checked my watch again. 14:04. Two minutes. In two minutes, the artillery would probably stop, or it would get closer. Either way, the calculus would change. I adjusted my grip on the rifle, feeling the cool metal against my cheek. The weight of it was a comfort. It was a simple, clean thing. It didn’t ask me to be a leader. It didn’t ask me to be a soldier. It just asked me to be the one who didn’t get shot.

I glanced back at Vance. He was still looking at me. His eyes were wide, pleading. He wanted me to tell him that it was going to be okay. He wanted me to be the guy who said, *Alright boys, let’s do this, let’s pull each other out, let’s be Marines.* I could say it. It would be easy. It would cost me nothing. And it would keep him right where he was, right in the crosshairs of the next shell, right in the path of the next bullet.

"No," I said.

Vance flinched.

"I’m not coming out," I said, keeping my eyes on him. "You’re not coming out. We’re staying here. We’re waiting."

"Waiting for what?" Vance whispered.

"For them to figure out they’re dead," I said. "Or for the shells to hit us. Whichever comes first."

I looked away from him. It was easier to look at the treeline. The enemy was still there. The shadows were still moving. The world was still spinning, indifferent to our little pile of meat and metal and fear. I was ready. I was ready to be the one who didn’t get shot. I was ready to be the problem solver. And for now, that was enough.

The mud in the trench was the color of rust and smelled like wet pennies. It was a smell I had learned to associate with impending death, a sensory cue hardwired into my brain during the last six weeks of deployment. I wiped a smear of it from my cheek with the back of a gloved hand, the motion automatic, devoid of thought. My rifle, a battered M4A1 with a custom suppressor that had cost more than my first car, lay across my knees. The polymer grip was warm from my palms, the familiar weight of it an anchor in the howling chaos outside the parapet.

Beside me, to my left, was Sergeant Miller. He was a large man, built like a brick wall, with a face that had been weathered by twenty years of service until it looked carved from granite. He was currently using his left arm as a shield.

"Easy, Sarge," I said, keeping my voice low, just above the roar of the incoming fire. "You’re bleeding out."

Miller didn’t look at me. His eyes were fixed on the ridge line in the distance, where the enemy had dug in. "I’m fine, kid," he grunted. "Just a scratch. Keep your head down."

I looked at his arm. The fabric of his uniform was soaked through, a dark crimson spreading rapidly across the sleeve. Blood was pooling on the ground between us, slick and black in the dim light. He wasn't just bleeding; he was dying. It was obvious to anyone with eyes, which, in this context, meant anyone who wasn't terrified.

I didn't move to help him. I adjusted my position slightly, angling my body so that Miller’s larger frame provided a better deflection profile for any stray rounds that might curve around the trench lip. It was a tactical decision, rational and efficient. If a bullet came over the top, it would hit Miller first. He was the bigger target. He had the more robust hit box. It was simple geometry, the kind of thing we drilled in the academy until it became instinct.

I checked my magazine. Thirty rounds. That was enough ammunition for the job. I checked my scope. The scope was clear. I checked the comms. The comms were filled with static. The radio had been dead for the last twenty minutes, a casualty of the electronic warfare that had turned this sector into a blind spot on the map. We were isolated, cut off from command, cut off from extraction, cut off from the rest of the world. That was fine. Isolation meant less bureaucracy, less interference. It meant we could do what needed to be done without waiting for a green light from a major in an air-conditioned bunker three thousand miles away.

A mortar shell landed close enough to send a spray of dirt and shrapnel over the trench. I flinched, a reflex I couldn't entirely suppress, but my hands stayed steady on the rifle. Miller hissed through his teeth, wincing as the vibration jolted his broken arm.

"Stay down, kid," he warned, his voice strained. "That was close."

I looked at him, then past him. Beyond the trench, in the open field, a cluster of figures was moving. They weren't wearing uniforms. They were wearing rags, tattered clothes that offered no protection, carrying no weapons. They were civilians. They were trying to cross the no-man's-land, their movements erratic, driven by the sheer panic of survival.

"Disengage," I said, my voice flat.

Miller frowned. "What?"

"Those civilians. They’re in the kill zone. If we shoot, we hit them. If they run, they draw fire from the enemy. They’re a liability."

Miller looked at me, his eyes narrowing. "They’re people, kid."

"They’re obstacles," I corrected. "And they’re slowing us down. I can’t maintain effective overwatch if I have to worry about friendly fire incidents. I need a clean line of sight. Clear the area."

It was a command, but it was also a test. I wanted to see how far he was willing to go, how much he still cared about the bigger picture. If he was too slow, I would have to take the initiative. I had the better position, the better gear, and the clearer mind. He was a good soldier, but he was getting old. His instincts were still sharp, but his reflexes were slowing. I could see it in the way he moved, the slight lag in his reactions. It was a gap I could exploit.

A burst of automatic fire cracked from the ridge. The sound was sharp, violent, and immediate. It tore through the air, passing over our heads, and then the world exploded.

The first round hit the trench wall beside Miller’s head, kicking up a spray of dirt. The second round punched through his shoulder, the one that was still functional. He gasped, a wet, gurgling sound, and slumped forward. His arm dropped, the broken one hanging limp.

"Miller!" I shouted, the name tearing out of my throat before I could stop it.

I looked at him. He was looking at me, his eyes wide with a shock that hadn't fully registered yet. Blood was spraying from his shoulder, a bright red fountain against the dark mud. He was going to die. There was no medical kit, no extraction, no hope. He was just a body, a bag of blood and bone that had served its purpose and was now leaking its contents onto the ground.

I didn't touch him. I didn't even look at him for more than a second. My eyes were already scanning the ridge, calculating the trajectory of the next round. I was alive. I was intact. I had a full magazine and a clear view. That was what mattered.

I raised my rifle, the motion smooth and practiced. I didn't aim at the civilians. I didn't aim at the enemy. I aimed at the ground between me and Miller, a small patch of dirt that served as a barrier. If I stayed behind it, I was safe. If I moved, I was exposed.

I checked my watch. 1400 hours. We had been pinned for forty-five minutes. We had lost three men in that time. Miller was the fourth. The rest of the squad was gone, scattered by the initial barrage, their fates unknown. I was alone.

I looked at Miller one last time. His eyes were closing, the light fading from them. He didn't ask for help. He didn't ask for forgiveness. He just looked at me, with a mixture of confusion and acceptance, and then his eyes rolled back into his head.

I lowered my rifle and sat back on my heels, the mud cold against my knees. I took a deep breath, the air tasting of dust and cordite. I was calm. I was efficient. I was the only one left who could still think.

I checked my magazine again. Twenty-nine rounds. I checked my scope. The scope was clear. I checked the comms. The comms were static.

I was ready.

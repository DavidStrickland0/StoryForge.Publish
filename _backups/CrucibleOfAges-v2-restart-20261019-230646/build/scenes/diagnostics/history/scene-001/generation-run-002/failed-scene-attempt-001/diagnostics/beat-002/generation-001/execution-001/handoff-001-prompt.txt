The shell didn’t just land; it arrived with a weight that pressed the air out of my lungs and turned the mud into a slurry of wet earth and shattered concrete. It was closer than the last one, and the sound was less a roar and more a crack, like the sky splitting open and dropping a truck onto our heads. The trench shook, and a clump of dirt the size of a fist punched through the lip above me, raining down onto my helmet.

I blinked the grit from my eyes. The world was still there, but it was distorted, framed by the grey haze of dust and the acrid tang of cordite. I checked my rifle. The chamber was still open, the safety still on. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Fear was a cold thing in my gut, but my fingers didn’t tremble. I had trained for this. I had paid for this. I was going to be a captain before I was twenty-five, and this was just the first invoice on the way to that rank.

"Vance!" I barked, my voice cutting through the ringing in my ears. "Status!"

No answer.

I turned my head sharply to the left, where Miller had been sitting. Miller was gone. There was no body, no blood, no dramatic pose of a fallen hero. There was just a pile of mud and a single, muddy boot sticking out at an angle that suggested its owner had been turned inside out. The cactus stem he’d been chewing was gone, swallowed by the dirt or the blast. I felt a spike of irritation, sharper than grief. Miller was expendable. He was slow, he was loud, and he chewed with his mouth open. I needed better soldiers. I needed soldiers who didn’t let me down.

I spun back around to the right, where Vance had been huddled.

Vance was still there, but he wasn’t moving. He was slumped against the trench wall, his eyes wide and open, staring at a point somewhere in the middle distance. His rifle was in his hands, but his fingers were loose, the weapon dangling by the sling. He was making a sound, a low, wet gurgling noise, like a drain clearing.

"Vance," I said again, softer this time. "Vance, report."

He didn’t look at me. He just kept staring, his lips moving silently. I reached out and touched his shoulder. He was cold. Not the cold of the wind, but the cold of something that had stopped working. I pulled my hand back, wiping the mud on my pants. I didn’t feel anything. That was the problem. I should have felt something. I was a Marine. I was a leader. Leaders cared about their men. Or so the textbooks said. The textbooks also said that leadership was about maintaining cohesion, and cohesion required decisive action. If Vance was a liability, removing him was the most decisive action I could take. I was efficient. I was necessary.

I looked around the trench. It was empty. The other two Marines from my squad were nowhere to be seen. The enemy was still out there, in the smoke, closing in. I could hear them now, the crunch of boots in the mud, the occasional cough, the whisper of conversation. They were close. Twenty meters, maybe less.

I was alone.

The realization hit me with a physical force. I was alone in a hole in the ground, with a rifle and no plan, no backup, and no orders. My identity, the thing that had defined me since I was sixteen, was built on the idea that I was part of something. I was a node in a network, a piece of a machine. Without the machine, I was just a boy with a gun.

I stood up. My legs felt weak, but I forced them to hold. I moved to the lip of the trench, peering out into the grey. The smoke was thinning, revealing the shapes of the enemy. They were in a loose line, moving with a terrifying calm. They weren’t panicked. They weren’t shouting. They were just walking, steady and purposeful, like they owned the ground they were crossing.

I raised my rifle. My hands were shaking now. Not from fear, but from the effort of holding the weapon steady. I took a breath, and the air tasted like ash and blood. I was going to die. I knew it with a clarity that was almost peaceful. I was eighteen years old, and I was going to die in a muddy ditch in a country I didn’t understand, for a flag I didn’t believe in, saving a world I didn’t want to save.

But I was still going to shoot. I was still going to fight. I was still me.

I squeezed the trigger.

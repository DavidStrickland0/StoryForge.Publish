The mud was cold. That was the first thing my brain registered, not the fear, not the silence, but the sheer, freezing viscosity of the slurry soaking through the thin fabric of my trousers. It felt like wet cement, heavy and suffocating, pulling at every step with a suction that threatened to yank me into the earth. I hated mud. It was the great equalizer of the infantry life, the one thing that didn’t care if you were a sergeant or a private, if you had a commendation or a black mark. It just sucked. It soaked. It rotted your boots from the inside out.

I stood there, five meters from the front line of the advancing enemy, and I waited for the world to end.

My knee buckled, just slightly, a micro-adjustment my body made to redistribute the weight of my gear. The rifle on my back, still full of rounds I had no intention of firing, pressed against my shoulder blades with a solid, reassuring weight. I could feel the polymer casing, the cold metal of the barrel, the familiar geometry of the stock. It was the most comforting thing in the world, a dead object that knew exactly what it was. I, on the other hand, was a mess of conflicting impulses and sweaty palms.

The enemy was young. I could see that now that the smoke had thinned enough for me to make out the faces. They were barely older than me. Their uniforms were a different shade of green, patched and stained, their boots caked in the same mud that was currently trying to digest my feet. They moved in a loose formation, steady and purposeful, their weapons raised but not yet firing. There was a strange, eerie calm to their advance. They weren’t screaming. They weren’t charging. They were just... They were just walking.

One of them, a boy with a scar running down his cheek, looked up and met my eyes. He didn’t look angry. He didn’t look triumphant. He looked tired. He looked like he had been walking for a very long time and was just waiting for the next order to come down so he could stop thinking about what he was doing.

I wanted to tell him to go home. I wanted to tell him that this was a mistake, that we were all stupid, that none of this mattered in the grand scheme of things. But the words died in my throat. What did I have to say? I was a Marine. I was supposed to be the good guy, the one with the better equipment, the one fighting for the right. But looking at him, looking at the mud on his boots and the fear in his eyes, I realized that "right" was a very abstract concept when you were standing in the dirt with a gun.

I thought about Miller. I thought about Vance. I thought about the way Miller had laughed at my terrible joke about the coffee machine, the way Vance had quietly passed me his last cigarette when the cold got too bad. They were gone. Their bodies were somewhere in that trench, or maybe further back, depending on where the shrapnel had caught them. I would never know. I would never find out. And now I was the one standing in the open, playing hero, knowing full well that I was probably doing the most stupid thing a human being had ever done.

But I didn't care.

The boy with the scar raised his rifle. His finger moved to the trigger guard. I watched it happen in slow motion, the mechanical inevitability of it. I saw the dust motes dancing in the shafts of light that pierced the smoke. I saw the woman behind him, still watching, her face a mask of detached curiosity. She wasn't crying. She wasn't screaming. She was just looking at me, as if I were a particularly interesting insect about to be squashed.

I felt a strange sense of peace. It was absurd, of course. I was eighteen years old, covered in mud, about to be killed by a stranger I had never met. My heart was hammering against my ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage. My lungs burned with the effort of breathing. The air tasted of dust and cordite and death.

And yet, for the first time in my life, I wasn't thinking about what I would gain. I wasn't calculating the cost-benefit analysis of my survival. I wasn't wondering how this would look on my record, or how I would use this moment to climb the ladder. I was just... I was simply present in the moment. I was here, in this mud, in this moment, and I was choosing to stay.

The boy pulled the trigger.

The sound was a sharp crack, loud and final. I felt the impact before I even registered it, a heavy blow to the chest that knocked the air out of me. My eyes widened. The world tilted. The mud beneath my feet seemed to soften, as if the earth itself was trying to catch me.

I looked down. I looked at the woman. She was still watching. Her expression hadn't changed. She was still curious.

I wondered if she would remember me. I wondered if she would tell her children about the strange soldier who came out of the trench and stood in the mud, choosing to die so that she might live. I wondered if it would matter to her, or if it would just be another story, another detail in the long tapestry of her life.

It didn't matter.

I let go. I let go of the rifle, of the mud, of the fear, of the future. I let go of the boy who wanted status, the boy who wanted power, the boy who wanted to be someone. I let go of all of it, and I fell into the dark.

The air in the trench had turned thick, heavy with the metallic tang of fear and the dusty smell of the sandbags baking in the midday sun. I kept my rifle raised, the stock pressed firmly against my shoulder, the crosshair fixed on a specific knot of branches in the treeline where the enemy shadows had last flickered. It was a comfortable position, if you ignored the fact that it was the most dangerous one available. I liked the stillness of it. It gave me time to think, or at least to pretend I was thinking. In reality, I was just calculating the odds.

Miller was still shouting. His voice had lost its command cadence and taken on the ragged, high-pitched edge of a man trying to convince himself that screaming would make the world make sense. He was grabbing at Diaz’s arm, shaking him, while O’Malley stood with his back to the wall, hands raised in a gesture that looked less like a surrender and more like he was trying to explain to a confused dog why the house was on fire.

"Left flank is gone!" Miller roared, spittle flying in the bright light. "They’re around us, Diaz! They’re fucking all around us!"

Diaz didn’t answer. He was staring at the ground, his eyes wide and unblinking, tracking something invisible in the dirt. O’Malley made a sound, a low, guttural groan, and slid down the side of the sandbag stack until he was sitting in the mud, his rifle lying across his lap like a dead limb.

I watched them. I didn’t join in. I didn’t offer comfort or tactical advice. I just watched, and I waited, and I kept my finger near the trigger without touching it.

Vance was the only one who still looked like he was trying to be a Marine. He was on his knees, his helmet finally falling off and rolling into the dirt. He looked up at me, his face a mask of wet sweat and terror.

"Tell me to go," Vance whispered. It wasn’t a question. It was a plea. He wanted me to give him an order, something concrete, something that would anchor him to reality. "Says to go, Sarge. Just tell me to go."

I looked at him. He was shaking, a fine tremor running through his limbs that made his hands useless. He was a kid. He was eighteen, which meant he was exactly my age, but he had none of the armor I had built around myself. He saw the battle as a catastrophe; I saw it as a problem with a specific, solvable equation. And the solution had nothing to do with holding a line that no longer existed.

"Stay down," I said. My voice was flat. "Wait for the order to run."

"But—"

"Stay. Down." I said it again, sharper this time. "If I tell you to move, you move. If I don’t, you stay here and you stay alive. Do you understand me?"

Vance swallowed hard, his throat working. He nodded, slowly, his eyes dropping to the ground. He was buying it. He needed it to be true. I let him believe it. It was easier to manage a static problem than a dynamic one.

Miller turned on me. His eyes were red-rimmed, the bloodshot whites stark against his pale skin. He pointed a shaking finger at me, his face twisted in a mixture of rage and desperation.

"Get up there, rookie!" he barked. "Help me coordinate a fallback! We need to consolidate, we need to pull back to the ridgeline, we need to—"

"We don't," I interrupted.

The silence that followed was heavier than the gunfire. Miller’s mouth opened and closed, the words dying on his tongue. He didn’t expect me to talk back. In the old days, back at the base, I was the guy who ran drills, who cleaned the rifles, who kept the equipment in order. I was the reliable one. I was the guy who didn’t make waves.

Now I was looking at him, and I was telling him the truth.

"Look at them, Miller," I said, nodding toward Diaz and O’Malley. "Look at what they’re doing. You can’t order them to do anything. You can’t even order them to think. The formation is gone. The command structure is gone. The only thing left is me, you, and two guys who are waiting to die or run, and you don’t know which. So stop shouting. You’re only making it harder for the other side to miss us."

Miller stared at me. For a second, I thought he might hit me. He had that look, the one where the authority in his head was warring with the reality in front of him. Then, slowly, the fight went out of him. His shoulders slumped. He lowered his rifle, letting it hang by his side, and leaned back against the sandbags, closing his eyes. He looked suddenly very old. Very tired.

"I don't know what to do," he whispered. It was a small, broken sound, barely audible over the distant crack of weapons in the trees.

I didn’t offer him sympathy. Sympathy was a luxury I couldn’t afford. I just looked at the treeline, keeping my aim steady.

"Then don't do anything," I said. "Just wait. The battle is already over. We’re just waiting for the other side to realize it."

Miller didn’t answer. He just sat there, his eyes closed, his breathing shallow and rapid. Diaz had stopped staring at the ground and was now hugging his knees, his face buried in his arms. O’Malley was rocking back and forth, murmuring to himself, his lips moving in a rhythm I couldn’t hear. Vance was still on his knees, watching me, waiting for the order to run that I hadn’t given yet.

I was alone.

That was the strange thing. I was surrounded by men, by equipment, by the machinery of war, and I was more alone than I had ever been in my life. The institutional support, the rank, the chain of command, the sense of belonging to something larger than myself—it had all evaporated in the last ten minutes. What was left was just me, and my rifle, and the cold, hard fact that I was the only one in this trench who was still thinking clearly.

I checked my watch. 14:03. One minute had passed. It felt like an hour.

I looked at the magazine in my rifle. Twenty rounds. Enough to kill two or three enemies, if I was lucky. Enough to save my own skin, if I was fast. I didn't know what I was doing anymore. I didn't know who I was anymore. I was just a guy in a trench, waiting for the world to break.

And I was ready for it to break. I had been ready for it to break for a long time. I just hadn't known how to ask for it.

The sun was high and bright, casting sharp shadows through the leaves of the trees. The light was indifferent. It didn't care about Miller or Vance or Diaz or O’Malley. It didn't care about me. It just was.

I kept my aim on the treeline. I didn't blink. I didn't breathe. I just watched, and I waited, and I listened to the silence that was growing around me, the silence that was going to be the loudest thing I ever heard.

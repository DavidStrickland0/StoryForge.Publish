The round didn’t hit anyone. It kicked up a spray of mud and debris three meters to the left, and the enemy didn’t even flinch. They kept walking. That was the worst part. In the movies, when the bad guys see a guy standing up in a ditch with a gun, they run or they return fire. They didn’t do either. They just kept coming, calm and purposeful, like they were walking to a coffee shop.

I fired again. And again. My hands were moving on their own, muscle memory doing the work while my brain screamed at me to stop. I was wasting bullets. I was wasting time. I was going to die, and I was doing it in the most unimpressive way possible. Standing in the mud, shaking, firing a rifle at a group of men who were probably thinking about lunch.

Then I saw her.

She was behind them. Or maybe beside them. It was hard to tell with the smoke and the dust. She was small, dressed in clothes that looked too clean for this environment, her hair pinned up in a way that suggested she hadn’t expected to be in a war zone. She was looking at me. Not with fear, exactly. With a kind of detached curiosity. Like I was a bug she’d stepped on and was now wondering why it was still moving.

She was a civilian. A noncombatant. The kind of person who was supposed to be in a bunker, or in a city, or somewhere safe. But here she was, in the line of fire, standing in the middle of a battlefield that had no business having a civilian in it.

And something inside me snapped.

I didn’t know why. I was an eighteen-year-old Marine who had joined the service because I wanted to feel important, because I wanted to escape the smallness of my life, because I wanted to be someone who could make other people afraid. I was selfish. I was ambitious. I was willing to use weakness for personal benefit. I had spent my whole life calculating the cost of every action, the return on every investment. I didn’t save people. I didn’t care about people. I cared about myself.

But looking at her, I felt a surge of something that didn’t fit. It wasn’t bravery. It wasn’t duty. It was a cold, hard certainty that if I let her die, I would never be able to look at myself in the mirror again. And I hated myself for feeling it. I hated that my instinct was to protect, to shield, to sacrifice. I hated that my body was already moving before my mind had finished the thought.

I stopped shooting.

The enemy was closer now. Ten meters. The enemy was five meters away. I could see their faces. They were young, too. Older than me, maybe, but young enough that they looked scared. They didn’t look like monsters. They looked like people. And that made it worse.

I put my rifle on my back. It felt heavy, like it was made of lead and regret. I stepped out of the trench. The mud sucked at my boots, trying to hold me down. I ignored it. I walked toward the enemy, toward the line of fire, toward the woman who was watching me with those detached, curious eyes.

I didn’t know what I was going to do. I didn’t have a plan. I didn’t have a weapon. I had a full magazine in my rifle, but I wasn’t going to use it. I was going to stand between them. I was going to be the wall. I was going to be the shield.

I was going to die.

And for the first time in my life, I didn’t care.

The first round cracked the earth three meters to my left, kicking up a spray of red clay that stung my eyes. I blinked, the grit burning on my eyelashes, and raised the rifle again. My hands were no longer shaking. They had gone rigid, locked in a vice-like grip that felt less like muscle memory and more like the weapon had fused to my palms.

I squeezed the trigger. The rifle bucked hard against my shoulder, a violent kick that rattled my teeth. The muzzle flash bloomed in the gray haze, a stark, violent white against the dirt. I didn’t watch where the bullet went. There was no time to watch, no time to count hits, no time to feel the satisfaction of a confirmed kill. There was only the next round.

I cycled the bolt. The action felt gritty, thick with dust and dried mud. I chambered another round. The sound was deafening in the sudden silence of the trench, a sharp *clack* that echoed off the concrete walls.

And then I saw her.

She was standing in the open, just beyond the trench lip, maybe ten meters away. She wasn’t wearing armor. She wasn’t holding a weapon. She was wearing a dress, a light fabric that clung to her in the humid air, and she was holding a small, dark object to her chest. A child? No, it was a bundle of cloth. A bag. She was looking at the enemy, her eyes wide, her mouth open in a silent scream that I couldn’t hear over the ringing in my ears.

The enemy had stopped. They were twenty meters out, frozen in that strange, calm formation. One of them, a man with a scarred face and a rifle slung over his back, turned his head. He looked at me. Then he looked at her.

It was a simple calculation. I could see it in the way his eyes moved, the slight tilt of his head. He was deciding. Deciding whether I was worth the effort, whether I was a threat, or just another obstacle. And he was deciding that she was in the way.

My training kicked in. *Assess the threat. Identify the target. Eliminate.*

But my hands were still locked on the rifle. My finger was on the trigger. And for a second, a fraction of a second, I didn’t move.

I looked at her. She was shaking. Her whole body was trembling, a high-frequency vibration that made her look like a leaf in a storm. She was terrified. She was alone. And she was going to die.

I didn’t know why I cared. I didn’t know who she was. I didn’t know why she was there. I didn’t even know if the child in her arms was alive. None of it mattered. The logic was gone. The strategy was gone. The Marine was gone.

There was just the girl with the bag, and the man with the rifle, and the space between them.

I lowered the rifle.

It was the wrong move. Every instinct I had, every lesson I’d ever learned, every scar I carried on my body, screamed at me to keep shooting. To keep firing. To make her a memory before the enemy could. But I lowered the rifle. I let it hang at my side, heavy and useless.

I stepped out of the trench.

The mud sucked at my boots as I climbed over the lip. The air was thick with dust and the smell of cordite. I walked toward her. My legs felt like they belonged to someone else, heavy and clumsy. I could feel the enemy’s eyes on me, a dozen pairs of eyes, cold and calculating. I could feel the weight of their rifles, the potential energy in their fingers.

I reached her. She flinched when I got close, her body twisting away from me. I ignored it. I turned, placing my back to her. I spread my arms. I stood there, a human shield, a target, a wall of meat and bone and stubbornness.

I looked back at the enemy. They were still standing there. The scarred man was watching me. He didn’t raise his rifle. He didn’t make a move. He just watched, with that same calm, purposeful stillness.

I waited.

My heart was hammering against my ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage. My breath was shallow, rasping in my throat. I could feel the sweat running down my spine, cooling in the air. I was going to die. I knew it with a clarity that was almost peaceful. I was eighteen years old, and I was going to die in a muddy ditch in a country I didn’t understand, for a flag I didn’t believe in, saving a world I didn’t want to save.

But I was still standing. I was still there. I was still me.

The scarred man raised his rifle.

I didn’t close my eyes. I didn’t pray. I didn’t think of my mother, or my father, or the girl in the bar in Quantico who had laughed at my jokes. I just looked at him. I held my ground. I held her.

The rifle fired.

The sound was a crack, a sharp, final punctuation to the sentence of my life. The bullet hit me. It hit me in the chest, a hot, heavy impact that pushed me backward. I felt the air leave my lungs in a violent rush. I felt the force of it, the sheer, undeniable power of the lead.

I didn’t fall. I couldn’t fall. Not yet. I was still holding her. I was still standing.

The enemy advanced. They were moving now, fast, purposeful. They were coming for me. They were coming for her.

I closed my eyes.

The darkness came.

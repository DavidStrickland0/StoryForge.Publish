The whistle blew. It was a high, thin shriek that cut through the low rumble of the distant guns, and for a second, the only thing I heard was the blood rushing in my ears. Then the world fell apart.

I didn’t wait for the order to go over the top. I didn’t have to. The ground itself seemed to reject the idea of staying put. Around me, the trench line erupted in a chaotic scramble of mud, metal, and screaming men. Miller was gone. One moment he was hunched over his rifle, his hands shaking so violently he couldn’t keep the stock steady; the next, a shell landed close enough to the left that the shockwave lifted him off his feet. I saw him fly, a ragdoll of uniform and flesh, before he crashed back down into the dirt. He didn’t move. I didn’t look back. There was no time for looking back, and more importantly, there was no point. He was a resource that had been expended. I was the asset that still had value.

I hit the lip of the trench and rolled forward, my boots slipping on the slick, churned earth. The air was thick with chalky dust, a suffocating gray fog that coated my tongue and stung my eyes. I blinked it away, my vision blurring, and pushed forward. The plan, whatever it had been, was gone. The artillery had adjusted its fire, but it hadn’t adjusted it for me. It had adjusted it for the enemy, and in doing so, it had turned the open ground into a killing field.

I was running blind.

My training kicked in, but it felt like trying to steer a car with a broken wheel. I knew the rules. Keep your head down. Move in bursts. Watch for suppressive fire. But the enemy wasn’t firing in bursts. They were firing everywhere. Tracers zipped past me, leaving bright red streaks in the dusty air. I dove into a crater, the impact jarring my teeth, and lay there, gasping, my rifle raised.

I was alone.

The thought hit me harder than the shockwave. I was alone. The squad was scattered. The lieutenant I had pushed to the front line to secure my own safety was somewhere in the mud, dead or dying. The radio was static. The chain of command was a ghost. I was just a man in a hole, holding a piece of metal, waiting for the world to end.

I checked my magazine. The magazine was full. I checked my grenades. I had four grenades left. I checked my watch. The hands were trembling, or maybe I was. Time had lost its meaning. The only thing that mattered was the next second. The next breath. The next step.

I stood up. My knees felt like they were filled with sand. I looked around. The trench line was empty. The men who had been there moments ago were gone. Some were running. Some were lying still. I couldn’t tell the difference. The dust was too thick. The noise was too loud.

I was supposed to be a leader. I was supposed to be the man who knew what to do. But I didn’t know what to do. I was an eighteen-year-old kid from a small town in Ohio, and I was about to die in a hole in the ground.

I tightened my grip on the rifle. My knuckles were white. I could feel the sweat running down my back, soaking through my uniform. I was cold and hot at the same time. I was terrified. I was angry. I was alive.

I started to move. I didn’t have a destination. I didn’t have a mission. I just had a direction. Away from the crater. Away from the dust. Away from the feeling of being trapped.

I moved through the mud, my boots sinking with every step. The tracers were getting closer. I could see them now, bright lines of light cutting through the gray. I dropped to one knee, moving in short, sharp bursts. I was fast. I was strong. I was the best.

I was lying to myself.

I was scared. I was alone. And I was running.

The next burst of fire hit the ground in front of me. Dirt sprayed up into my face. I coughed, choking on the dust, and kept moving. I didn’t stop. I couldn’t stop. If I stopped, I would die. If I moved, I might live.

I was a Marine. I was a survivor. I was the man who knew what to do.

And I was alone.

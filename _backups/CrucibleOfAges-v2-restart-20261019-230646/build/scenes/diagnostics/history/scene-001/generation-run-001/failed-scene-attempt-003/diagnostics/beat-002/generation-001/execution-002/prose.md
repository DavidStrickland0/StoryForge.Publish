The shadow in the window was a blur, indistinct against the grey afternoon light, but it had been there. My finger tightened on the trigger guard, not pulling, just holding the tension. It was a reflex, the muscle memory of a man who expected the world to try and kill him every three seconds. I waited for the glass to shatter, for the muzzle flash, for the sound of someone trying to prove they were still alive.

Nothing happened. The shadow didn’t reappear. The window remained a dark rectangle in the crumbling brick, framed by twisted rebar and shattered panes that glittered like cheap jewelry in the dust.

I let out a breath I didn’t realize I’d been holding. The silence in the room was heavy, pressing against my eardrums, broken only by the faint, rhythmic thrum of distant mortar fire. It was a steady beat, almost musical, a reminder that the war was still happening, just far enough away to be background noise.

"Clear," I said, my voice flat. I keyed my radio. "Diaz, status."

Static.

A burst of white noise crackled in my ear, then silence. I tapped the mic. "Diaz, do you copy?"

More static. A low, humming whine that sounded like the radio was dying, or maybe just the battery giving up the ghost. I checked the indicator light. It was blinking red, a slow, morbid pulse. Battery low. Or the signal was down. Given the way the building was shaking, the latter seemed more likely. The comms tower across the street had been hit twenty minutes ago, and I hadn’t heard a word from command since.

I looked at Diaz. He was still in the other room, a few feet away, crouched near the wall where the woman sat. He hadn’t moved. He was watching the window, too. His face was pale, the skin stretched tight over his cheekbones, and his eyes were wide, searching the glass with a desperate, frantic intensity. He looked young. He looked like he was eighteen, or maybe nineteen, but in this moment, he looked like a kid who had been told a monster was in the closet and was trying to convince himself the closet door was locked.

I raised my rifle and pointed it at the window. I didn’t fire. Firing would bring attention. Firing would make me a target. I just held the line of sight, a silent promise that if anything moved again, it would be the last thing it saw.

"Radio’s dead," I said, more to myself than to him. "We’re on our own."

Diaz flinched. He didn’t look at me. He kept his eyes on the window. "We should go," he said. His voice was thin, strained. "We need to get out of here. The building’s unstable."

"I’m not leaving them," I said. "I’m going to get them out. You cover me."

"I can’t—" Diaz started, but I cut him off.

"You can. You’re good at covering. You’re good at not thinking too hard about the consequences. Just do it."

I moved. I didn’t run; running drew attention. I moved with the slow, deliberate grace of a predator, keeping low, keeping my rifle up. The woman was still sitting on the floor, holding the child. She hadn’t moved. She was staring at the window, her face a mask of terror, but her body was frozen, locked in a state of shock that was more efficient than courage. She was waiting for the bullet.

I reached her. I didn’t touch her. I just stood there, a wall between her and the glass. I could feel the heat radiating off the brick behind me, the residual warmth of the building’s destruction. I could smell the dust, the dry, choking powder that coated my throat.

"Get up," I said. I didn’t raise my voice. I didn’t have to.

She didn’t move.

I looked at Diaz. He was still crouched, his rifle trained on the window, his hands shaking. He was trying to be brave. He was trying to be a Marine. But he was failing. He was just a boy in a uniform, watching a stranger die in slow motion.

"Get up," I said again, louder this time. I snapped my fingers. "Now."

She blinked. Her eyes focused on me. She looked at my mask, at the black visor, at the rifle in my hand. She saw the threat. She saw the danger. And she moved.

She stood up. Her legs were trembling, but she stood. She held the child tighter, pulling the small body against her chest. The child was asleep, or pretending to be. The child didn’t care about the war. The child only cared about the warmth of the woman’s arms.

"Go," I said. I pointed to the door. "That way. Fast."

She ran. She didn’t look back. She ran past Diaz, who flinched as if she had hit him, and disappeared into the hallway. The child was still in her arms, a small, silent bundle.

I waited. I counted to five. I listened. I heard the sound of her footsteps, quick and frantic, fading into the distance. I heard the sound of the door closing. I heard the sound of the latch clicking.

I turned to Diaz.

"Cover me," I said.

He nodded. His face was blank. He had no expression left. He was empty, a shell of a man who had been hollowed out by the weight of his own survival.

I moved to the door. I opened it. The hallway was dark, choked with dust. The air was thick, heavy with the smell of burnt plastic and old blood. I stepped out.

I closed the door behind me.

I was alone.

The hallway was a tunnel of ruin. Cracks ran up the walls like spiderwebs, and chunks of plaster hung from the ceiling, threatening to fall. The floor was a mess of debris, broken glass, and twisted metal. I had to step over it, navigate it, find a path.

I moved. I kept my rifle up. I kept my eyes on the windows. I kept my ears open.

But there was no one else.

The radio was dead. The unit was gone. The institution that had defined me, the structure that had given me purpose, was silent. I was just a man in a mask, holding a gun, in a building that was falling apart.

I was alone.

I walked. I didn’t know where I was going. I didn’t know what I was supposed to do. I didn’t know who I was supposed to be.

I was just a Marine.

And Marines don’t get lost.

I kept walking.

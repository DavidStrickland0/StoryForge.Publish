The mud in the trench was cold, seeping through the rubber soles of my boots and into the bone of my ankle. It was a miserable, gray slurry that smelled of wet earth and old blood, a perfect backdrop for the kind of Tuesday I had been dreading since the chutes dropped. I adjusted the strap of my rifle, feeling the familiar weight of the M4 against my shoulder. It was a good rifle. Reliable. Deadly. And right now, it was the only thing standing between me and becoming a statistic in some general’s quarterly report.

"Vega, you’ve got a target, left flank, forty meters," I said, my voice steady over the comms. My tone was flat, efficient. I wasn’t asking. I was telling.

Vega’s head popped up from the sandbag line next to me. He was young, barely out of boot camp, with a face that looked like it hadn’t finished growing. He blinked, his eyes wide and darting around the trench like a trapped rat. "Copy that, Sergeant. Scanning."

"Scan faster, kid. They don’t take a break for us to find cover." I leaned back against the muddy wall, crossing my arms. I was wearing a tactical vest that weighed more than Vega, and I didn’t like it. I preferred to move light, to be the one who could outmaneuver the opposition. Vega, on the other hand, was a tank. Slow, steady, and utterly useless if he didn’t have someone to tell him where to look.

"Sir," Vega said, his voice cracking slightly. "Do we have eyes on the enemy?"

I glanced at him. "I am the eyes, Vega. You are the hands. Keep them clean."

He swallowed hard, his Adam’s apple bobbing in his throat. "Yes, sir."

"Good." I turned back to the slit in the sandbags. Beyond the wire, the battlefield was a haze of smoke and dust. Somewhere in that gray blur, there were men who wanted to kill me. Men who had families, probably. Men who had mothers who worried about them. I didn’t care. Their mothers weren’t paying my salary. Their mothers weren’t the ones who had to file the paperwork when someone died.

A mortar shell screamed overhead, a high-pitched whine that drilled into my teeth. I didn’t flinch. I waited for the impact, for the ground to shake, for the dust to settle. It did. Nothing happened to us. Just a crater ten meters to our left. A near miss. Lucky, or bad aim. I preferred to think it was bad aim.

"Vega," I said. "You feel lucky?"

He didn’t answer. He was staring at the crater, his face pale.

"Say it, son. It builds character."

"I... I think I'm just tired, Sergeant," he muttered.

"Tired is better than dead. And right now, you're not dead. So, you're doing fine." I tapped the side of my helmet. "Stay sharp. The next one won't miss."

He wasn't listening. He was shaking. Not with cold, but with fear. I could see it in the way his hands trembled on his rifle. Fear was a useful tool, I decided. It kept people honest. It kept them following orders. If Vega was scared, he would do exactly what I told him to do, without question, without complaint. It was efficient.

I looked down the line of the trench. There were five of us in this section. Me, Vega, and three others. Miller, who was smoking a cigarette despite the no-smoking orders because he claimed it calmed his nerves. Chen, who was cleaning his gear with a precision that bordered on obsessive. And Torres, who was just sitting there, staring at the sky, his eyes distant.

"Torres," I said. "You with us?"

He blinked, pulling himself back to the present. "Yeah, Sarge. Just thinking."

"About what?"

"About home," he said. "About my daughter. She has a birthday party this weekend."

I looked at him. He was a good soldier. Strong, reliable, no complaints. But he had a weakness. Family. Emotions. Things that made you predictable. Things that made you exploitable.

"Keep her in your head, Torres," I said. "But don't let her get in the way of your job. If you die here, she won't get a birthday party. She'll get a flag and a pension. And that's not the same thing."

He looked down at his hands. "I know, Sarge."

"Good. Then stop staring at the sky and start checking your ammo."

He nodded, and I watched him do it. I saw the way his shoulders slumped, the way his eyes darkened. I felt a pang of something, a flicker of empathy, but I crushed it immediately. Empathy was a luxury I couldn't afford. Not here. Not now.

The battle had been going on for three hours. We had taken some ground, lost some men. The enemy was pushing back, trying to break our line. We were holding, but it was getting harder. The support was slow to arrive. The reinforcements were always late, always just out of reach. And that was when the real work began.

I pulled out my radio. "Command, this is Vega. We are taking heavy pressure on the left flank. Requesting immediate air support and medical evacuation for two wounded. Over."

Static.

"Command, do you copy? We are in danger. Over."

Static.

I frowned. I checked the frequency. It was correct. I checked the battery. It was full. I looked at Vega, who was watching me with a mixture of hope and fear.

"Maybe they're busy," I said, though I knew that wasn't true. Command was never busy. Command was always waiting for something else to happen first.

"Sir, the enemy is advancing," Vega said. "I can see them. They're close."

I looked out the slit. He was right. The smoke was clearing, and I could see the shapes of men moving through the mud. They were fast, coordinated. They weren't the ragged militia we had been expecting. They were professional.

"Vega, give me your rifle."

He hesitated. "Sergeant?"

"Your rifle. Now."

He handed it to me. I checked the magazine. Full. I handed it back to him. "Stay down. I'm going to take a look."

I crawled out of the trench, my body low to the ground. The mud was cold and slick. I moved quickly, using the cover of the sandbags to stay out of sight. I reached the edge of the trench and peeked out.

The enemy was twenty meters away. They had rifles, too. And they were aiming at us.

I pulled the trigger.

The first shot hit a man in the chest. He fell. The second shot missed. The third hit another man in the head. He dropped.

I kept firing, controlled, precise. I wasn't in a hurry. I had time. I had ammunition. And I had the high ground.

But then I saw the mother.

She was running. Alone. No weapon. No uniform. Just a woman, in a torn dress, her hair wild, her face covered in dirt and blood. She was running toward the trench, toward us.

I stopped firing.

"Vega!" I shouted. "Get a weapon!"

He didn't answer. He was still down in the trench, shaking.

I looked at the mother. She was looking at me. Her eyes were wide, filled with terror. She was reaching for the sandbags, trying to climb up.

I looked at the enemy. They were reloading.

I looked at the mother. She was still running.

I made a decision.

I stood up.

I was exposed. I was a target. I was an idiot.

But I was also a Marine. And Marines don't leave people behind.

I raised my rifle and aimed at the enemy. I was going to stop them. I was going to save her.

And then the world went white.

I was counting my steps. One, two, three. The rhythm was a comfort. It kept the noise in my head at a manageable level, a mechanical tick-tock that drowned out the ringing in my ears. My boots crunched against the broken asphalt, kicking up dust that tasted like ash and burnt plastic. The air was thick with it, a grey haze that coated the back of my throat. I didn’t cough. Marines don’t cough when they’re trying to look like they know what they’re doing. I adjusted the strap on my pack, feeling the weight of the rifle against my back, and kept moving toward the extraction point.

Diaz was behind me. Or he had been.

I checked the radio. The radio emitted only static. The usual white noise of a frequency that was supposed to be clear but wasn’t. I tapped the side of my helmet, a nervous habit I’d picked up in boot camp and never quite lost. “Sarge, you copy? We’re moving on the extraction zone. Over.”

Nothing. Just the hiss.

I stopped. The silence was louder than the static. It pressed against my eardrums, a physical weight. I turned around, scanning the ruins. The street was a graveyard of shattered concrete and twisted rebar. Smoke curled from the wreckage of a bus that had lost its wheels. The sun was still high, but the light seemed filtered through the dust, turning the world into a sepia-toned nightmare.

“Diaz,” I said. My voice sounded strange in my own head, hollow and distant. “Where the hell are you?”

No answer.

I moved faster, my boots slapping hard against the ground. I checked the alleyways, the shattered storefronts, the broken cars. I found nothing. Just the debris. Just the silence.

I felt a cold knot tighten in my stomach. It wasn’t fear, exactly. Fear was a clean emotion, something you could identify and manage. This was something else. It was the feeling of a missing piece in a puzzle you were supposed to have solved. It was the realization that the world was bigger and more chaotic than my training had prepared me for.

I ran.

I ran until my lungs burned and my legs felt like lead. I ran through the streets, dodging the shells and the shrapnel that whistled past my head. I didn’t look back. There was nothing to see. Just the ruins. Just the dust.

Finally, I reached the extraction point. It was a clearing in the woods, a small patch of green amidst the brown and grey of the destroyed city. The helicopter was there, its rotors spinning, the blades cutting through the air with a deafening roar.

I ran toward it, my heart pounding in my chest. I could see the soldiers on board, their faces obscured by their helmets and goggles. One of them waved me on.

I climbed into the back of the helicopter, my legs shaking. I dropped to my knees, my rifle across my lap. I was breathing hard, my chest heaving. I looked up at the soldiers.

“Where is Diaz?” I asked.

The soldier closest to me looked at me. His face was expressionless. He didn’t answer.

I looked at the other soldiers. They were looking at the ground. They were looking at their hands. They were looking at anything but me.

“Where is Diaz?” I asked again, my voice rising.

One of the soldiers turned to look at me. He pulled his helmet off, revealing a face that was pale and drawn. He looked tired. He looked scared.

“He’s dead,” the soldier said.

The words hung in the air, suspended between us.

“Dead,” I repeated. The word felt foreign in my mouth. It didn’t fit. Diaz was young. He was funny. He was scared, yes, but he was alive. He was here.

“Hit in the hallway,” the soldier said. “When you came out. We didn’t see it happen. We were focused on the extraction. We’re sorry, Sarge.”

I stared at him. I wanted to scream. I wanted to hit him. I wanted to shake him until he told me it was a joke. But I didn’t move. I just stared at him, my eyes burning.

The helicopter lifted off, the wind from the rotors whipping my hair and clothes. I looked out the open door, watching the city shrink below me. The ruins, the smoke, the dust. It all looked so small. So insignificant.

I was alone.

The realization hit me like a physical blow. I was alone in a world that didn’t care about me. I was alone in a body that was failing me. I was alone in a life that I hadn’t chosen.

The helicopter banked, turning away from the city. The sun was setting, the sky turning a deep, bruised purple. I watched the city disappear into the darkness, and I felt a strange, cold calm settle over me.

It wasn’t relief. It wasn’t grief. It was just a void. A hole in the center of my chest where my heart should have been.

I closed my eyes. The darkness inside my head was louder than the wind. It was a darkness that had no end. A darkness that had no light.

And in that darkness, I felt something shift. Something break.

I didn’t know what it was. I didn’t want to know. I just knew that I was different now. I was broken.

And for the first time in my life, I didn’t care.

The order came through the radio with a burst of static that sounded like a gun firing in a tin can. "Move out. All elements. Now."

My stomach dropped, a physical sensation that had nothing to do with the adrenaline pumping through my veins. Twenty minutes had evaporated. I was still standing, my boots caked in chalky dust, my mind still trying to process the taste of the protein bar. Around me, the trench was waking up. The lethargy of the wait was being forcibly stripped away by the urgency of the command. I saw Miller fumble for his harness, his hands shaking so badly he dropped his magazine. He cursed, a high, thin sound that got lost in the growing murmur of activity.

I moved. I didn't run; running made you a target, and I had no desire to be a target before I had covered myself. I fell into a crouch, moving toward the breach in the parapet that served as our exit. The air outside the trench was different here, heavier, charged with the metallic tang of cordite and the fine, pervasive dust that had settled over the field.

The battle was a mess. That was the only word that fit. There was no clean line of advance, no coordinated push that the briefing slides had promised. Instead, there was a chaotic churn of men, mud, and metal. We were supposed to be taking the ridge, a gentle slope that offered a clear field of fire and a tactical advantage. What we found was a graveyard of shattered earth and a fog of smoke that rolled across the valley floor like a living thing.

I scanned the area, my eyes tracking the movement of shadows in the grey haze. Where were our supporting elements? The mortar teams should have been softening the target area before we moved, but all I could hear was the distant, rhythmic thud of artillery adjusting its fire. It was a slow, ponderous sound, like a giant hammer striking an anvil miles away. It wasn't the rapid, suppressive fire we needed. It was something else. It was the sound of a system trying to correct an error it couldn’t see.

"Thorne!" A voice shouted from behind me. I turned to see Corporal Vance, his face a mask of dirt and sweat, his eyes wide and bloodshot. He wasn't looking at the enemy. He was looking at the sky, at the trails of smoke that arced lazily across the grey. "They’re short. They’re way the hell short."

I looked past him. A shell screamed overhead, a high-pitched whistle that drilled into my brainstem. It impacted with a dull *thud* behind us, closer than comfortable, closer than safe. The ground vibrated under my feet. A spray of dirt and debris erupted from the trench wall three meters back.

"We’re moving," I said. My voice was flat, detached. I was already calculating the risks. If the artillery was firing short, it meant their targeting solution was wrong, or the wind had shifted, or the range was miscalculated. Any of those meant the next round would be closer. The one after that would be on top of us.

"Vance, get your men moving. Miller, stay with me." I grabbed Miller’s shoulder, pulling him forward. He was pale, his lips white, and he was staring at the sky with a dawning, animal terror. He didn't move. He was frozen, his body locked in a state of primitive survival response that his training hadn't been able to override.

"Move!" I shouted, shoving him toward the breach.

We spilled out into the open. The moment my feet hit the churned mud, I felt exposed. There was no cover, no concealment, just the vast, empty expanse of the valley and the smoke that drifted lazily through the air. I checked my rifle, the familiar weight of it a small anchor in the chaos. I was alive. I was functional. I was here.

Then the second shell hit.

It landed fifty meters to our left, a direct impact that turned the earth into a cloud of dust and shrapnel. I felt the shockwave in my chest, a solid punch that knocked the wind out of me. My ears rang, a high-pitched tone that drowned out all other sound. For a moment, the world went silent.

When the dust settled, the landscape had changed. A crater had formed in the mud, a dark, jagged hole that steamed in the cool air. And I was still there. But I wasn't alone in the open.

Vance was gone.

I looked to my left, where he had been standing. There was a pile of debris, a tangle of twisted metal and broken wood. And a piece of uniform, red and dark, draped over a protruding rock. I didn't know if it was him. I didn't want to know. The fear that coiled in my gut told me he was dead, killed by a round that was supposed to hit the enemy.

I looked to my right. Miller was on his knees in the mud, his rifle clutched to his chest, his face buried in his arms. He was shaking, a full-body tremor that shook the mud loose from his uniform. He was crying, or maybe screaming, I couldn't tell with my ears still ringing. He was a liability. He was a weight that would slow me down, a burden I could not afford to carry.

I should have left him. I should have grabbed my gear and moved to higher ground, to safety, to a place where I could survive. That was the training. That was the logic. Leave the dead and the dying, keep moving.

But I didn't. I stood there, frozen, my hand on my rifle, my mind reeling. The institutional support, the rank, the equipment, the plan. It was all gone. The army had dropped us, the artillery had missed, and the men I had relied on were either dead or broken. I was alone.

I looked down at Miller. He was still shaking, still weeping. He was useless. He was a problem. And I was the only one left who could think, who could act, who could survive.

I took a step forward. Then another. I moved through the mud, my boots sinking into the wet earth. I reached Miller and pulled him to his feet. He didn't fight me. He didn't speak. He just hung on me, his weight dead against my side.

I was alone. And I was afraid.

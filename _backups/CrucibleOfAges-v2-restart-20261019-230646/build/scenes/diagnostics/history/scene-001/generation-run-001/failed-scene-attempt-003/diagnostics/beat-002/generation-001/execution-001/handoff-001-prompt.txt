The shadow in the window was a trick of the light, or maybe a piece of torn plaster shifting in the draft. My eyes tracked it for a second, waiting for it to move again, but the glass remained dark and still. I let out a breath I didn’t realize I’d been holding, my shoulders dropping a fraction of an inch.

"Done?" I asked, my voice flat.

Diaz didn’t answer. He was standing by the door, his helmet slightly askew, his eyes wide and fixed on something just above my head. He looked like a kid who’d just realized the teacher was actually listening to his lie.

"Diaz," I repeated, louder this time. "Did you finish the sweep?"

He swallowed. The sound was audible in the quiet room, a dry click in his throat. "Sir, the radio is... it's dead."

My first thought was annoyance. Not fear, not concern, just the pure, irritating friction of a tool failing me when I needed it. I tapped the side of my helmet. "I heard you. The radio is dead. Do you have a spare battery?"

"I checked. It’s gone, sir. No signal."

I frowned, tilting my head. "No signal? We’re on the net. We’ve been on the net for two hours. If you say there’s no signal, you’re lying, and if you’re lying, I’m going to have to reconsider your usefulness to me."

Diaz flinched. It was a small movement, barely noticeable, but I saw it. He hated me. I could see it in the way he held his rifle, gripping it too tightly, his knuckles white. He was terrified of me, and he was terrified of the world outside that window. Those two fears were keeping him in line, but they were also eating away at him.

"I’m not lying, sir," he said, his voice tight. "The comms officer went down in the hallway. I saw him. He didn’t get up. And the channel... it’s just static. Or silence. I can’t tell the difference anymore."

I looked at him for a long moment. I wanted to believe him. It would be easier to believe him than to accept the implication. If the comms were down, if the support was gone, then we were alone. And I did not like being alone. I liked the structure, the chain of command, the clear lines of authority that told me exactly what to do and exactly who was responsible for my safety. Being alone meant I had to make the calls myself, and I wasn’t sure I was built for that.

I lowered my rifle to my hip, the weight of it a familiar comfort against my leg. "Stay here. Keep your eyes on the door. If anything moves, you shoot it. If anything talks, you listen. If anything looks like it might be a problem, you shoot it first and worry about the details later. Understood?"

Diaz nodded, his face pale. "Understood, sir."

I moved toward the window, keeping my back to the wall. The glass was cracked, a web of fractures spreading from the center. I pressed my eye to the gap, looking out into the street.

The street was empty.

That was the problem. It was too empty. The mortar fire had stopped, or at least, the rhythmic thumping had faded into a distant, muffled buzz. The dust was settling, creating a gray haze that hung in the air. I could see the twisted rebar of a collapsed building across the street, and the shattered remains of a car that had been parked under a tree. The tree was gone. Only the stump remained, charred and black.

I looked down the block. There were no Marines. No movement. No sound.

I pulled back from the window and turned to Diaz. "How long have you been here?"

"Since we came in, sir. About twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes," I muttered. "And no one has moved. No one has made contact. No one has reported."

Diaz looked at the floor. "I don’t know, sir."

I walked to the door, checking my magazine one more time. Fourteen rounds. That was plenty of ammunition. I had plenty of time, plenty of skill, plenty of luck. I had all the things that mattered.

I pushed the door open and stepped into the hallway.

The hallway was dark. The lights had been shattered, leaving only the dim glow of the streetlight filtering through the broken windows at the end. The floor was covered in debris, glass, and dust. I could see the blood on the tiles, dark and dried. I recognized the pattern. It was the comms officer.

I walked over to the body. He was slumped against the wall, his helmet hanging off his head. His face was twisted in a silent scream. I looked at his radio. It was shattered, the casing cracked, the wires exposed.

I felt a cold knot in my stomach.

I turned back to the door, where Diaz was waiting. He was holding his rifle with both hands, his eyes wide and wet. He looked like he was about to cry.

"We’re alone," I said. It wasn’t a question.

Diaz nodded. "We’re alone, sir."

I looked at him, really looked at him. I saw the fear in his eyes, the uncertainty, the lack of faith. He was a good soldier. He was obedient. He was useful. But he was weak. And in a situation like this, weakness was a liability.

I raised my rifle and pointed it at him.

Diaz froze. His eyes widened, his mouth opening in a silent gasp.

"Put the rifle down," I said, my voice calm and steady.

"Sir, I..."

"Put. The. Rifle. Down."

His hands shook as he lowered the weapon. He set it on the floor, the sound of metal on concrete echoing in the silent hallway. He looked at me, his face a mask of terror.

I kept my rifle pointed at him. I didn’t know what I was doing. I didn’t know why I was doing it. I just knew that I couldn’t trust him. I couldn’t trust anyone. The only thing I could trust was the rifle in my hand and the instinct to survive that lived in my bones.

"Stay there," I said. "Don’t move."

I turned and walked back into the room with the noncombatant mother and child. I didn’t look at them. I didn’t need to. I knew they were there. I knew they were a problem. I knew they were a distraction.

I sat down on the floor, my back against the wall, my rifle across my knees. I closed my eyes.

I was alone.

The silence was heavy. It pressed against my eardrums, a physical weight. I could hear the distant hum of the city, the faint ringing in my ears, the slow beating of my own heart.

I was alone.

And for the first time in my life, I didn’t know what to do.

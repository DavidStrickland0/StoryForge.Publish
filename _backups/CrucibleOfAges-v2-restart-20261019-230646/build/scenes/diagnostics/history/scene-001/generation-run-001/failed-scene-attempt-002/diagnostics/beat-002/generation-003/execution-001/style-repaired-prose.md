The street was a graveyard of broken infrastructure.

Concrete slabs lay across the asphalt like shed skins, and the air tasted of wet chalk and cordite. My boots crunched over gravel and shattered glass as I moved forward, my rifle held high, the stock digging into my shoulder. I was checking the periphery, the way the instructors had drilled into us at Parris Island, the way they tried to beat the civilian out of us before the first live-fire exercise. *Situational awareness. Threat identification. I maintained strict control over my breathing and movements.*

I had seven rounds left. That was a number. A number was something I could manage. As long as I stayed in the open and kept moving, the geometry of the fight remained in my favor. I could see the rooftops, the broken doors, the debris fields. I could see everything. That was the point.

"Marine, hold position!"

Diaz’s voice cracked over the radio, tinny and thin against the backdrop of distant, rhythmic thuds. The mortar fire had picked up again, closer this time. The ground shuddered under my feet, sending a fresh spray of dust into the air. I didn’t stop. I was already halfway down the block, my eyes scanning the upper stories of the buildings flanking the road.

"Copy, Diaz. I’m moving to the secondary position. Extraction is twenty minutes out. I’m not sitting here waiting for a bus."

"You're light on ammo, sir. We need to consolidate."

I laughed, a short, dry bark that felt like a hackle in my throat. I needed to consolidate my position. A word for people who had the luxury of time. I checked the magazine again. Seven rounds. It was enough for the next three contacts, assuming the next three contacts were the only ones I’d see.

I rounded the corner of a collapsed market stall. The awning was torn, hanging in tatters, and behind it, the skeleton of a delivery truck sat at a precarious angle, its cargo of concrete blocks spilled onto the street. I used the truck as cover, sliding around the rear wheel well. My knees ached from the impact of the earlier building collapse, a dull, throbbing pain that I ignored. Pain was data. It told me I was still alive and that my body was functioning as a biological machine. I had to appreciate the machine.

From my vantage point, I could see Diaz’s helmet, white and dusty, emerging from the doorway of the ruined shop I had just left. He was hunched, moving with the careful, deliberate grace of a man who was terrified of being shot and was trying very hard to make sure he wasn't.

"Status," I said into the mic.

"Two bodies down. I see no other hostiles in the immediate radius. But the sector is hot, sir. Mortar impact was close. Maybe fifty meters east."

"Fifty meters east is a problem for the guy in the shell hole," I said. "I'm pushing forward. I need to clear the intersection before the Chopper gets here. If we have a sniper on the cross streets, I want them identified before we're on the helo deck."

Diaz was quiet for a second. I could hear his breathing, the wet, rattling sound of air being dragged through a throat that was too dry.

"Sir, I have two mags. I'm not running out. I'm... I'm tired."

"Tired is a feeling, Diaz. Tired is what happens when you're doing it wrong. Keep your head down, watch your six, and stop breathing so loud. You're telegraphing your position to everyone in the sector."

"I'm trying to stay calm."

"Calm is a luxury. Focus is a requirement. Stay behind the doorframe. I'm going to sweep the east side."

I moved out from behind the truck. The street ahead was empty, a long stretch of grey rubble and broken pavement that led to the intersection. It was too quiet. In a firefight, silence usually meant one of two things: either the other side was reloading, or they were dead. In my experience, it was usually reloading.

I kept my rifle up, the optic centered on the far corner. My eyes darted, searching for movement, for the glint of a scope, for the shadow of a muzzle flash. I saw nothing but dust and debris. Just dust and debris.

I took a step. Then another. My boots found purchase on a piece of twisted rebar, and I adjusted my balance, my weight shifting to the outside foot. I was in the zone. The world had narrowed down to the black rectangle of the optic and the world beyond it. I was the only thing that mattered. I was the only variable in the equation that could change the outcome.

"East side clear," I said.

I moved to the corner, my back to the wall, checking the alleyway that branched off to the right. It was a narrow gap between two buildings, choked with trash and broken furniture. I shined my tactical light into the dark. The beam cut through the dust, illuminating a pile of cardboard boxes and a overturned shopping cart.

Nothing there.

I turned back to the street. And that was when I heard the noise.

It wasn't a gun. It wasn't a grenade. It was a sound that didn't belong in a combat zone. It was a whimper. A small, high-pitched sound, like a kitten caught in a drain.

I froze. My hand tightened on the trigger guard. The sound came from behind me, from the direction of the shop where Diaz was supposedly holding position.

"Diaz?" I said, my voice flat.

No answer. Just the whimpering, and a new sound, a low, guttural moan that seemed to come from the throat of a man who was having a heart attack.

I spun around, my rifle tracking the source of the noise.

Diaz was on the ground.

He wasn't holding the doorframe. He was on his back, his legs splayed out, his helmet askew. His face was a mask of white shock, his eyes wide and unseeing, staring at the sky. He was trying to move, but his hands were shaking so badly he couldn't grip the hilt of his knife, which lay on the asphalt beside him.

"Hey, hey," I said, lowering my rifle slightly. "Diaz, look at me. What happened?"

He couldn't speak. He just stared at me, his lips moving silently, his chest heaving in a rhythm that was all wrong. Too fast. Too shallow.

I approached him, my boots crunching on the glass. I knelt beside him, my hand hovering over his shoulder. He was pale, a sickly grey color that had nothing to do with the dust.

"Talk to me," I said. "Diaz, talk to me."

He coughed, a wet, rattling sound that seemed to tear something loose in his chest. A spurt of dark blood hit the concrete between us.

I looked at the blood. I looked at Diaz. I looked at the empty street.

"Medic!" I shouted into the radio. "I have a casualty in position Bravo-4! Diaz is down! Bleeding out!"

Static. Then, the voice of the comms officer, distant and concerned. "Copy, Bravo-4. Medevac is en route. ETA four minutes. Stay with him."

Four minutes.

I looked at Diaz. His eyes were losing focus, the pupils dilating into black holes. He was slipping away, his consciousness drifting into a dark place from which I couldn't follow.

"Stay with me, Diaz," I said, my voice breaking. I grabbed his collar, shaking him. "You don't get to check out. You don't get to do this to me. You hear me? Stay with me."

He smiled, a weak, trembling expression that didn't reach his eyes. "Good shot," he whispered.

Then his hand fell from my shoulder, and his eyes rolled back.

I sat there, kneeling in the blood and the dust, my rifle still in my hands. The street was silent again. The mortar fire had stopped. The only sound was the wind, whistling through the broken windows of the buildings, carrying the scent of smoke and death.

I was alone.

I looked at my rifle. I looked at my hands. I looked at the dead man in front of me.

I was the Marine. I was the one who made the decisions. I was the one who kept the others alive.

But Diaz was dead. And I was alone.

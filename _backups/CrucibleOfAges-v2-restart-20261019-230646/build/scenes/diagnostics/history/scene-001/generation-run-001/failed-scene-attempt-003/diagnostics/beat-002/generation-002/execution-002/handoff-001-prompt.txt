The shadow in the window didn’t move again. It just hung there for a second, a smear of darker dust against the grey afternoon light, before dissolving into the building’s broken facade. I kept my rifle up, but my finger stayed off the trigger. In my experience, shadows that move like that usually mean something is wrong with the mission, not necessarily something is about to kill you.

I keyed the radio. "Diaz, status."

Static. Just a wash of white noise that sounded like the sea, if you’d ever heard it while wearing three thousand dollars’ worth of Kevlar and a helmet that made you look like a medieval torturer.

I tapped the mic again. "Diaz, say again. We need to move. The room is clear, the civilians are secured, and I have a deadline."

The static continued. It wasn’t the kind of static that meant the channel was open; it was the dead, flat hiss of a connection that had simply stopped existing. I checked the battery light on my wrist. Solid green. Full charge. I checked the antenna. It was still attached, though it had bent slightly during the breach. I straightened it out, a small, futile gesture that felt like trying to fix a car engine by adjusting the steering wheel.

"Command, this is Team Six, do you copy?"

No answer. The air in the room seemed to thicken, the dust motes hanging in the shafts of light like suspended stars. I could hear the distant, rhythmic thump of the mortars in the background, a steady heartbeat that had been keeping time with my thoughts for the last twenty minutes. Now, without the voice of my platoon leader or the crackle of comms, the silence in my earpiece felt louder than the artillery.

I looked at Diaz. He was standing near the noncombatant woman, his back to me, his posture rigid. He hadn’t moved. He hadn’t responded to my order to move either. He was just standing there, watching the window.

"Diaz," I said, my voice lower now. "Check your comms."

He turned slowly. His face was pale, the sweat on his forehead catching the light. He looked at me, then at the window, then back at me. There was a confusion in his eyes that I didn’t like. It was the look of a man who had just realized that the map he was reading didn’t match the territory.

"Command is gone," he said. His voice was rough, scraped raw. "No signal. Nothing."

I frowned. "That’s impossible. We’re in the building. The repeater is on the roof."

"I don’t know what you mean by repeater," Diaz said, and then he seemed to realize what he’d said. He blinked. "I mean... I don’t know if it’s working."

I checked my watch. 14:22. We were supposed to be out of this sector by 14:00. We were late. We were always late, because I always insisted on checking every corner, every shadow, every potential threat. It was a habit that had saved my life more than once, and it had cost me more than a few points in my performance reviews. But today, the delay felt different. It felt like the walls of the world were closing in, not with violence, but with indifference.

"Get the civilians out," I ordered, my voice sharp. "Move them to the extraction point. I’m going to check the perimeter."

"Diaz," I said. "You’re staying here. Keep them calm. If anything moves, you shoot it. Simple."

He nodded, but he didn’t move. He just looked at me, his eyes wide and wet. I saw the fear in him, the raw, animal terror of a man who knows he is alone in a room that no longer has doors. I felt a pang of something that wasn’t quite pride, and wasn’t quite pity. It was the same feeling I got when I looked at a stray dog that had wandered into a construction site. It was alive. It was trying to survive. And it was completely out of its depth.

I moved toward the door. My boots crunched on the broken glass. I paused, looking back at the woman. She was holding her child tightly, her face buried in the boy’s hair. She didn’t look at me. She didn’t look at Diaz. She was already gone, mentally, already somewhere else, probably in a world where there were no marines, no mortars, and no shadows in the windows.

I stepped out into the hallway. The air was cool, smelling of concrete and cordite. I walked quickly, checking each doorway, each corner. My training told me to move with purpose, to be efficient, to be a weapon. But my gut, that primitive, stupid part of my brain that kept me alive, told me to stop.

I stopped.

I listened.

The mortars were still thumping in the distance. But there was something else. A low, humming sound, like a bee trapped in a bottle. It was coming from everywhere and nowhere. It was in the walls. It was in the floor. It was in my teeth.

I realized then that the radio wasn’t broken. The radio was working. It was just that there was nothing on the other end. There was no command. There was no platoon. There was no extraction. There was only me, the humming, and the shadow that had moved in the window.

I was alone.

The realization didn’t hit me like a punch. It settled over me like a shroud, heavy and cold. I was a Marine. I was a specialist. I was a killer. I was all those things, but they were just titles. They were just words that meant something when there was a structure around them. Without the structure, without the unit, without the mission, I was just a guy in a room.

I looked down at my rifle. It was still loaded. I had fourteen rounds. I had a knife. I had a grenade. I had all the tools I needed to kill, and none of the tools I needed to live.

I started to laugh. It was a short, dry sound, like a branch snapping. I didn’t know why I was laughing. Maybe it was because it was funny. Maybe it was because it was the only thing that made sense.

I walked back to the room. Diaz was still there. The woman was still there. The child was still there. The world was still there, but it was smaller now. It was a room with a window, and a shadow, and a man who was afraid.

I put my hand on Diaz’s shoulder. He flinched.

"We’re going to move," I said. "Right now. Get them up. We’re going to go through the back door."

Diaz looked at me. "What about Command?"

"We don’t have Command," I said. "We have us. And we are going to leave this place before the next shell lands."

I didn’t wait for his answer. I was already moving, my boots crunching on the glass, my heart beating in a rhythm that no longer matched the mortars. I was alone. And for the first time in my life, I was happy about it.

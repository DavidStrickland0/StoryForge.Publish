The dust in Khorath’s trench line had the distinct, chalky taste of pulverized limestone, and it was currently coating every inch of my skin, settling into the creases of my neck and the corners of my eyes. I spat it into the dirt with a wet, gritty click, watching the globule land next to the strap of my rucksack. Around me, the air vibrated with the rhythmic, concussive thud of distant artillery, a heartbeat that seemed to come from the ground itself rather than the sky. It was a steady, mechanical pulse, and for a man who had spent the last eighteen months learning to kill people for a paycheck, it was the most comforting sound I had ever heard. It meant the enemy was where we expected them to be. It meant the plan was working. Or at least, it meant the plan was loud enough to cover the sound of my own breathing, which was slightly too fast.

I leaned back against the packed earth wall, adjusting the strap of my rifle so it hung just enough off my shoulder to look like I was resting, but not enough to suggest I was tired. Resting was a luxury. Tired was a confession. I was Private Elias Thorne, and I was currently the most valuable asset in this specific stretch of the front because I knew how to make a machine gun talk without breaking the barrel, and I knew exactly where the enemy’s supply lines were thin. I had learned that from a nervous lieutenant three days ago, a man who had been sweating through his uniform and muttering about his mother’s health. I had taken his notes, thanked him for his service, and then moved his position to the front line during the next rotation. He had looked at me with a mixture of fear and gratitude, and I had smiled. It was an efficient exchange. He got to live another day, and I got to hold the high ground. The math was simple.

Across the trench, Specialist Miller was trying to repair a cracked stock on his rifle with a strip of tape and a prayer. Miller was a good soldier. He followed orders, he cleaned his gear, and he never asked questions that didn’t have a clear, operational answer. He was also completely, hopelessly terrified. You could tell by the way his hands trembled when he reached for the ammunition clip, a subtle shiver that he tried to hide by gripping the metal too tightly. I watched him work, feeling a surge of protective affection that was entirely performative. I liked Miller because he was useful. He was the kind of person who would stay in the line while I moved to the rear, taking the worst of the incoming fire so that I could observe the battlefield from a position of relative safety. It was a symbiotic relationship, if you squinted hard enough and ignored the ethical implications.

"Thorne, check the comms again, will you?" Miller called out, his voice cracking slightly on the last word. He didn't look up from his rifle. "Sergeant says he’s got a request from the forward observer. We need to know if the artillery adjustment is coming in on the grid or if it’s drifting."

I sighed, letting the sound carry a weight of exhausted authority. "I’m checking it, Miller. Give it a minute. The static is eating the signal, but if I can get a clear channel, I’ll patch him in. For now, keep your head down. The next volley is going to be closer."

Miller nodded, his eyes wide behind the grime on his face. He looked like a dog waiting for a kick, except the kick was a shell, and the dog was wearing a uniform. I felt a brief, sharp pang of irritation at his weakness. Why couldn't he just be steady? Why did he have to be so visibly human? It complicated things. A steady soldier was a tool. A shaky soldier was a liability. I pulled the radio handset from my vest and pressed the talk button, my voice dropping into the clipped, professional baritone that had carried me through three deployments and two commendations.

"Grid control, this is Thorne. Requesting immediate confirmation on the targeting solution. We are observing lateral drift on the left flank. Adjust accordingly."

The response was a burst of static, followed by a grunt of acknowledgment from an officer whose name I didn't care to learn. The system was working. The bureaucracy of war was a beautiful, intricate machine, and as long as I kept the parts oiled and the gears turning, I would be safe. I would be protected by the weight of the institution, by the sheer, overwhelming mass of the military-industrial complex that existed to keep men like Miller alive and men like me in charge. I was a cog, but I was a cog that knew how to turn. That was the secret. Everyone else was just spinning in place, waiting for the world to hit them. I was moving with it.

I checked my watch. The second hand swept past the twelve, a smooth, silent motion that seemed to mock the chaos erupting around us. We had twenty minutes until the next phase of the assault. That was plenty of time to rest, to eat, to plan. I pulled a protein bar from my pack, the wrapper crinkling loudly in the quiet of the trench. It was a cheap, salty thing, the kind they gave out in the mess hall, but it tasted like victory. It tasted like control. I took a bite, chewing slowly, watching Miller work. He was still shaking. I wondered if he knew that his fear was a resource. I wondered if he realized that his terror was what kept me sharp, what gave me a sense of perspective, a reminder of what was at stake. Without him, I would have to do it all myself. And I didn't want to do it all myself. I wanted to lead. I wanted to be the one making the decisions, the one taking the credit, the one who could look back on this day and say that he had won because he was the best. That was the job. That was the deal.

The earth beneath me shuddered, a low, groaning rumble that traveled up through my boots and into my spine. The dust fell in a fine, gray mist, settling on my shoulders and the barrel of my rifle. The distant thumping had changed pitch, growing deeper, more urgent. The artillery was adjusting. The plan was shifting, and I was right there in the center of it, ready to adapt. I finished the protein bar, wiped my hands on my pants, and stood up. The trench was narrow, the walls pressing in on me, but I didn't feel confined. I felt ready. I was the man who knew what to do. I was the man who would survive. And if Miller had to tremble to make it happen, then let him tremble. It was part of the cost of doing business.

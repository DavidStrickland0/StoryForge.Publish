The dust in the compound tasted like wet concrete and old blood, a gritty film that coated the back of my throat with every breath I forced through my mask. I crouched behind the overturned transport crate, the metal groaning softly under the weight of the debris pinned against it, and checked my magazine for the third time. It was full. It was always full. That was the problem with the new recruits, or so I liked to believe. They treated their weapons like holy relics, checking them with a reverence that bordered on religious ecstasy, while I treated mine like a tool that had a job to do.

"Check your perimeter, Miller," I called out over the comms, my voice low and steady, cutting through the static. "Keep your head down. You’re a sitting duck if you stick your neck up there."

Miller was to my left, pressing his back against the crumbling brick wall of the warehouse. He was young, barely older than me in terms of rank, but younger in every other metric that mattered. He still had the look in his eyes that came from basic training, where the world was a series of correct answers and wrong ones, where following protocol was the same thing as staying alive. I didn’t have that look anymore. I had the look of a man who had seen what happened when protocol met reality in a place where the locals didn’t care about our rules of engagement.

"I’m clear, Sergeant," Miller said. His voice was tight, a thin thread of tension running through the words. "But the movement in the alleyway isn’t stopping. There’s at least two more."

I didn’t answer immediately. I shifted my weight, the knee pads on my tactical gear scraping against the rough concrete. The sound was small, but in the chaos of the firefight, it felt loud enough to echo. I could hear the distant crack of rifle fire, the sharp pop of suppressors, and the wet thud of bodies hitting the ground. It was a symphony of violence that I had learned to read fluently. Every sound had a meaning, a direction, a consequence.

"Two is a lot," I said finally, keeping my tone casual, almost bored. "Do you have a clear line of sight?"

Miller hesitated. I could hear the intake of breath, the slight tremor in his vocal cords. "I… I think so. But if I fire, I might miss. They’re moving fast."

"Then don’t fire," I said. "Just watch. Tell me when they’re in the open."

I waited. The seconds stretched out, each one a small victory of patience over panic. I could feel the adrenaline humming in my veins, a low-frequency vibration that kept my hands steady and my mind sharp. This was the part of the job that I actually liked. The thinking. The calculating. The way the world broke down into vectors and probabilities, where human lives were just data points in a larger equation.

A shadow moved in the alleyway. Then another. Two figures, dressed in the nondescript clothing of the locals, moving with the fluid grace of people who had been fighting since they could walk. They were closing in, their eyes scanning the crates and the debris, looking for a threat, looking for a weakness.

"Contact," I said. "Two hostiles, bearing zero-four-five. They’re moving toward Miller’s position."

Miller’s breathing quickened. "Sergeant, they’re coming for me."

"Stay down," I said. "And do exactly what I tell you to do."

I didn’t wait for his confirmation. I already had my plan. It was simple, elegant, and entirely effective. I pulled a single round from my magazine and chambered it with a smooth, practiced motion. The click of the bolt was sharp and final. I raised my rifle, the scope aligning with the alleyway, and waited for the first figure to step into the open.

The moment he did, I fired.

The round struck him in the shoulder, spinning him off balance. He stumbled, his rifle clattering to the ground, and he fell to his knees, a hand going to the wound. The second figure froze, his head snapping toward the sound of the shot, his eyes widening in shock.

That was all the time I needed.

"Miller," I said, my voice calm and steady. "Stand up."

Miller didn’t move. I could hear his confusion, his fear, the sudden realization that I had just put a bullet in a man’s body and was now ordering him to stand. "Sergeant? What the hell?"

"Stand up, Miller. Now."

The command was absolute. It carried the weight of rank, of authority, of the unyielding hierarchy that kept us alive in places like this. Miller was a Marine, and Marines obey orders. Even when they didn’t make sense. Even when they felt wrong.

Slowly, with the trembling hands of a man who couldn’t understand what was happening, Miller stood up.

He was tall, lanky, with a face that was still soft with the remnants of youth. He looked at me, his eyes wide, his mouth slightly open. He looked at the wounded man on the ground, then back at me. He didn’t understand. He didn’t have to.

I raised my rifle again. The scope aligned with Miller’s chest.

The second figure in the alleyway saw it. He saw the barrel pointed at his own comrade, at the only other person in the room who wasn’t a threat to him. He saw the hesitation in Miller’s eyes, the confusion, the sudden realization that he was no longer a soldier, but a shield.

He fired.

The round struck Miller in the chest, spinning him backward. He hit the ground with a thud that seemed too quiet, too final. His eyes were open, staring at the ceiling, his mouth working silently as if he were trying to form a word.

I didn’t flinch. I didn’t hesitate. I fired twice more, the rounds tearing through the second figure’s body, dropping him to the ground. The alleyway was silent, save for the ringing in my ears and the sound of my own breathing.

I looked at Miller’s body, lying there in the dust, his chest heaving with the last of his breath. I felt a flicker of something, a cold, distant sensation that I quickly pushed down. It was useless. It was a liability. I had done what needed to be done. I had eliminated the threat. I had secured the position.

I holstered my rifle and stood up, my boots crunching on the debris. I looked around the compound, my eyes scanning for any other movement, any other threat. There was nothing. Just the silence, and the dust, and the two bodies on the ground.

I checked my watch. We were behind schedule. I had to move.

I walked over to Miller’s body, my face a mask of indifference. I didn’t look at his eyes. I didn’t look at the blood spreading across the concrete. I just looked at the empty space where he had been, and I felt a strange, cold satisfaction. I had used him. I had used his weakness, his confusion, his obedience, to save my own life. It was efficient. It was effective. And it was mine.

I turned and walked toward the exit, my steps steady and sure. The dust settled around my boots, a fine layer of gray coating my gear. I didn’t look back. I didn’t need to. The job was done. The threat was eliminated. And I was still alive.

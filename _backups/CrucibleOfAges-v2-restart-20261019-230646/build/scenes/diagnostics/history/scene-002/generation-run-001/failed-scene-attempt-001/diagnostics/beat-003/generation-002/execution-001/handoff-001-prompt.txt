He tried to move.

It was a reflex, the same impulse that made his body tense before a jump, or the subtle shift of weight before a sprint. In the void, the command traveled through his neural pathways, firing off in a cascade of electrical signals that had nowhere to go. There were no muscles to contract, no bones to pivot, no air to displace. The effort hung in the nothing, a phantom limb sensation that left him feeling hollowed out, stripped of the physical anchor that had defined his existence for eighteen years.

Caelen let the frustration simmer. It was a familiar flavor, sharper than the fear he had felt on the battlefield, more corrosive than the pain that had finally taken him. He had spent his life believing that if he was smart enough, if he was fast enough, if he could just outmaneuver the odds, he would be safe. He had used that belief to climb, to take what he wanted, to push people aside when they got in the way. Private Davis had been easy; the boy was eager to please and terrified of failure. Sergeant Miller had been harder, a rigid block of authority that Caelen had learned to sidestep by finding the cracks in the sergeant’s discipline.

But here, in the colorless expanse, there was no one to manipulate. There were no weaknesses to exploit, no concrete benefits to extract. There was only the grid.

It was still there, hovering at the periphery of his awareness, faint and unchanging. The deep red line. The pale silver line. Both stopped at Level 1.

Caelen tried to reach for it again. He didn’t know how he was doing it, or if he was doing it at all. He simply focused his intent on the interface, trying to pull the numbers closer, to make them speak, to demand an explanation for what they meant. Why did saving a woman count as a transaction? Why did his years of calculated self-promotion also count, just as cleanly, as a ledger entry?

The interface did not respond. It did not flicker, or expand, or offer a tutorial. It remained a static overlay, a piece of furniture in a room he could not see. It was not a guide. It was not a judge. It was simply a record.

The realization settled into him with a cold, heavy weight. He had assumed the System was a reward, a prize for the hero who saved the day. But looking at the red line, the one that represented his selfishness, his manipulation, his desire to be the most valuable man in the room, the distinction blurred. The System didn’t care about the morality of the act. It only cared about the transaction.

He thought of the mother. She had been crouched in the rubble, holding the dead weight of her infant, her face a mask of such profound, exhausted shock that it had looked more like a statue than a person. When he had thrown himself in front of the bullets, it wasn’t because he loved her. It wasn’t because he had a sudden surge of altruism. It was because the alternative was to let her die, and for some reason, in that split second, that option had become unbearable. He had chosen to be the one who broke, the one who bled, because he couldn’t stand to be the one who watched.

Was that sacrifice? Or was it just another form of control, the need to dictate the outcome of the violence? He didn’t know. The System didn’t tell him.

He tried to imagine what happened next. He tried to project himself forward, to see the next step in the grid, to find the path that led to power, to safety, to a body that was stronger and faster than the one he had lost. But the grid offered no paths. It only showed the present. Level 1. Level 1. A dead end, or perhaps a starting line, but he couldn’t tell the difference.

Time had always been a resource he could spend. He had spent it training, spent it scheming, spent it sleeping. Here, time was a void, an empty space where the clock had been removed. He felt the edges of his memory beginning to fray, the details of the last few months becoming slightly less vivid. The smell of the gunpowder, the specific shade of the sky above the courtyard, the sound of Miller’s voice as he had ordered him to fire.

They were still there, but they were receding.

Caelen resisted it. He grabbed onto the memory of the bullet hitting his chest, the searing heat that had spread through his ribs. He held onto the pain, using it as an anchor, a way to prove that he was still real, that he was still Caelen, the Marine, the survivor, the man who had made it to the top.

But the pain was fading. It was becoming a story he was telling himself, rather than a sensation he was feeling. And with the pain went the urgency.

He was tired. Not the physical exhaustion of a long march, but a deep, spiritual fatigue, the kind that came from carrying a weight he no longer needed. He realized that he had been fighting the void, fighting the silence, fighting the interface. He had been trying to solve it, to break it, to use it.

He stopped.

He let the frustration go. He let the fear go. He let the sharp, jagged edges of his anger soften into a dull, quiet hum.

The grid remained. It was still there, a faint whisper in the back of his mind, a reminder of the levels he had achieved and the ones he had yet to reach. It was passive. It was patient. It did not require his attention, and it did not offer his salvation.

Caelen settled. He didn’t know how long he would be here. He didn’t know where he would go when he left. He didn’t know if he would be a hero or a monster, if he would save the world or break it. He only knew that he was here, and that for the first time in his life, he was not in control.

He waited.

The void stretched out around him, infinite and indifferent. The grid pulsed once, a faint, rhythmic throb that he felt in the center of his being. Then it went still.

Caelen closed his eyes, even though he had no eyelids. He let the darkness take him, not as a defeat, but as a rest. He was ready to wait. He was ready to see what the next step would be, whenever the grid decided to show him.

For now, there was only the silence. And for the first time, it didn’t feel like a threat. It felt like a pause. A breath between the notes.

He was ready.

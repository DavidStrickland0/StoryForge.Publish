The grid did not resolve into a map, or a blueprint, or anything that had a name in any language Caelen had ever spoken. It simply expanded, filling the void with a faint, pulsing luminescence that felt less like light and more like the sudden clarity of a thought fully formed.

In the center of this geometric expanse, two columns of data appeared.

They were not words. They were not symbols in any script he could read. They were raw information, bypassing the need for interpretation and settling directly into the architecture of his mind. He knew them the way he knew his own heartbeat, or the weight of the rifle he had dropped in the courtyard.

Left: Dominion.
Right: Sacrifice.

Beneath each heading, a single number pulsed with a steady, indifferent rhythm.

There was only one.
There was only one.

Caelen stared at them, or rather, he projected his awareness toward them, and the numbers remained suspended in the air, unchanging, unyielding. There was no fanfare. There was no voice booming from the heavens, congratulating him on his virtue or warning him of the price of his soul. There was only the data, stark and mechanical, hanging in the silence.

He tried to look away, but in a space without edges, looking away was a matter of shifting his focus, and his focus kept snapping back to the numbers. The number one kept appearing. The number one kept appearing.

It felt absurd.

Caelen had spent his entire life, the short, sharp, loud eighteen years of it, believing he was a creature of ambition. He had joined the Marines because he wanted the uniform, the respect, the power to make people listen when he spoke. He had pushed harder, trained longer, and climbed the ranks of his squad not because he loved the country or the cause, but because being the best felt good. It felt safe. It meant that when the world tried to crush him, he could crush it back. He had used his friends, his superiors, and his subordinates as stepping stones, trading loyalty for information and favor for power. He had been selfish. He had been sharp. He had been efficient.

And for that, the void gave him a single point in Dominion.

It was a pittance. It was a rounding error. It was the digital equivalent of a shrug.

But then there was the other number.

The concept of sacrifice was the first point. The concept of sacrifice was the first point.

Caelen felt a cold, sharp spike of confusion drive itself into the center of his consciousness. He had not sacrificed anything. He had not given his blood to a charity, or his time to a stranger, or his comfort to a friend. He had taken. He had seized. He had manipulated. That was what he did. That was what Caelen did.

So why was there a one on the other side?

He reached out with his mind, probing the edges of the Sacrifice column. The interface did not respond to his touch, but it did not resist it either. It simply allowed him to look deeper, to pull the thread of memory that the number was attached to.

The courtyard flashed before him.

The smell of cordite and wet brick. The screaming of the wind. The mother, crouched in the rubble, her face a mask of terror and exhaustion, holding the bundle of cloth against her chest. The dead infant. The terrible, heavy silence of the thing she was holding.

And then Miller.

Sergeant Miller, his face twisted in that ugly, righteous anger, his rifle raised, his finger tightening on the trigger. The order. *Neutralize the threat.*

Caelen remembered the moment his body had moved before his mind had caught up. The sheer, animalistic rejection of the geometry. The refusal to let the line of fire complete its trajectory. The impact. The heat. The sudden, violent absence of the world.

He had not done it to save the world. He had not done it to save her. He had done it because he could not stand the sight of her dying. He had done it because the alternative—watching it happen, letting it happen, being the one who let it happen—was a version of himself he could no longer tolerate. It was a selfish act of self-preservation, a desperate attempt to preserve his own identity as a human being rather than a weapon.

And yet, the System had looked at that desperate, ugly, selfish impulse and called it Sacrifice.

It had taken his refusal to be a monster and registered it as a point for the path of giving.

Caelen felt a laugh bubble up in his chest, a dry, rattling sound that had no air to carry it. It was a joke. It had to be. The universe was a joke, and he was the punchline. He was a Marine who had died for a stranger and been rewarded with a single point in a game he didn’t understand, while his years of calculated, efficient, selfish ambition had earned him exactly the same amount of credit.

The symmetry was insulting.

He focused on the Dominion number again. The number was one.

He thought about the time he had stolen the credit for Davis’s tactical assessment. He thought about the way he had manipulated the supply officers to get extra rations for himself while his squad went hungry. He thought about the cold, detached way he had calculated the risk-to-reward ratio of every single action he had ever taken. Those were acts of exploitation. Those were acts of taking another’s weakness and turning it into his own strength.

And they had earned him one point.

The same as saving a life.

Caelen tried to find the logic in it, to find the hidden variable, the secret multiplier, the cosmic rulebook that would explain why his best act and his worst acts were valued equally by this cold, mechanical interface. He dug through his memories, searching for a hidden cost, a hidden benefit, a hidden motive that might tip the scales.

But there was nothing.

The interface was flat. It was neutral. It did not care about his intentions. It did not care about his motives. It simply measured the outcome and registered the data.

He was beginning to understand, with a dawning, cold horror, that the System did not care who he was. It did not care that he was a Marine, or a son, or a man who had just died. It cared only about the mechanics of the act.

And if that was true, then he was not a hero. He was not a villain. He was just a data point.

The two numbers pulsed in the void, identical and indifferent, waiting for him to make a choice that would break the symmetry.

Caelen stared at them, his mind racing, his heart pounding in a chest he no longer had. He felt the weight of his own potential pressing down on him, the terrifying, empty space where his future was supposed to be. He had the tools. He had the map. And he had no idea which way to go.

The silence of the void deepened, holding its breath.

The grid didn’t answer. It didn’t pulse, or shimmer, or offer a prompt. It simply *was*, a faint lattice of geometry suspended in the absolute nothing, waiting with the patience of stone. Caelen probed it again, pushing against the concept of it with a mental pressure that felt like straining against a wall made of water. There was no resistance, but there was no give. The interface remained a passive overlay, a map of a territory he hadn’t yet learned to traverse.

He pulled his focus back from the edges and turned his attention inward, toward the source of the anomaly. The silence of the void was a heavy, physical weight, pressing against a mind that had been built for noise, for the roar of engines and the snap of radio traffic. Without the hum of the world, his own thoughts sounded loud, jagged, and uncomfortably clear.

He was eighteen years old. He had a rifle, a squad, and a future that was supposed to be a long, straight line of rank and paychecks. He had died in a courtyard in a country whose name he couldn’t remember, holding a woman whose eyes were wide with a terror that didn’t belong to her. And now, he was floating in a colorless dark, trying to understand a geometric ghost.

It was, by any standard of logic he had ever applied to a battlefield, insane.

Caelen floated there, suspended in the quiet, and began to dismantle his own life. He had been good at it, at the dismantling. He had learned to look at people and see their leverage points, the specific weaknesses he could press to get what he wanted. He had been a Marine because it was the most efficient way to get from being invisible to being someone. He had told himself it was duty, but the truth was simpler and colder: he wanted to be the one giving the orders, the one whose word mattered, the one who didn’t have to ask permission to exist.

He had used people for that. He had smiled at Private Davis and handed over his extra rations, knowing Davis would owe him a favor, a debt of gratitude that Caelen could cash in when the moment was right. He had listened to Sergeant Miller’s grievances and validated them, not out of respect, but because a happy sergeant was a sergeant who overlooked his own mistakes and covered for his own laziness. He had been a manipulator of small, human details, and he had been proud of it. He had called it intelligence. He had called it survival.

And then there had been the mother.

He tried to remember the moment he had thrown himself in front of Miller’s gun. He remembered the heat of the barrel, the smell of gunpowder and dust, the sharp, electric crack of the shot hitting his chest. But the memory of the *why* was slippery, eluding his grasp. He had been trained to assess threats, to prioritize the survival of the unit. The woman had been a non-combatant, a variable, a problem to be solved or ignored. Yet, in that split second, the logic of his training had snapped, replaced by a raw, animal refusal to let her die.

It hadn’t felt heroic. It hadn’t felt like a grand, cinematic choice. It had felt like a reflex, a sudden, violent rejection of the geometry of the situation. He had wanted to be seen, not as a weapon, not as a Marine, but as a person who mattered enough to stop a bullet.

And the universe had rewarded him. Or maybe it had just logged him.

The grid in his periphery seemed to expand, the lines becoming clearer, more defined. He focused on it, and the abstract lattice resolved into something almost tangible. Two distinct lines of light, or perhaps just two areas of brighter clarity, stretched out before him. They were separate, independent, and yet they shared the same underlying framework.

One was a deep, saturated red. The other was a pale, almost white silver.

They were identical in their structure, in their length, in their progression. Both began at a single, small point. One level.

Caelen stared at them. The red line was marked with a single, simple symbol that he couldn’t quite read, and the silver line had a different one. But the numbers, the *quantity* of the progress, was the same. One and one.

It was a score. A tally.

He felt a cold, sharp spike of confusion. He had done one thing for his own benefit, using his position and his skills to keep his head down and his rank climbing. That was the red. And he had done one thing for another person, throwing his body into the path of a bullet to save a life that had no connection to his own survival. That was the silver.

The System didn’t care about his intentions. It didn’t care about his pride or his fear or his selfish ambition. It had simply watched the transaction and balanced the books. It had seen a man who had used another man’s weakness to get ahead, and a man who had surrendered his own life to protect a stranger. And it had given him one point for each.

It was the most terrifying thing he had ever seen. It meant that his life, his choices, his very soul, were just entries in a ledger. It meant that the good he did and the bad he did were weighed against each other, not to cancel each other out, but to build him up, to make him something more, something stronger.

He had been a Marine. He had been a killer. He had been a selfish, ambitious kid who wanted to be important.

And now, he was Level 1 in both.

The interface didn’t offer a choice. It didn’t ask him to pick a side. It simply displayed the facts, the mechanical reality of his existence. He could see the progress, he could see the levels, and he could see the potential for more. But he didn’t know how to use it. He didn’t know what it was for. He didn’t know if the red was good or the silver was good. He didn’t know if he was supposed to be proud of his sacrifice or ashamed of his selfishness.

He just knew that he was here. In the void. With a map of his own soul that he couldn’t read, and a future that he couldn’t see.

The silence of the void pressed in, heavy and indifferent. Caelen let his focus drift, letting the grid fade back into the background, a faint whisper of geometry at the edge of his mind. He was tired. Not physically tired, but mentally drained, exhausted by the sheer effort of trying to process a reality that defied every law he had ever known.

He floated there, suspended in the nothing, his consciousness sharp and clear. The memories of the battlefield were still there, but the emotional intensity was already beginning to dull, the sharp edges of the fear and the pain softening under the weight of the void. He was waiting. He didn’t know for what, or how long. He just knew that the clock had started, and he had no way to stop it.

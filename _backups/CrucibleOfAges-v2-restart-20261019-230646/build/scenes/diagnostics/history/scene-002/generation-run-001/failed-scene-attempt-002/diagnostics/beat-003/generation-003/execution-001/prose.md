The stillness had become a texture rather than an absence. Caelen floated in the gray suspension, the interface hovering in the corner of his perception like a fixed star. For what felt like hours, or perhaps minutes, he had simply existed in that quiet, letting the data hang there without demanding an answer. Now, the weight of that passivity began to press against the edges of his consciousness. It was an uncomfortable itch, the kind that comes when a soldier watches a breach and does nothing.

He turned his attention back to the interface. Not with the frantic probing of before, but with the detached assessment of a man inspecting a weapon he had been issued but had not yet been trained to use. The lines remained sharp, the numbers unyielding. *Dominion: 1. Sacrifice: 1.*

Caelen focused on the Dominion marker. He could see the raw data, but the interface offered no context, no definition of what "Level 1" actually meant in terms of capability. It was a quantity, not a quality. A tally. He tried to force the interface to expand, to show him a list of available skills or talents, the way a video game might present a menu of options after a level-up. The interface did not respond to his intent. The numbers remained static, inert.

He shifted his focus to the Sacrifice marker. The logic was the same. A number. A record of a debt paid. He remembered the weight of the mother’s body against his own, the cold shock of the impact, and the strange, hollow silence that had followed in the void. The interface had simply recorded that event and assigned it a value.

A thought crept in, unbidden and irritatingly pragmatic. If the interface showed him his levels, it must show them to everyone who had the System. He had no way to verify this, of course. The interface was private, a ghost that only he could see. But the premise of the System, as far as he could infer it, was a tool for the soul. A tool implies a user. If he was the user, what was the tool for?

He looked at the disparity. He had one level of Dominion, accrued from a lifetime of small cruelties, petty manipulations, and the steady erosion of his own empathy for the sake of survival. And he had one level of Sacrifice, earned in a single, unplanned moment of panic and instinct. The interface treated them with equal indifference. One point for years of slow poisoning. One point for a second of sudden, terrible grace.

The realization landed with a dull thud. The System did not judge him. It did not reward his goodness or punish his evil. It merely measured. It was a scale, and he was the weight on it. The terrifying part was not that he had been judged; the terrifying part was that he had been measured, and the scale was the only thing keeping him in the dark.

He tried to interact with the data again. He reached out with his mind, a subtle nudge, a query. *What do I do with this?*

The interface remained silent. There was no tutorial, no blinking cursor, no prompt. There was only the truth of what he was, laid bare in a format that stripped away all the excuses he had spent eighteen years building. He was a man who had used people to get where he was, and a man who had, once, chosen to lose everything to save someone else.

Caelen felt a strange, dry laugh bubble up in the void. It was a joke only he could hear, a soldier’s joke, told in the dark of a bunker before the mission starts. He had died to save a stranger, and the universe had handed him a calculator.

He looked at the Dominion level again. If he were to act on his old impulses, to use his position, his strength, his knowledge to dominate and control, that bar would fill. The interface would simply register the points. It would not stop him. It would not even seem to disapprove. The same was true for the Sacrifice path. If he gave, he would gain. If he exploited, he would gain.

He was alone in the void with the most dangerous tool he had ever encountered: a record of his own choices that did not hide, did not forgive, and did not guide.

Caelen let out a long, slow breath that he did not need. The gray mist settled around him, soft and absolute. He did not try to sleep, and he did not try to think. He simply let the interface sit in the corner of his vision, a fixed point in the dark. He was waiting. He did not know for what, or for how long. The years would pass, or the moments, or the ages. The time did not matter. Only the levels mattered, and the choice of how to raise them.

He closed his eyes, or the mental equivalent of closing them, and let the silence of the void fill the space where his thoughts used to be.

The interface remained.

It would, he suspected, remain for a very long time.

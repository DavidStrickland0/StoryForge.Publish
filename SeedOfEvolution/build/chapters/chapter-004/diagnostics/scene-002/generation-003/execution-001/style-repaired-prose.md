Adrian kept his hands in his lap, fingers intertwined to stop the trembling. The water bottle in his right hand was sweating, the condensation making his skin prickle with a sensitivity that felt like static electricity under the epidermis. He looked at the manila binder on Thorne’s desk, the tabs sticking out in a neat, bureaucratic row. It looked heavy. It looked like the weight of the last two weeks of his life, condensed into paper and staples.

"Before we proceed with the specimen collection," Thorne said, her voice flat and even, "we need to address the final administrative requirement for the trial phase."

Adrian’s stomach twisted, a cold knot tightening beneath his ribs. He had heard the phrase "administrative requirement" before. It was the language they used when they wanted to change the terms. It was the language they used when the money was about to stop being a simple exchange and become a debt.

He didn’t ask what she meant. He didn’t ask if he could step out into the hallway to get some air, or if the red emergency button on the desk was within reach. He just waited, his gaze fixed on the corner of the room where the ceiling light hummed with a faint, electric buzz.

Thorne reached for the binder and pulled out a single sheet of paper. She held it out toward him with two fingers, her expression unchanged.

"Please read this."

Adrian took the paper. The texture was rough, cheap stock, the kind used for internal memos. The text was dense, printed in a small, sans-serif font that made his eyes ache. He scanned the first paragraph, then the second. His brain, already foggy from the medication and the lack of sleep, struggled to keep the words from blurring together.

*...in the event of a life-threatening emergency... research team authorized to initiate emergency intervention... including but not limited to cardiac arrest management, organ stabilization, and administration of unapproved therapeutic agents...*

He frowned, tilting the paper closer. The words swam before his eyes, the black ink smearing against the white background. He blinked, forcing his vision to clear, and read the line again.

*...in the event of a life-threatening emergency... research team authorized to initiate emergency intervention...*

His heart rate, already elevated, seemed to climb another notch. The air in the room felt suddenly thin, as if the oxygen levels had dropped without warning. He looked up at Thorne. She was watching him, her pale eyes unreadable.

"What does this mean?" he asked. His voice came out thinner than he intended, a dry rasp that scraped against his throat.

"It means," Thorne said, leaning back in her chair, "that if your condition deteriorates to a point where immediate intervention is required to prevent death, the research team will have the legal authority to proceed with life-saving measures. This includes procedures that you may not be able to consent to in the moment."

Adrian looked down at the paper. His thumb traced the edge of the sheet, a nervous habit he couldn’t control. The tremors in his hands were worse now, a fine vibration that made the paper shiver against his palm.

"Does this mean you can operate on me?" he asked.

"If necessary," Thorne said. "If your vital systems fail. If your body enters a state where refusal of treatment would result in imminent death."

The words landed in his chest with a dull, heavy thud. He thought about the diner, the grease-stained apron, the way his legs felt like lead at the end of a shift. He thought about the rent due in three days, the tuition invoice waiting in his email, the empty fridge in his apartment. He thought about the money in his pocket, the manila envelope containing the check that would keep him alive for another week.

He looked at the red button on the desk. It was bright, almost aggressive against the dull wood. He knew what it was for. He had signed a form that said it was for him to press if the pain became unmanageable. But this was different. This was them pressing it for him. This was them deciding his body was a machine that needed fixing, and they held the wrench.

"And if I refuse to sign?" he asked.

Thorne didn’t blink. She didn’t sigh, or soften her tone, or offer a warning. She simply stated the fact.

"Then you are no longer eligible for the trial. The payment will be voided. You will be required to cease all participation immediately and return to your baseline medical status."

The silence that followed was loud. Adrian could hear the hum of the air conditioning, the distant sound of a car passing outside the window, the frantic, erratic thumping of his own heart. He looked at the paper again. The words were still there, solid and unyielding.

He thought about the side effects. The way his skin felt like it was on fire, the way his thoughts moved through a thick syrup, the way his hands shook when he tried to hold a pen. He thought about how much worse it was getting. He thought about how he couldn’t afford to stop.

He looked at Thorne. Her face was a mask of clinical detachment, devoid of pity, devoid of judgment. She was a doctor, or a researcher, or something in between. She was the gatekeeper to the money that kept him breathing.

Adrian folded the paper in half. The crease was sharp, a line of tension across the document. He tucked it into his pocket, next to the water bottle and the manila envelope. The weight of it was insignificant, a few grams of paper and ink. But it felt heavier than anything he had ever carried.

He didn’t sign it. Not yet. He hadn’t been asked to. He had only been shown the cost.

Thorne watched him tuck the paper away. She didn’t ask if he had understood. She didn’t offer to explain it again. She simply reached for the pen on her desk and tapped it against the wood, a small, rhythmic sound that marked the passage of time.

"The specimen collection room is on the left," she said, her voice returning to its previous, professional cadence. "Past the waiting area. Take your time. We have an hour."

Adrian stood up. His legs felt unsteady, the dizziness rising again like a tide. He gripped the edge of the desk for a second, his knuckles white, before letting go. He picked up the water bottle, his hand shaking so badly he nearly knocked it over. He took a sip, the water cold and clean, grounding him for a moment.

He walked to the door. The hallway outside was white, sterile, and silent. The other doors were closed, identical and unmarked. He could hear the faint, muffled voices of the other participants, the sound of their lives being measured and recorded and paid for.

He stopped in front of the door to the left. The handle was cold under his fingers. He looked back at the door behind him, where Thorne was already turning to her computer screen, typing, erasing him from her immediate focus.

He turned the handle and stepped into the room. The door clicked shut behind him, the sound small and final.

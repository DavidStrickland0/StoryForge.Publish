The automatic doors hissed shut behind Adrian, cutting off the ambient hum of the clinic’s air conditioning. The silence that replaced it was heavier, pressing against his eardrums. He stood on the sidewalk for a moment, the late afternoon sun beating down on the back of his neck. The heat felt wrong. It felt too sharp, too immediate, as if the air itself had turned into a fine, vibrating mist that stung the exposed skin of his face.

He checked his phone again. 7:02 PM.

Two hours and fifty-eight minutes until the diner. Two hours and fifty-eight minutes until he could sit in the back booth, order a coffee he wouldn’t drink, and let the fluorescent lights buzz until the tremors in his hands subsided. But first, he needed food. His stomach had been growling since noon, a low, persistent rumble that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with the empty, hollow feeling in his chest. He hadn’t eaten since the granola bar he’d crushed into his pocket the previous night, and even that had felt like a chore.

He began to walk.

The street was busy with the evening rush. Cars idled at the light, their engines ticking in the heat. A group of students in matching t-shirts clustered by a bus stop, laughing and shouting. Adrian kept his head down, his shoulders hunched slightly, trying to make himself smaller. The fabric of his jeans felt abrasive against his thighs, a constant, low-level friction that made his skin itch. He shifted his weight from one foot to the other, the soles of his sneakers scuffing against the pavement.

A bodega was three blocks down. The sign flickered, the neon ‘OPEN’ buzzing with a faint orange glow. He turned the corner, his eyes fixed on the doorway. The glass door was propped open with a steel bar, letting out a wave of cool, stale air that smelled of dust and cheap candy.

He stepped inside. The bell above the door jingled, a bright, sharp sound that made his eyes water. He winced, blinking rapidly. The clerk, a heavyset man with a mustache, looked up from his phone.

“Can I help you?”

Adrian nodded. He couldn’t speak. The effort of forming words felt like lifting a weight. He walked to the shelves, his fingers trembling as he reached for a bag of chips. The plastic crinkled loudly in the quiet store. He grabbed a bottle of water, too, though he didn’t know where he would put it. He paid with the few coins he had left in his pocket, his hands shaking so badly that a quarter clattered onto the counter and rolled away. He fumbled for it, his knuckles white, his breath coming in short, shallow bursts.

The clerk watched him with a mixture of confusion and mild irritation. Adrian ignored him. He didn’t have the energy for social interaction. He just wanted to leave.

He walked out of the store, the bag of chips heavy in his hand. The sun was lower now, casting long, distorted shadows across the pavement. The light was turning gold, but to Adrian, it looked sickly, like the glow of a fever. He found a bench under a tree, the leaves rustling in the breeze. He sat down, the metal seat cold against his jeans.

He opened the bag of chips. The smell of salt and grease hit him, and his stomach lurched. He took a chip, chewing slowly. It tasted like cardboard. He drank the water, the liquid cold and shocking in his throat. He sat there for ten minutes, just breathing, watching the people walk by. They moved with a fluidity that he envied. They didn’t seem to feel the world pressing in on them. They didn’t seem to feel the electricity in their skin.

He thought about the clinic. About the white walls, the sterile smell, the way Dr. Thorne had looked at him with those pale, empty eyes. She hadn’t judged him. She hadn’t cared. That was better. Caring was complicated. Caring meant asking questions. Asking questions meant finding out the truth. And the truth was that he was running out of time. Running out of money. Running out of himself.

He stood up. His legs felt weak, like jelly. He had to get to the diner. He had to work. He had to be somewhere where he was expected to be something, even if that something was just a shadow behind the counter, wiping down tables and pretending to be alive.

He walked the rest of the way, the sun burning his skin, the air thick and heavy. The world was loud, bright, and sharp. And he was just a thread in it, thin and frayed, waiting to break.

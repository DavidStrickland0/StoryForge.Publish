The room was smaller than Adrian had imagined.

It was a sterile, white-walled space, roughly ten feet by twelve. A single window, frosted glass, let in a flat, diffused light that cast no shadows. The air smelled of antiseptic and cold plastic. In the center of the room stood a stainless steel table, and beside it, a chair that looked like it had been imported from a dentist’s office.

A digital clock on the wall read 8:14 AM.

Adrian stood in the doorway, his hand still resting on the metal handle. He could feel the hum of the ventilation system in his ears, a low, constant drone that seemed to vibrate in his teeth. The door behind him clicked shut, locking with a soft, definitive sound.

He let go of the handle. His fingers were trembling. He shoved his hands into his jacket pockets to hide it, his thumb brushing against the strip of paper Evans had given him. *Don’t panic.*

Panic, Adrian realized, was already a distant memory. What replaced it was a cold, heavy weight in his stomach. It wasn’t fear anymore. It was the simple, mechanical reality of the task. He had to do this. He had to do it now.

He walked to the center of the room. The floor was smooth, polished linoleum that reflected the overhead lights. He could see his own reflection in the glossy surface: a thin, pale boy in a jacket that had seen better days. He looked like a student who had wandered into the wrong building.

On the stainless steel table, there was a single specimen cup. It was clear plastic, cylindrical, with a label already affixed to the side. Adrian’s name. *Adrian Vale.* Below his name was a date and a time stamp.

He stared at it.

The cup sat there, waiting. It was an ordinary object, the kind of thing you might find in a pharmacy or a hotel bathroom. But in this room, in this light, it felt like a verdict.

Adrian pulled a pair of disposable gloves from a dispenser on the wall. They were blue, thin as paper. He pulled them on, the latex snapping against his wrists. The smell of rubber filled his nose, sharp and chemical.

He sat down in the chair.

The chair was hard. It had no cushioning, no give. It was designed for short intervals, not for the kind of lingering, uncomfortable wait that Adrian now found himself in. He adjusted his position, trying to find a angle that would make the process less... The process made him feel exposed.

He looked around the room. There was a sink in the corner, with a faucet that ran cold. There was a trash can lined with a black plastic bag. There was a small sign on the wall, printed in bold, black letters: *COLLECTION MUST BE COMPLETED WITHIN 30 MINUTES.*

Thirty minutes.

Adrian looked at the clock. 8:16 AM.

He had twenty-eight minutes left.

He took a breath. It didn’t help. The air in the room felt thick, resistant to his lungs. He closed his eyes for a moment, focusing on the rhythm of his breathing. In, out. In, out.

When he opened them, the cup was still there.

He reached for it. His hand hovered over the plastic for a second, then he pulled it closer to himself. The label stared up at him. *Adrian Vale.*

He set it on his lap.

The weight of it was negligible. A few ounces of plastic. But it felt heavy. It felt like the entirety of his future, distilled into a small, clear cylinder.

He began.

The process was mechanical. It was something his body knew how to do, something he had done a hundred times before in the privacy of his own bathroom. But here, under the flat light, in the sterile silence, it felt different. It felt observed. He could feel the walls watching him. He could feel the clock ticking.

He focused on the task. On the physical sensations. On the rhythm.

Five minutes passed.

The number was ten.

The room remained silent. The ventilation system hummed. The clock ticked.

Adrian’s mind began to wander. He thought about his apartment. The peeling paint. The mold in the corner of the shower. The stack of unpaid bills on the kitchen counter. He thought about Friday. He thought about the money. Four thousand, two hundred dollars. It was a number that meant survival. It meant rent. It meant food. It meant staying enrolled.

He thought about Evans. *It’s normal,* he had said. *It’s just a tool.*

Adrian wanted to believe him. He wanted to believe that this was just a job. A strange, degrading, but ultimately manageable job.

But as the minutes ticked by, the feeling of exposure grew. He felt naked, even with his clothes on. He felt stripped of his dignity, his privacy, his self.

Twenty minutes passed.

The cup was still empty.

Adrian’s heart began to beat faster. The panic he had pushed down was creeping back, a cold, icy hand gripping his throat. He was running out of time.

He forced himself to focus. He thought about the money. He thought about the hunger. He thought about the fear of being evicted, of being expelled, of ending up on the street.

He pushed through.

Twenty-five minutes.

The air outside was warmer. It smelled of soap and metal, but also of life. He could hear the faint sounds of the building: the hum of the ventilation, the distant murmur of voices, the click of heels on the floor.

He had done it.

He was a participant.

The hunger was quieter. The fear was quieter.

But the weight in his stomach remained.

He walked down the corridor, the cup in his hand, the weight of it a constant reminder of what he had become.

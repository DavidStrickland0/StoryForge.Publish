The walk to the clinic took forty-five minutes. Adrian counted the steps because counting was a task that required no memory, only rhythm. Left, right. Left, right. The pavement was damp from a rain that had passed an hour ago, and the air clung to his skin with a humid, sticky weight that made his shirt feel like a second layer of skin. By the time he reached the building, his legs were shaking in a low, persistent frequency that he could feel in his teeth.

He pulled his collar up, a useless gesture against the chill that had settled deep in his marrow, and pushed through the glass doors. The lobby was a study in aggressive sterility. The air conditioning hummed at a low, constant pitch that vibrated in the hollow of his throat. The floor was polished to a mirror sheen, reflecting the overhead fluorescent lights in long, white streaks. Adrian kept his eyes on the floor, navigating by the geometry of the tiles rather than the faces of the people sitting in the waiting area.

The waiting room was sparse. Two chairs, a magazine rack with brochures for dental insurance and local real estate, and a small potted plant that looked like it was dying of neglect. Adrian chose the chair furthest from the desk. He sat down, and the plastic was cold through his trousers. He placed his bag on his lap and gripped the straps with both hands, his knuckles white. The trembling in his fingers had worsened during the walk; they buzzed against each other, a static charge that made it difficult to hold the straps steady.

A woman was already seated in the other chair. She was scrolling through a phone, her thumb moving in a rhythmic blur. Adrian did not look at her. He looked at the plant. The leaves were brown at the edges, curling inward. He wondered if they were dead, or if they were just waiting for something that was never coming.

The receptionist’s desk was a curved slab of white laminate. Behind it, the receptionist was typing on a keyboard. Her gray eyes were fixed on the monitor, but they snapped up the moment Adrian’s shoes made contact with the floor. The movement was sharp, precise. She did not smile. She did not offer a greeting. She simply looked at him, and Adrian felt the weight of that gaze settle on his chest.

"Adrian Vale," she said. Her voice was flat, devoid of inflection.

"Yes," he said. His voice sounded thin to his own ears, scraped raw by the dry air in the lobby.

"Check-in is completed via the portal," she said, returning her eyes to the screen. "Please remain seated. Dr. Thorne will be with you shortly."

Adrian nodded. He wanted to ask if there was water, if there was a restroom, if the air could be turned down, but the words died in his throat. He was too tired to form the sentences. The effort of speech felt like lifting a weight. He closed his eyes and focused on the sensation of his own breath, in and out, a steady rhythm that he could control.

The minutes stretched. The hum of the air conditioning grew louder. Adrian’s vision blurred at the edges, the white walls of the lobby bleeding into a soft, indistinct haze. He blinked hard, trying to clear the fog, but it returned immediately. His skin felt tight, as if it had shrunk over his bones. Every sound in the room was amplified. The tap of the receptionist’s keys was like a hammer strike. The distant murmur of a phone ringing in another office was a jagged screech.

He opened his eyes. The receptionist was still typing. She had not looked up again. Adrian shifted in his seat, the plastic creaking under his weight. The movement sent a jolt of pain through his lower back. He gritted his teeth, forcing his spine to straighten. He would not let her see him hunch. He would not let her see the tremor in his hands. He was Adrian Vale, nineteen years old, a student, a participant in a clinical trial. He was a person, not a specimen.

But the body he was in was failing. The medication was a foreign object in his bloodstream, a parasite that fed on his energy and excreted instability. He could feel it in the way his heart beat, a frantic, irregular thumping that echoed in his ears. He could feel it in the way his muscles twitched, tiny spasms that he could not suppress.

The door to the corridor behind the receptionist’s desk opened. Adrian’s head snapped up. His neck was stiff, the movement jerky.

Dr. Thorne stood in the doorway. She was tall, her black bob cutting a sharp line against her pale face. Her eyes, light and almost colorless, swept over Adrian with a clinical detachment that made his stomach clench. She did not look at the receptionist. She did not look at the dying plant. She looked only at Adrian.

"Adrian," she said. Her voice was calm, professional. "Come to Room 4."

Adrian stood up. His legs buckled slightly, a momentary loss of balance that he corrected by grabbing the back of the chair. The plastic groaned under his grip. He let go, straightening his posture. He picked up his bag, his hands steady now, a conscious effort of will that left him feeling hollowed out.

He walked toward the corridor. The receptionist’s eyes followed him. He did not look back. He did not need to. He knew she was watching. He knew she was assessing. He was just another data point in a spreadsheet, a variable in an equation that he could not solve.

The corridor was longer than it appeared from the lobby. The walls were painted a soothing beige, but the light was harsh, clinical. Adrian’s footsteps echoed, a lonely sound in the empty space. He passed a glass door with a sign that read *Staff Only*. He passed a window that looked out onto a parking lot, the cars dark and silent. He reached the door to Room 4.

He did not knock. He pushed the door open and stepped inside.

The room was small, lit by a single overhead light that cast a yellowish glow. A desk dominated the center, cluttered with papers and a computer monitor. A chair sat opposite the desk, the same plastic chair from the waiting room. Adrian looked at the chair, then at Dr. Thorne, who was standing behind the desk.

"Sit down," Dr. Thorne said.

Adrian sat. The plastic was cold again. He placed his bag on the floor beside him. He looked at his hands, resting on his knees. They were still. For now.

Dr. Thorne pulled a folder from the desk and placed it on the surface in front of him. She did not speak. She waited.

Adrian looked at the folder. It was thick, bound in a blue cover. The title was printed in bold, black letters: *Emergency Intervention Authorization*.

He did not open it. He did not need to. He knew what it was. He had seen the form before, in the intake packet, buried under a pile of other documents he had skimmed and signed. He had not read it then. He was not going to read it now.

He looked up at Dr. Thorne. Her face was unreadable. Her eyes were fixed on him, waiting for a reaction. Waiting for a refusal. Waiting for a question.

Adrian reached for the folder. His hand moved slowly, deliberately. He opened it. The pages were dense with text, legal language that blurred together in his exhausted mind. He scanned the first page, his eyes moving down the lines without taking in the words. He found the signature line at the bottom.

He picked up the pen Dr. Thorne had placed on the desk. The cap was off. He uncapped it, the plastic clicking softly in the quiet room. He held the pen over the paper. His hand did not tremble. He signed his name.

Adrian Vale.

The ink was black, bold, definitive. He set the pen down. He closed the folder.

Dr. Thorne took the folder from his hands. She flipped through the pages, checking the signature. She nodded once, a small, sharp movement.

"Thank you, Adrian," she said. "You are fully enrolled in the program. Your daily visits are confirmed. Please collect your phone from the charging station on the counter, and then you may leave."

Adrian stood up. He picked up his bag. He walked to the counter where his phone sat, plugged into a charger. He unplugged it. The screen was dark. He put the phone in his pocket.

He walked to the door. He paused, his hand on the handle. He did not turn around.

"Dr. Thorne," he said.

"Yes?"

"Thank you."

He pushed the door open and stepped into the corridor. The sound of the door clicking shut behind him was the only sound in the hallway. He walked back to the lobby, his steps measured, his posture straight. The receptionist was still at her desk. She looked up as he passed.

"Have a good evening, Adrian," she said.

"Goodbye," he said.

He walked out of the clinic and into the night. The air was cold, biting through his clothes. The city lights were bright, glaring, harsh. He pulled his collar up and began to walk home, his steps steady, his hands at his sides, his face a mask of calm that he maintained until the doors of the clinic were locked behind him and the silence of the street swallowed his footsteps.

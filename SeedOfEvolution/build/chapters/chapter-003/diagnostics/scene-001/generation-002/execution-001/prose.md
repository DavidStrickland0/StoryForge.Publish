David slid a standard-issue pen across the polished countertop. It was black, plastic, and heavy. He didn’t ask if Adrian wanted to read it first; he simply pointed to the signature line at the bottom of the fourth page, then the third, then the first. The motion was circular, efficient, and utterly devoid of warmth.

"Standard liability and consent," David said. His voice was flat, the cadence of someone reading a list of items he had already sorted. "Participation is voluntary. You can withdraw at any time. Payment is contingent on attendance and sample adequacy."

Adrian picked up the pen. The plastic was cool against his fingers, a small, grounding detail in the sterile hum of the lobby. He looked down at the paper. The font was small, sans-serif, and relentless. Paragraphs of legalistic abstraction stretched out in neat, justified columns. He didn't read every word. He couldn't afford the time, and the words would have blurred anyway, his mind too tired to parse the nuance between "breach of contract" and "termination of enrollment." He looked for the bolded sections, the ones that required a separate initial.

There were three.

He signed the first one near the top, his handwriting sharp and jagged, the letters pulling tight as if he were gripping the pen too hard. He signed the second in the middle. By the time he reached the third, near the bottom, the pen felt heavier. He signed it, then set the pen down with a click that sounded too loud in the quiet room.

David took the folder back, flipping through the pages with the casual speed of a man checking a grocery list. He stamped each page with a red ink seal, the *thwack-thwack-thwack* of the rubber stamp against the paper marking the rhythm of the transaction. It was a sound Adrian had imagined for days, a rhythmic, mechanical finality.

"Direct deposit setup," David said, pulling a smaller form from the folder. "We need your bank information. Routing number, account number, and account holder name."

Adrian pulled out his phone. The screen was cracked, a spiderweb of fractures radiating from the top left corner, but the display still worked. He typed in the numbers from the checkbook he kept in his wallet, his thumb moving over the glass with the careful, deliberate slowness of someone handling a broken thing. He double-checked the routing number, a sequence of digits he had memorized because there was no one else to remember it for him.

David entered the information into his tablet. He typed quickly, his eyes not leaving the screen until he was done.

"First payment will hit on the first of the month," David said. "The stipend is four thousand two hundred dollars. It’s already been processed as part of your onboarding. It should clear within two business days."

The number landed in Adrian’s mind with a dull, heavy thud. Four thousand two hundred dollars. It was more than he had seen in three months. It was more than he had seen in a semester. The hunger in his stomach, which had been a constant, low-grade companion for weeks, seemed to recede slightly, replaced by a different kind of emptiness, a hollow space where the fear had been.

"Thank you," Adrian said. The words felt inadequate, thin and dry.

David closed the folder. "Your start date is today. The medication will be dispensed by Dr. Aris in the pharmacy wing. I’ll have Sarah from reception direct you to the collection room for your first sample. It’s room 104, down the left corridor."

He slid a small, laminated card across the counter. Adrian picked it up. On the front, it said *Apex Research Institute – Participant ID: V-014*. On the back, a barcode and his name.

"Keep this with you," David said. "You’ll need it for entry to the clinical areas. If you lose it, the replacement fee is fifty dollars."

Adrian put the card in his jacket pocket, next to his keys. He felt the weight of it there, a small, cold rectangle against his ribs.

"Is there anything else I need to know?" Adrian asked.

David looked up. His gray eyes were pale and unblinking, the color of dirty ice.

"The medication must be taken within a two-hour window. If you miss the window, the day is forfeited. The sample must be provided within thirty minutes of entering the room. If you cannot complete the process within that time, you must leave, and the day is forfeited."

Adrian nodded. He had heard the rules explained before, in the abstract, in the brochures, in the minds of other people who had done this before. But hearing them stated so plainly, so devoid of empathy, made them feel solid. Real. They were the gears of the machine, and he was the oil.

"Okay," Adrian said.

"Sarah will be ready," David said. He turned back to his computer, his fingers hovering over the keyboard, already moving on to the next task, the next participant, the next transaction. The red stamp lay on the counter, unused, a small symbol of the authority that had just been exchanged for a signature.

Adrian walked away from the desk. His feet felt heavy, the soles of his sneakers sticking slightly to the polished floor. The air in the lobby was cool, smelling of lemon disinfectant and nothing else. No coffee, no smoke, no life. Just the clean, antiseptic scent of a place where bodies were processed.

He walked down the corridor, passing the glass doors of the waiting area. Through the glass, he could see the empty chairs, the magazine rack with its glossy, outdated covers. He didn't stop. He kept walking, the card in his pocket feeling like a secret, a weight, a key.

He was in. He had done it. The money was coming. He was a participant. The words didn't feel right in his mouth, but they were true. He was part of the system now. The hunger was quieter. The fear was quieter.

He reached the end of the corridor, where the door to the pharmacy wing stood open. He stopped, taking a breath. The air from the door was warmer, carrying the faint scent of medicine and plastic.

He pushed the door open and stepped inside.

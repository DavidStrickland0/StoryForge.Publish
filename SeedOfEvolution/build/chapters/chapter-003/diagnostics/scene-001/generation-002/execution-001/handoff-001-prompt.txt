The fluorescent lights of the clinic intake office hummed with a frequency Adrian felt in his teeth before he consciously registered the sound. He stood near the reception desk, his back pressed against the cool vinyl of the waiting chair, though he hadn't fully sat down. His legs had buckled slightly when he crossed the threshold, a subtle betrayal of muscle that he had masked with a quick, controlled step forward. Now, the movement cost him. The air conditioning in the lobby was set to a standard temperature, but to his hypersensitive skin, it felt like a draft of cold water running down the back of his neck. He shifted his weight, the sole of his shoe scraping against the linoleum, a sound that sounded too loud in the quiet room.

Across the desk, the receptionist looked up from her computer screen. Her gray eyes were tired, the kind of dull, administrative fatigue that suggested she had been doing this for a long time and saw little point in questioning the peculiarities of the people who walked through her door. She did not offer a smile. She did not ask how he was. She simply waited for him to speak, her fingers resting lightly on the mouse.

"Adrian Vale," he said. His voice sounded thin in his own ears, stripped of its usual confident projection. He had to make an effort to keep it steady, to prevent the slight tremor in his jaw from leaking into the words. "Second visit. Day two."

The receptionist pulled up his file. The cursor blinked on the screen, a small, rhythmic pulse that seemed to mock the stillness of the room. "Yes. Mr. Vale. Dr. Thorne is running a few minutes behind. The examination room is still being sanitized from the previous patient."

"Is there a specific time I should be there?" Adrian asked. He knew the answer. He knew the protocol. The trial required a one-hour observation window after medication administration, and he had already administered his dose in the privacy of his own apartment, a ritual that had become increasingly grotesque in its mechanical precision. He had swallowed the pill with a mouthful of tap water, the chalky texture of it lingering on his tongue, and then waited. The waiting was the hardest part. The waiting was when the shivering started, a violent, internal oscillation that made his bones feel like they were vibrating out of alignment.

"Room 4," the receptionist said. "You can wait here, or you can go to the waiting area in the back. The door is unlocked."

Adrian looked at the door she indicated. It was a heavy, fire-rated door, painted a sterile beige. Beyond it, he could see the faint outline of a waiting room, empty chairs, a magazine rack, the standard accoutrements of a place where people spent their time in suspended animation. He wanted to go. He wanted to get out of the direct line of sight of the receptionist, who was watching him with a professional, detached curiosity. He wanted to be somewhere he could press his palms against the cool glass of a window and feel the chill seep into his skin, grounding him. But his legs were leaden, and the effort of moving to the back and returning seemed like a monumental task.

"I'll wait here," he said.

He settled into the chair properly this time, sinking into the vinyl. The material was warm from the ambient heat of the building, and the contact made his skin crawl. He closed his eyes, focusing on the sensation of the fabric against his cheek, a tactile anchor in the storm of his internal sensations. The headache was a dull throb behind his left eye, pulsing in time with his heartbeat. He could feel the blood moving through his veins, a thick, sluggish current that made him feel heavy, dense.

He opened his eyes and looked at the clock on the wall. 2:45 PM. He had been walking for twenty minutes. The journey from the diner had taken an hour. His body was processing the medication, and the side effects were peaking. He could feel the tremors in his hands, a fine, rapid vibration that made it difficult to hold his phone, which he had left charging in Room 4. He had left it there because the battery was critically low, and he had no choice but to leave it plugged in. It was a small, insignificant detail, but it represented a loss of control that echoed through every aspect of his life right now. He was dependent on the clinic for his phone, for his medication, for his income. He was a component in a machine, and the machine was grinding.

The receptionist was typing. The sound of the keyboard was a rapid, percussive tap-tap-tap that seemed to sharpen his already heightened auditory sensitivity. He flinched, a small, involuntary twitch of his shoulder. The receptionist looked up again, her gaze flicking to him.

"Is there a problem, Mr. Vale?" she asked. Her voice was neutral, but there was a hint of concern in it. She was a professional. She knew the signs. She had seen people like him before. Desperate, hungry, willing to do anything for a paycheck.

"No," Adrian said. He forced a smile, a small, tight expression that did not reach his eyes. "Just tired. Long shift."

"Shift work is difficult," she said. It was a statement, not a question. She did not elaborate. She did not offer sympathy. She simply acknowledged the fact and returned to her work.

Adrian leaned back in the chair, closing his eyes again. He focused on his breathing. In for four counts, hold for four, out for four. A technique he had read about in a self-help book he could not afford to buy, one he had found in the library and memorized. It did not stop the tremors. It did not stop the headache. He did not know what "necessary" meant. He did not know what they would do if he collapsed. He did not know if they would save him or dissect him. He needed to survive.

Adrian stood up. His legs felt unsteady, but he kept his balance. He straightened his shirt, adjusting the collar to hide the sweat that was beginning to bead on his neck.

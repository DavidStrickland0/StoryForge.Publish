The walk to the clinic took twenty minutes, a duration that felt stretched thin by the tremor in Adrian’s hands. The city lights smeared into long, horizontal streaks across his vision, the streetlamps buzzing with a frequency that seemed to vibrate directly against his temples. He kept his gaze fixed on the middle distance, forcing his eyes to track the movement of pedestrians and the slow crawl of traffic, a technique the nurse had mentioned once in passing, though he hadn’t known it would feel so unnatural until now.

His body felt heavy, as if the muscles in his legs were filled with wet sand. Every step required a conscious effort to lift his foot, to place it down, to shift his weight. The faint, pale scar on his shoulder itched with a dull, persistent ache that had nothing to do with the fabric of his jacket rubbing against the skin. He reached into his pocket, his fingers fumbling for the crumpled receipt from the diner, a useless talisman against the growing pressure in his chest. The air was cool, carrying the scent of damp pavement and exhaust, but his skin felt raw, as if the atmosphere itself were a coarse cloth rubbing against exposed nerves.

He turned the corner onto the street where the research hospital stood. The building was a block of gray concrete and glass, unremarkable against the darker skyline, except for the blue glow of the signage above the entrance. The clinical trial facility occupied the second floor, a section of the building that seemed to exist in a different state of time, sterile and suspended above the messy reality of the street below.

Adrian climbed the stairs to the second floor, his breath coming in short, shallow bursts. The elevator was out of order, a fact he had noted with a grim sense of resignation on his first visit. The stairs were a test of endurance he was failing to pass. He leaned against the railing for a moment, his fingers digging into the cold metal, waiting for the worst of the dizziness to subside. His heart was beating too fast, a frantic, irregular rhythm that echoed in his ears. He closed his eyes, counting to ten, feeling the sweat prickling at his hairline.

When he opened his eyes, the hallway was empty. The fluorescent lights overhead hummed with a low, electric drone that seemed to amplify the silence. He walked to the intake office, his footsteps muffled by the thick carpet. The door was slightly ajar, a sliver of light spilling out into the corridor.

He pushed the door open with his shoulder, wincing as the movement pulled at his strained muscles. The office was small, dominated by a wide desk and a row of waiting chairs that looked uncomfortable enough to make a person want to stand. The receptionist was seated behind the desk, her gray eyes looking up as Adrian entered. She did not smile. Her expression was neutral, professional, and entirely devoid of the warmth that might have eased the tension in Adrian’s jaw.

"Mr. Vale," she said. Her voice was flat, carrying the practiced detachment of someone who had heard every possible excuse and complaint. "You’re running late."

Adrian swallowed the lump in his throat. "I was at work. The shift ran over."

"The trial protocol requires your presence at 8:00 AM for the daily sample collection and medication administration," she recited, her gaze dropping to a clipboard in front of her. "However, today’s schedule has been adjusted. Dr. Thorne is conducting a final administrative review for all participants in the initial cohort. You are required to be in Room 4 immediately."

Adrian frowned, the confusion cutting through the fog of fatigue. "I already completed my intake. I signed the consent forms."

"The initial consent forms cover the basic participation and sample collection," the receptionist said, her tone remaining level. "This is a separate review. It is mandatory for the continuation of the trial. Please proceed to Room 4. Dr. Thorne is expecting you."

She did not look up again, her attention returning to the computer screen in front of her. Adrian felt a spike of anxiety, a tightness in his chest that had nothing to do with his physical exhaustion. A final review. The phrase carried a weight that he could not quite articulate, a bureaucratic threat that loomed large in his mind. He had no time to argue, no energy to question the necessity of another meeting. He turned and walked down the hallway toward Room 4.

The door to the room was closed. He reached out, his hand trembling slightly as he touched the handle. The metal was cold, a stark contrast to the heat that seemed to radiate from his own skin. He pushed the door open and stepped inside.

Room 4 was smaller than the intake office, the walls lined with shelves of medical journals and binders. The air smelled of antiseptic and old paper, a sharp, clean scent that did little to settle his nerves. A desk sat in the center of the room, cluttered with files and a single cup of coffee that had long since gone cold. A red emergency call button sat on the desk, a small, bright dot of color in the otherwise muted palette of the room.

Adrian looked around, his eyes drifting to the chair across from the desk. It was a simple wooden chair, unupholstered, the kind that was designed for brief periods of sitting rather than extended waiting. He pulled it out and sat down, his legs swinging slightly as he tried to steady his breathing. The room was quiet, save for the hum of the air conditioning unit in the corner.

He leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees, his hands clasped together to hide the tremor. The wood of the chair was hard against his back, a solid, unyielding presence that grounded him. He closed his eyes, focusing on the sensation of the chair, the coolness of the air, the steady rhythm of his own heartbeat. He was here. He was in Room 4. He was waiting.

The door opened.

Adrian’s eyes snapped open. Dr. Thorne stepped into the room, her dark bob haircut catching the light from the window. She was tall, her lean, athletic build filling the doorway. Her pale eyes were focused, scanning the room with a quick, efficient gaze before landing on Adrian. She did not smile. She carried a folder in her hand, the cover worn and slightly bent from use.

"Mr. Vale," she said, her voice clear and calm. "Thank you for coming in."

She closed the door behind her, the click of the latch sounding loud in the quiet room. She walked to her desk and placed the folder on the surface, her movements precise and deliberate. She pulled out the chair on her side of the desk and sat down, her posture straight, her hands resting on the folder.

"Please," she said, gesturing to the chair across from her. "Sit."

Adrian shifted in his seat, straightening his back. He felt the weight of her gaze, a physical pressure that seemed to tighten the muscles in his shoulders. He did not speak. He waited, his hands resting on his thighs, his breathing slow and controlled.

Dr. Thorne opened the folder, her fingers tracing the edge of the papers. She did not look at him immediately. She glanced at the clock on the wall, a small digital display that showed the time in bright green numbers. 8:15 AM. She had taken her time.

"The trial has been ongoing for two weeks," she said, her tone conversational but firm. "We have received the data from your initial samples. The results are... promising."

Adrian said nothing. He did not want to interrupt. He did not want to show that he was listening, that he was paying attention, that he was afraid of what "promising" might mean. He kept his eyes fixed on the folder in her hands, the white pages stark against the dark cover.

"However," Dr. Thorne continued, her voice dropping slightly, "there are administrative requirements that must be fulfilled before we can proceed to the next phase of the study. This is a standard procedure for all participants in the experimental-medicine program. It ensures that the hospital has the necessary legal authority to act in your best interest should an unexpected emergency occur."

She looked up, her pale eyes meeting his. There was no malice in her gaze, no hidden agenda. It was a look of clinical detachment, of professional duty. She was a doctor. She was following a protocol. And Adrian, despite his exhaustion, despite his fear, knew that he had no choice but to listen.

He nodded, a small, stiff movement. "I understand," he said. His voice was hoarse, barely a whisper.

Dr. Thorne nodded in return. She picked up a pen from the desk, a simple black ballpoint, and held it out to him.

"Please," she said. "Sign here."

The scene ends with Adrian seated in Room 4, the pen in his hand, the document open before him, and Dr. Thorne watching him with clinical detachment.

The walk to the clinic took twenty minutes, a duration that felt like an hour because of the distance between his feet and the ground. The streetlights buzzed with a low, electrical hum that Adrian felt in his teeth. He adjusted the strap of his bag, which contained only his wallet and a crumpled receipt from the diner, and kept his eyes on the pavement. The concrete was uneven, and every slight rise in the ground required a conscious decision to lift his leg. His muscles were leaden, heavy with a fatigue that sleep did not seem to touch.

When he reached the clinic, the glass doors slid open with a pneumatic sigh. The lobby was empty, the reception desk lit by a single, harsh fluorescent panel. The receptionist was there, her gray eyes scanning the entryway before they settled on Adrian. She offered a thin, professional smile that did not reach her face.

"Mr. Vale. You’re right on time for the final block," she said. Her voice was steady, clipped by the routine of the place.

Adrian nodded, unable to speak above a whisper. He pointed a trembling finger toward the corridor at the back of the room.

"Dr. Thorne is expecting you," the receptionist said. "Room 4. She’ll be with you in a moment."

Adrian walked the hallway. The carpet was a muted blue, thick under his shoes, absorbing the sound of his steps. He passed the closed doors of the examination rooms, each one identical to the others. His hands were shaking again, a fine tremor that started in his wrists and traveled up his forearms. He clenched his fists against his sides, forcing the muscles to contract, hoping the tension would steady them. It did not.

Room 4 was open. Dr. Thorne was sitting at her desk, a stack of paperwork arranged in neat piles. She looked up as he entered, her pale eyes assessing him with a detached clinical interest.

"Sit down, Mr. Vale," she said.

Adrian lowered himself into the chair opposite her. The movement made his head swim, and the room tilted slightly before righting itself. He kept his hands on his knees, interlacing his fingers to hide the tremor.

"How are you feeling today?" Dr. Thorne asked. She did not look at his face. She was looking at a folder on her desk.

"Fine," Adrian said. The word felt foreign in his mouth. He had said it to the barista, to the manager at the diner, to the empty room of his apartment. It was a wall he had built to keep the rest of the world at a distance, a way to say that he was functional, that he was present, that he was not the shivering, hollowed-out thing he felt like inside.

Dr. Thorne made a small mark on her paper. "Good. I trust you took the medication as directed this morning?"

"Yes."

"Any nausea?"

"None."

Adrian’s stomach tightened. It was a lie, but it was the only thing that kept him in the room. He could hear the hum of the ventilation system in the ceiling, a constant, rhythmic drone that seemed to press against his eardrums.

Dr. Thorne closed the folder. She reached under her desk and pulled out a different document, a single sheet of paper clipped to a manila folder. She slid it across the desk.

"Before we proceed with the final administrative steps of your enrollment, there is one more form I need you to review and sign," she said.

Adrian looked at the paper. The title was printed in bold, black letters: **Emergency Intervention Authorization**.

He picked up his pen. His hand shook so badly that the first line of text blurred into a wavy mess. He set the pen down and picked it up again, breathing slowly, counting the seconds in his head until his vision cleared.

"Is this mandatory?" he asked.

"It is a requirement for all participants in the experimental-medicine program," Dr. Thorne said. Her tone was flat, informative. She did not offer a smile, nor did she offer a pause. "It grants the research team permission to utilize any necessary experimental treatments should a medical crisis occur that standard protocol cannot address."

Adrian read the first paragraph. The words were dense, filled with legal jargon and medical terminology that seemed to dance on the page. *Uncontrolled hemorrhage.* *Multi-organ failure.* *Cellular instability.*

He looked up at Dr. Thorne. "What kind of treatments?"

"Any that are deemed medically necessary to preserve life," she said.

Adrian felt a cold spot form in the center of his chest. He had signed the consent form for the fertility trial because he knew what it involved: the daily visits, the samples, the medication. It was a transaction he understood. He gave them his time and his body; they gave him the money he needed to survive. But this was different. This was a blank check. This was a surrender of control over what would happen to him if his body failed.

"Does this apply if I’m outside the clinic?" he asked.

"If you are experiencing a medical emergency related to the trial, yes," Dr. Thorne said.

Adrian stared at the paper. He thought about the diner, the grease-stained floor, the noise of the coffee machine. He thought about the walk back to his apartment, the darkness of the stairwell. He thought about the money in his account, the rent due in three days, the tuition that would be overdue by the end of the month.

If he did not sign, he would not be removed from the trial immediately. But the enrollment would be incomplete. The payments would stop. The safety net he had so desperately woven would unravel.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was waiting. Her posture was relaxed, her hands resting on the desk. She did not look concerned, nor did she look impatient. She simply existed in the space, a fact of the world, as inevitable as the hum of the air conditioning.

Adrian picked up the pen again. The tip of the ballpoint pressed into the paper, leaving a small, dark dot. He did not write. He just held the pen there, feeling the weight of it, the small, cold metal against his skin.

"I need to read it," he said.

Dr. Thorne nodded. "Take your time."

Adrian began to read. The words were small, and his eyes kept slipping off the lines. He had to keep his finger on the page to track the sentences, a slow, deliberate motion that felt like tracing a path through fog.

*The participant acknowledges that the use of experimental treatments may involve risks that are unknown or unquantifiable...*

He read the sentence again. *Unknown or unquantifiable.*

He thought about the shivering in his hands. He thought about the way the light from the desk lamp felt like it was burning into his skin. He thought about the empty feeling in his stomach, the hunger that had become a constant, dull ache.

If something happened to him, if his body broke in a way that the fertility drug could not fix, they would be allowed to try anything. They would be allowed to change him in ways he could not predict, in ways he could not control.

He looked at the signature line at the bottom of the page. It was blank, white, waiting.

Adrian set the pen down. He leaned back in the chair, the metal scraping against the desk. The sound was sharp, sudden, and it made him flinch.

He was still in the room. He was still in the chair. The paper was on the desk. The pen was in his hand.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was watching him. Her expression was unreadable, a mask of clinical neutrality.

"I have questions," Adrian said.

Dr. Thorne did not move. "Ask them," she said.

Adrian opened his mouth to speak, but the words did not come. He looked back at the paper. The title still stared up at him: **Emergency Intervention Authorization**.

He picked up the pen. His hand was steady now, or at least it felt steady. He pressed the tip to the paper and began to write. The ink flowed smoothly, forming the letters of his name.

A. V a l e.

He signed the document. He did not look up when he finished. He simply set the pen down and pushed the paper across the desk toward Dr. Thorne.

Dr. Thorne picked up the document. She examined the signature, her pale eyes scanning the page. She made a small mark on her own paper, then placed the authorization into a different folder.

"Thank you, Mr. Vale," she said.

Adrian sat in the chair. The hum of the ventilation system seemed louder now, a constant, pressing presence. He felt a strange lightness in his chest, as if a weight had been lifted, or as if a void had been filled with something cold and hard.

He looked at the desk, at the neat piles of paperwork, at the red emergency button that sat on the far edge, unused, waiting.

He was still in the room. He was still in the chair. The air was cool, and the light was bright, and he was waiting.

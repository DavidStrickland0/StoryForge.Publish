The walk to the clinic took twenty minutes, a duration that stretched under the weight of Adrian’s own limbs. The air in the late afternoon had turned humid, clinging to his skin like a damp cloth. He could feel the heat radiating off the pavement through the soles of his sneakers, a sensation that had become uncomfortably distinct since the first dose. His hands, tucked into the pockets of his jacket, trembled with a low, persistent vibration that made the fabric rustle against his palms. He walked slowly, keeping his eyes on the middle distance to avoid the blur that crept in whenever he tried to focus on a single point.

He arrived at the clinic building just as the sun began to dip below the skyline, casting long, sharp shadows across the parking lot. The glass front of the building reflected the orange sky, a warm glow that felt alien against the cold, sterile interior he knew awaited him. He pushed through the automatic doors, the rush of air conditioning hitting his face and raising the hairs on his arms. The lobby was quiet, the reception desk empty for a moment before a chair creaked and the receptionist, a woman with gray eyes and a precise posture, looked up from her computer screen.

"Mr. Vale," she said, her voice flat and professional. "You’re a few minutes late for your scheduled window."

Adrian stopped, his breath catching in his throat. He checked the small digital clock on the wall. It was 6:22 PM. His appointment was for 6:00, but the protocol allowed for a grace period. "I’m here," he said, his voice sounding thinner than he intended. "I’m ready for the procedure."

The receptionist nodded, tapping a few keys. "Step through to the examination area. Nurse Halloway will be with you in Room 2. Dr. Thorne is in Room 4, but you need to complete the sample collection first. Do not go to her office until you have submitted the specimen."

Adrian felt a wave of dizziness, a sudden tilt in the world that he countered by gripping the edge of the reception desk. The wood was cool and smooth under his fingers, a solid anchor. He forced his legs to move, walking down the corridor with a deliberate, shuffling gait. The linoleum floor was polished to a mirror shine, reflecting his pale, haggard face back at him. He avoided the reflection, keeping his eyes on the door of Room 2.

Inside, the room was identical to the others: white walls, a metal examination table, and a small sink in the corner. Nurse Halloway stood by the counter, a clipboard in her hand. She was a younger woman, with a no-nonsense demeanor that masked a certain weariness. She didn’t look up when he entered.

"Close the door," she said.

Adrian closed the door, the latch clicking with a sound that seemed too loud in the silence. He stood by the table, his heart hammering against his ribs. The medication in his system made his pulse feel like a frantic bird trapped in his chest. He could feel the sweat breaking out on his forehead, a cold, prickly sensation that had nothing to do with the temperature.

"Sit down," the nurse said, gesturing to the chair. "We need to do the collection. The usual procedure."

Adrian sat. The chair was hard, the metal legs scraping against the floor. He placed his hands on his knees, trying to steady the tremors. His fingers were numb, the tips tingling with a static charge that made it difficult to grip his own hands. He looked at the nurse, who was already preparing the specimen cup on the counter.

"Is there anything I should know?" he asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

The nurse looked up, her eyes scanning his face. She noticed the pallor, the dark circles under his eyes, the way his skin seemed translucent. She didn’t ask about his condition. She didn’t offer a glass of water or a cracker. She simply nodded. "Just do your best. The researchers are looking for consistency. If you’re struggling, tell me now, and I’ll log it as a side effect. If you don’t, and you fail to produce a viable sample, it goes on your record."

Adrian swallowed, his throat dry. He knew the stakes. A failed sample meant a missed payment. A missed payment meant a delay in his rent. A delay in his rent meant eviction. He couldn’t afford to fail. He couldn’t afford to be honest.

"I’m fine," he said, the lie tasting like ash in his mouth. "I’m just tired."

The nurse nodded, turning back to the counter. "Take your time. I’ll be in the hall. If you need anything, press the button."

She left, the door clicking shut behind her. Adrian was alone in the room. The silence was heavy, pressing against his eardrums. He looked at the specimen cup on the counter, the white plastic stark against the stainless steel surface. He felt a surge of nausea, a physical rejection of the task at hand. His body, saturated with the experimental compound, felt foreign to him. Every nerve ending seemed to be firing at once, a cacophony of sensations that made it hard to think, let alone perform.

He stood up, his legs shaking. He walked to the counter, his movements slow and deliberate. He picked up the cup, the plastic warm from the ambient heat of the room. He held it in his hands, feeling the weight of it, the reality of it. He closed his eyes, taking a deep breath. The air smelled of antiseptic and something metallic, the scent of the clinic itself. He focused on the sensation, grounding himself in the physical. He could feel the texture of the cup, the coolness of the air on his skin, the steady, frantic beat of his heart.

It took twenty minutes. Twenty minutes of standing, of trying to ignore the tremors in his hands, of fighting the cognitive fog that threatened to swallow him whole. When he finally finished, he set the cup on the counter with a trembling hand. He washed his hands, the water cold and shocking against his skin. He dried them on a paper towel, his fingers still shaking.

He walked out of Room 2 and down the corridor to Room 4. The door was closed. He knocked, his knuckles tapping against the wood with a faint, rhythmic sound.

"Come in," Dr. Thorne’s voice said, calm and measured.

Adrian opened the door and stepped inside. The office was small, cluttered with papers and medical equipment. Dr. Thorne sat at her desk, her dark bob cut sharp and neat, her pale eyes fixed on a computer screen. She didn’t look up immediately. She finished typing a few lines, then turned to face him.

"Mr. Vale. You’re here."

"Yes, Dr. Thorne."

She gestured to the chair across from her desk. "Sit down."

Adrian sat, his legs feeling like they were made of lead. He placed his hands on his lap, interlacing his fingers to hide the tremors. He looked at the red emergency button on the desk, a small, bright dot of color in the sea of gray and white. He knew what it was for. He knew that if he pressed it, the trial would end. He would lose the money. He would lose his future.

Dr. Thorne looked at him, her expression unreadable. "How are you feeling today?"

Adrian met her gaze, forcing his voice to remain steady. "Fine. A bit tired, but fine."

Dr. Thorne nodded, her eyes lingering on his face for a second longer than necessary. She reached into a drawer and pulled out a folder. She placed it on the desk, sliding it across to him.

"We have one final step in your enrollment," she said. "Before we can finalize your participation in the broader experimental-medicine program, you need to sign the emergency-intervention authorization."

Adrian looked at the folder. His heart rate spiked, a sudden jolt of adrenaline that made his vision blur. He knew what this meant. He had read the initial consent forms, but this was different. This was the permission that would allow the hospital to use experimental treatments on him, treatments that were not yet approved for general use. Treatments that could save his life, or take it away.

He opened the folder. The pages were thick, the text dense and small. He began to read, his eyes scanning the lines, his mind struggling to keep up. The words blurred and shifted, his concentration slipping away. He forced himself to focus, reading the first paragraph again, then the second. It was a legal document, filled with jargon and clauses that he couldn’t fully understand. But the meaning was clear. He was surrendering control. He was giving them the right to intervene, to operate, to treat, regardless of his own wishes.

He looked up at Dr. Thorne. She was watching him, her expression neutral. She didn’t offer any reassurance. She didn’t explain the risks or the benefits. She simply waited.

Adrian looked back at the document. His hand hovered over the pen. The tremor in his fingers was strong now, a visible shake that made the pen wobble in his grip. He closed his eyes, taking a deep breath. He thought about the rent. He thought about the tuition. He thought about the hunger that gnawed at his stomach, the exhaustion that weighed on his bones. He thought about the alternative.

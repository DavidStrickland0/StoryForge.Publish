The hallway to the clinic was dim, lit by the low-humming tubes that buzzed with a faint, persistent vibration Adrian felt in his teeth. The air smelled of antiseptic and old carpet, a scent that had become as familiar and uninviting as his own skin. He walked slowly, each step a negotiation with his knees, which had begun to ache with a dull, grinding pressure that the medication’s side effects seemed to amplify rather than soothe. His bag hung heavy against his hip, the straps digging into shoulders that felt raw under his t-shirt.

He reached the glass doors of the intake office and pushed them open. The chime above the door jingled, a bright, cheerful sound that felt obscenely out of place against the grey afternoon light filtering through the blinds.

The receptionist looked up from her computer. She had gray eyes, sharp and watchful, framed by a neat blonde bun that seemed to hold the day’s frustrations in check. Her fingers were poised over the keyboard, waiting.

"Mr. Vale," she said. Her voice was flat, professional, devoid of surprise or warmth. "You’re here for your sample."

"Yes," Adrian said. His voice sounded hollow to his own ears, scraped raw by the dry air of the diner and the effort of keeping his jaw unclenched. "I’m early. I wanted to make sure I had time to finish up before the afternoon rush at the diner."

It was a lie, or at least a half-truth. He hadn’t wanted to be early; he had wanted to be nowhere else. The walk from the diner had taken forty-five minutes, and the waiting room was the only place in the city where he was permitted to sit still without feeling like an intruder.

The receptionist glanced at the clock on her desk, then back at her screen. "Dr. Thorne is running behind. We’re still processing the morning’s data. Please take a seat."

She gestured to the row of plastic chairs along the wall. The chairs were bolted to the floor, their white plastic surfaces worn and yellowed at the edges. Adrian lowered himself into the first one, his movements slow and deliberate to avoid the tremor that seized his legs when he moved too quickly. The plastic was cold against his thighs, a small, sharp contrast to the heat that prickled under his skin.

He sat there, hands resting on his lap, fingers interlaced to keep them from shaking. The room was quiet, save for the murmur of the computer fan and the occasional shuffle of paper from behind the reception desk. A magazine rack stood in the corner, filled with glossy periodicals whose covers depicted smiling people doing things Adrian could no longer imagine wanting to do. *Healthy Living*, *City Guide*, *Tech Weekly*. The images were bright, saturated, and completely alien.

Adrian stared at the floor, watching a smudge of dirt on the tile work its way under his shoe. He counted the tiles from his position to the door. Twelve. He counted them again. Twelve. It was a simple task, but by the third count, the numbers had blurred together, the tiles merging into a single grey rectangle.

He forced himself to breathe. In through the nose, out through the mouth. The rhythm helped, a small anchor in the sea of static that had been growing inside his skull since the morning. His head throbbed behind his eyes, a rhythmic pulse that matched the hum of the lights. The light itself was too bright, a harsh white glare that seemed to penetrate his eyelids even when he squeezed them shut.

He opened his eyes and looked at the receptionist. She was typing, her lips moving slightly as she read something on her screen. She didn’t look up. To her, he was just another name on a list, a set of data points to be processed, a body to be weighed and measured and sampled. He was a line item in a ledger.

The realization should have made him angry. It didn’t. It just made him feel smaller, thinner, less real. He was already fading, his edges blurring into the background of this sterile, efficient world.

He shifted in his chair, the plastic creaking. The sound was louder than it should have been, a sharp squeak that cut through the quiet. The receptionist’s fingers paused on the keyboard.

"Is something the matter, Mr. Vale?" she asked, her tone still neutral, but with a slight edge of caution.

"No," Adrian said. "Just... shifting."

She nodded, as if this were a reasonable explanation for a nineteen-year-old man who looked like he hadn’t eaten in two days. She returned to her typing.

Adrian closed his eyes again. The darkness behind his lids was better, a soft, velvety black that offered no judgments, no bright lights, no ticking clocks. He tried to recall the last time he had felt this tired. It had been during his senior year of high school, when he had been working two jobs to save for a car that he would never actually buy. But that tiredness had been manageable, a weight he could set down at the end of the day. This was different. This was a hollowing out, a slow erosion of the solid self.

He thought about the payment. It was in his account, a number that represented food, rent, tuition. It was enough to keep the lights on for a month, enough to keep the eviction notice at bay. But it came with a price. Every day, he had to come here. Every day, he had to sit in this chair, wait for this woman to call his name, walk into that room, and do what he had to do.

The door to the examination room at the end of the hallway opened. A nurse emerged, her scrubs a crisp blue, her face set in a mask of professional efficiency. She walked past Adrian, her shoes clicking on the tile. She didn’t look at him. She didn’t look at anyone.

Adrian watched her go, then looked down at his hands. They were still, but only because he was holding them tightly together. Under the skin, his veins were visible, thin and blue. He flexed his fingers, feeling the resistance of the joints. They felt stiff, like old hinges that needed oiling.

The receptionist cleared her throat. "Mr. Vale."

He looked up. She was looking at him now, her gray eyes steady.

"Dr. Thorne is ready," she said. "Room 4."

Adrian stood up. The room seemed to tilt slightly, the floor shifting under his feet. He gripped the edge of the chair to steady himself, his knuckles white. The cold plastic bit into his palms, a sharp, grounding pain.

"Thank you," he said.

He walked toward the door, his steps slow and careful. The hallway stretched out before him, a long, white corridor that seemed to go on forever. At the end of it was Room 4, the door closed, the number painted in black on the white surface.

He reached the door and paused. His hand hovered over the handle, trembling slightly. He could turn back. He could walk out of the building, go to the diner, and work another shift. He could avoid this. He could avoid the medication, the sample, the questions, the judgment.

But then the rent would come due. The tuition would come due. The empty fridge would remain empty.

He pushed the door open.

The room was small, lit by the same harsh fluorescent lights. Dr. Thorne was sitting at her desk, her back straight, her pale eyes focused on the paperwork in front of her. She looked up as he entered, her expression unreadable.

"Come in, Mr. Vale," she said. "Please, have a seat."

Adrian stepped into the room, the door closing behind him with a soft click. He walked to the chair opposite her desk and sat down. The chair was leather, black and smooth, and it swallowed his heat instantly.

Dr. Thorne set down her pen. She picked up a folder from her desk and placed it on the table between them. It was a thick folder, its edges worn and soft from handling.

"Before we begin your procedure for the day," she said, her voice calm and measured, "there is one more document we need to review. It is part of the final enrollment process for the broader experimental medicine program."

Adrian looked at the folder. His throat tightened. He knew what this was. He had seen it in the initial intake packet, but he had assumed it was for later. He had assumed he would have time to think about it, to weigh the pros and cons, to find a way out.

He didn’t have time now.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was waiting, her face a mask of professional detachment. She did not offer sympathy. She did not offer reassurance. She simply waited.

Adrian reached out and opened the folder. The papers inside were dense with text, the font small and precise. He read the title, his eyes skimming over the words until they lost their meaning, until they were just shapes on a page.

*Emergency-Intervention Authorization.*

He looked up at Dr. Thorne. She met his gaze, her pale eyes steady. She nodded, once, a small, confirming gesture.

"Take your time," she said. "Read it carefully. It is important that you understand exactly what you are agreeing to."

The walk to the clinic took twenty minutes, a duration that felt significantly longer than the twenty minutes it actually spanned. Adrian’s legs felt filled with wet sand, each step requiring a conscious decision to lift one foot and place it down again. The city lights hummed with a low, electric buzz that seemed to vibrate directly against his eardrums, a persistent tinnitus that had settled into his skull hours ago and refused to leave. He kept his eyes fixed on the pavement, counting the cracks in the concrete to maintain a tether to reality. One, two, three. The numbers were anchors, solid and immovable, unlike the rest of the world which had begun to shimmer and blur at the edges.

When he finally reached the building, the automatic doors slid open with a pneumatic hiss that was too loud in the quiet evening air. The lobby smelled of antiseptic and stale coffee, a scent that had become as familiar and nauseating as the taste of his own blood. He walked past the reception desk, his fingers brushing the cold metal of the handrail for support. The floor was polished marble, slippery and unforgiving. He had nearly slipped twice on the way in, catching himself with a sharp intake of breath that he immediately suppressed. He could not afford to fall. He could not afford to make a scene.

The waiting area was a small, windowless room with three plastic chairs bolted to the floor. They were arranged in a shallow arc, facing a wall covered in informational posters. One poster depicted the life cycle of a sperm cell in bright, clinical blue. Another listed the dietary restrictions for the trial, a list that included almost everything Adrian could afford to eat. He sat in the middle chair, the plastic cold against his thigh. The chair was hard, designed for brief visits, not for a man whose muscles were beginning to tremble with a low, constant vibration that he could feel in his fingertips and his knees.

He looked at the clock on the wall. It was 7:42 PM. He had been scheduled for 8:00 AM, but the protocol required him to arrive early for the pre-administration check. Or perhaps it was the other way around. The details had blurred together after the first two weeks. He knew he had to be here. He knew the payment would not clear until he was signed in. He knew that if he was late, or if he was dismissed, he would not be able to pay for the groceries he had skipped two days ago. He did not know why the chair felt like it was slowly sinking into the floor, or why the fluorescent lights above were flickering in a rhythm that matched his own erratic heartbeat.

The door to the office opened, and the receptionist stepped out. She was a woman in her thirties with hair pulled back in a tight, severe bun. Her name tag read *Sarah*. She carried a clipboard and a pen, the pen clicking against the plastic cover with every step she took. Her expression was neutral, a mask of professional indifference that she had likely worn for years. She did not look at Adrian. She looked at the clipboard.

"Mr. Vale," she said. Her voice was flat, devoid of any inflection that might suggest warmth or curiosity. "You are late for your pre-assessment."

Adrian tried to stand, but his legs buckled slightly. He caught the arm of the chair, his knuckles whitening. "I'm here," he said. His voice sounded thin, reedy, as if it were coming from a distance. "I was walking."

"Your window is 8:00 AM," Sarah said, not looking up. "But Dr. Thorne has requested an earlier check-in for you today. There are adjustments to the dosage schedule. You need to be in Room 4 within ten minutes."

The mention of Dr. Thorne caused a spike of anxiety that Adrian felt in his gut, a cold knot tightening around his stomach. Adjustments. The word was vague, ominous, and completely meaningless in its specificity. He nodded, unable to trust his voice to form a question. He pushed himself up from the chair, his legs shaking violently. He had to walk to Room 4. The door was down the hall, to the left, past the nurse’s station.

He took a step, then another. The hallway was long, the walls a sterile beige. The air conditioning hummed from the vents in the ceiling. He could hear his own breathing, a wet, rattling sound that he tried to ignore. He reached the door to Room 4. It was slightly ajar, a sliver of light spilling out into the hallway. He pushed it open with his shoulder.

The room was small, cluttered with monitors and equipment. The red emergency button was on the desk, bright and accusing in the dim light. Dr. Thorne was not there. The chair was empty. The computer screen was blank, a dark mirror reflecting Adrian’s own pale, haggard face. He looked at the button, then at the empty chair. He was supposed to be in here for ten minutes. He was supposed to be assessed. He was supposed to be paid.

He stood in the doorway, his bag hanging limp from his hand. The weight of the bag was heavier than it had been an hour ago. The weight of the day was heavier still. He looked at the clock on the wall, the second hand ticking with a steady, indifferent precision. 7:45 PM. He had three minutes left. He could not wait here. He could not go back to the waiting area. He had to be somewhere. He had to do something. He turned and walked back out into the hallway, his footsteps echoing in the silent corridor.

The waiting area was empty. The plastic chairs were cold. The posters on the wall stared down at him, their bright colors mocking his exhaustion. He sat down again, the plastic biting into his skin. He closed his eyes. The darkness behind his eyelids was not black, but a swirling vortex of grey and blue, the colors of the sky, the colors of the sea, the colors of things he could not name. He waited. The minutes passed. The hum of the refrigerator in the kitchen next door was a constant drone, a reminder that the world was continuing, that life was going on, that he was just a small, broken part of a larger, indifferent machine.

He opened his eyes. The clock read 7:52 PM. He stood up, his legs trembling. He walked to the bathroom, the tiles cold under his feet. He turned on the tap, the water running clear and cold. He cupped his hands and drank, the water shocking his system, making him shiver. He looked at his reflection in the mirror. His face was gaunt, his eyes sunken. He looked like a ghost. He looked like a man who had given too much and received too little.

He turned off the tap. The water dripped from his chin, hitting the sink with a soft, rhythmic plop. He dried his face with a paper towel, the rough texture scratching against his skin. He was clean. He was present. He was here. And he was still waiting.

The door to the hallway opened. Sarah stepped out, the clipboard in her hand. She looked at Adrian, then at the clock. Her expression did not change, but her eyes lingered on him for a fraction of a second longer than usual. She turned and walked down the hallway, her footsteps clicking against the floor. Adrian followed, his legs carrying him forward despite the exhaustion that pulled him down. He was going to Room 4. He was going to sit in the chair. He was going to wait for Dr. Thorne. He was going to sign the paper. He was going to survive.

He pushed open the door to Room 4. The room was empty. The chair was empty. The red button glowed in the dim light. He sat down. The plastic was cold. The air was still. He waited.

The walk to the clinic took twenty minutes, a duration that felt both interminable and insufficient. The city at this hour was a blur of sodium-vapor orange and the occasional sharp white of a storefront sign. Adrian’s legs felt like they were filled with wet sand, each step a negotiation between his will to move and the heavy, dragging resistance of his muscles. The air was cool, carrying the faint, metallic scent of the river that bisected the district, but the temperature registered on his skin as a prickling, electric sting. His shirt, a thin cotton tee that had been damp with sweat since the second hour of his shift, clung to his torso. The fabric felt abrasive, a coarse grit against skin that had grown impossibly thin and reactive.

He adjusted the strap of his bag on his shoulder. The leather was warm against the bare patch of skin above his collarbone, a heat that flared into a sharp, localized pain. He winced, a small, involuntary twitch of the jaw, and shifted the weight to his other side. The tremor in his hands had not faded. It had settled into a low, persistent vibration, a hum that traveled up his forearms and into the bones of his skull. When he reached for his keys, his fingers fumbled, the metal jangling with a sound that was too loud, too sharp, a discordant shriek in the quiet street.

He found the correct key on the third try, the cold steel biting into his fingertips. The clinic door was heavy, commercial-grade steel that required a firm push to open. As he leaned his weight against it, the hinges groaned, a sound that seemed to vibrate through his feet and up his spine. He stepped inside, and the change in atmosphere was immediate. The air was sterile, filtered, and cold. It smelled of antiseptic and floor wax, a scent that usually signaled safety and order but now felt aggressive, stripping away the layer of grime and exhaustion he had carried with him from the diner.

The reception area was dimly lit, the overhead fluorescents buzzing with a faint, staccato rhythm. The receptionist was behind the desk, a woman with gray eyes and a stack of paperwork that seemed to defy gravity. She looked up as Adrian entered, her expression neutral, professional. She did not smile. She did not frown. She simply processed his arrival.

"Mr. Vale," she said. Her voice was flat, devoid of inflection. "You're here for your appointment."

"Yes," Adrian said. His voice sounded thin to his own ears, a dry rasp that scraped against the back of his throat. He swallowed, the motion painful. "I'm here for the sample."

The receptionist nodded, her gaze sweeping over him with the detached efficiency of a machine scanning a barcode. She did not acknowledge his pale complexion, the dark circles under his eyes, or the way he was leaning slightly against the doorframe as if the floor might give way. She typed something into her keyboard, the keys clicking in a rhythmic, indifferent cadence.

"Room 4," she said. "Dr. Thorne is expecting you. The nurse will meet you there."

Adrian pushed off the doorframe and walked toward the hallway. The floor was polished tile, slick and cold under his shoes. He could feel the vibration of the building’s air conditioning system through the soles of his feet, a subtle thrum that matched the tremor in his hands. He kept his eyes fixed on the middle distance, focusing on a point on the wall to steady his vision, which had begun to blur at the edges. The world was softening, the sharp lines of the hallway melting into a smear of beige and gray.

He reached the end of the hallway and turned the corner. Room 4 was at the end of the corridor, a double door marked with a small, white sign. The number *4* was printed in black, bold and clear. Adrian stopped outside the door, his hand hovering over the handle. The handle was cold, a sharp contrast to the heat that had been building in his chest. He could hear voices inside, low and indistinct, the murmur of clinical conversation.

He took a breath. The air tasted of nothing, a void that offered no comfort. He turned the handle and pushed the door open.

Room 4 was larger than the examination rooms he had seen in other clinics. The walls were a sterile white, the floor a light gray vinyl. A desk sat in the center of the room, cluttered with folders, a laptop, and a red emergency call button that sat prominently on the desktop. The button was bright, an aggressive splash of color in the monochrome environment. Adrian’s eyes were drawn to it, a fixed point of reference in the swirling fog of his perception.

Dr. Thorne was standing by the window, her back to the door. She was tall, her silhouette sharp against the dim light of the hallway. She turned as Adrian entered, her pale, almost colorless eyes meeting his. Her expression was unreadable, a mask of professional detachment.

"Adrian," she said. Her voice was calm, measured. "Come in."

Adrian stepped into the room. The door clicked shut behind him, the sound final and absolute. He felt the lock engage, a mechanical confirmation of his presence. He was in the room. He was in the protocol.

A nurse stood near the examination table, a young woman with dark hair pulled back in a tight bun. She held a clipboard, her pen poised over the paper. She looked at Adrian, her gaze clinical and assessing.

"Name?" she asked.

"Adrian Vale," he said.

"Date of birth?"

"August twelfth, twenty-oh-four."

The nurse wrote, her pen moving swiftly across the paper. Adrian watched her hand, the movement precise and efficient. He felt a surge of frustration, a hot, prickly irritation that rose in his throat. Why did he have to say it? Why did he have to be reduced to a date of birth, a name, a set of data points? He was a person, a body, a mind. He was here because he needed the money. He was here because he had no choice.

"Vital signs," the nurse said. She moved toward him, her movements fluid and economical. She held a blood pressure cuff, the rubber cool and smooth. She wrapped it around his upper arm, her fingers touching his skin. The contact sent a jolt of sensation through his nerves, a sharp, electric spike that made him flinch.

The nurse paused, her eyes flicking to his face. She did not comment. She simply tightened the cuff, her grip firm. The pressure built, a steady, unyielding force against his skin. Adrian’s heart rate was elevated, he could feel it pounding in his ears, a rapid, frantic drumbeat that matched the tremor in his hands. He tried to slow his breathing, to force his body into a state of calm, but his muscles would not respond. They remained taut, coiled, ready to spring.

The nurse listened to his heartbeat, the stethoscope head cold against his chest. She checked his pulse, her fingers pressing against his wrist. She noted the rate, the rhythm, the strength. She did not ask how he was feeling. She did not ask if he was in pain. She recorded the data, a set of numbers that would be entered into a file, a record that would be reviewed by someone who would never see his face, never hear his voice, never know the cost of those numbers.

Adrian stood still, his arms at his sides, his hands trembling. The room felt smaller, the walls closing in. The air was thick, heavy with the scent of antiseptic and the hum of the air conditioning. He felt the weight of his compliance, the physical and psychological burden of his decision. He had signed the forms. He had agreed to the protocol. He had surrendered a part of his autonomy in exchange for survival. And now, here, in this room, he felt the full extent of that surrender.

The nurse finished her examination. She uncapped the pen and wrote a final note. She looked at Adrian, her expression neutral.

"You're ready," she said.

Adrian nodded. He could not speak. His throat was tight, his voice gone. He felt a wave of nausea, a rolling, churning sensation in his stomach. He pressed his lips together, forcing down the urge to retch. He would not be sick. He would not be weak. He would endure.

He walked to the desk. Dr. Thorne was waiting, her hands resting on the surface. She looked at him, her eyes steady, unblinking. She did not offer a chair. She did not offer a glass of water. She simply waited.

Adrian placed his hand on the desk. The surface was cool, smooth. He felt the vibration of the laptop fan, a low, constant hum. He looked at the red emergency button. It was bright, aggressive, a promise of help that he knew he would not need. He would not press it. He would not break the protocol. He would endure.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was watching him, her gaze sharp and assessing. She saw his exhaustion. She saw his fear. She saw his desperation. And she said nothing.

The silence in the room was heavy, pressing down on him. It was a physical weight, a tangible force that bore down on his shoulders, his chest, his mind.

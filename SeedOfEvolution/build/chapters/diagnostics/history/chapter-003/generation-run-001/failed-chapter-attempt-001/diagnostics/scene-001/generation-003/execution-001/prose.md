The walk from the diner to the clinic usually took ten minutes if the traffic was light, twenty if it wasn’t. Today, with his legs feeling like they were filled with wet sand and his vision swimming in a haze of grey static, it felt like a trek across a desert. The evening air, which should have been cool and crisp, pressed against his skin with a gritty, abrasive weight. Every step sent a dull throb through his calves, a reminder that his body was running on empty.

He clutched the strap of his bag. His knuckles were white, the skin stretched tight over the bone, trembling slightly. The streetlights above cast pools of yellow light that seemed to ripple at the edges, a visual artifact of his exhausted eyes. He blinked hard, trying to clear the blur, but the city didn’t sharpen. It just pulsed.

When he reached the clinic, the building looked different. It was no longer just a sterile, modern structure of glass and steel; it was a monolith of white tiles and humming air conditioning. The automatic doors slid open with a soft hiss, admitting him into the intake office.

The receptionist was behind the counter, her gray eyes scanning a tablet screen. She looked up as Adrian entered, her expression neutral, professional.

"Mr. Vale. You're here for your evening visit," she said. Her voice was flat, clipped. She didn't ask how he was. She didn't comment on the fact that he was leaning heavily against the doorframe, breathing in shallow, controlled bursts to keep the dizziness at bay.

"Yes," Adrian managed. His voice sounded thin to his own ears, a reed-like rasp.

"Dr. Thorne is expecting you. Room 4. The nurse will meet you at the elevator."

Adrian nodded, turning toward the bank of elevators. His hands were shaking now, a fine, persistent tremor that he had been suppressing since he left the diner. He slid them into his pockets, his fingers digging into his own thighs to ground himself. The tactile sensation of his own fabric against his skin was overwhelming, the texture of the denim feeling like rough sandpaper against his hypersensitive epidermis. He winced, pulling his hands out, letting them dangle by his sides, the tremor continuing its erratic dance.

The elevator car was cold. The air conditioning was set to a temperature that felt arctic against his flushed, overheated skin. He pressed himself against the back wall, closing his eyes. The hum of the machinery was a low, droning vibration that seemed to rattle directly into his teeth. He counted his breaths. In for four. Hold for four. Out for four. It was a trick he had picked up from a stress-management video on his phone, a memory that felt like it belonged to a different person.

When the doors opened on the fourth floor, a nurse was waiting.

She was a woman in her forties, with short, practical hair and a face that had seen too many tired patients to offer false comfort. She wore a light blue scrubs uniform, her shoes squeaking softly as she took a step forward.

"Adrian," she said. It wasn't a question. She didn't smile. "Let's see how you're holding up."

He followed her out of the elevator. The hallway was tiled in white ceramic, the surface polished to a mirror shine. The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed, a sound that seemed to amplify the ringing in his ears. Adrian kept his gaze fixed on the floor, tracing the grout lines between the tiles. It was a focus anchor, a way to keep the world from tilting.

They walked to Room 4. The door was open, a narrow slit of light spilling out into the corridor. Adrian could see the edge of the desk, the chair, the red emergency button sitting innocuously on the surface. It looked like a toy, a plastic piece of red plastic that was supposed to save his life. He felt a strange, detached curiosity about it. What would happen if he pressed it? Would someone come? Would they stop the process? Would they kick him out?

The nurse guided him inside. "Sit down," she said.

Adrian lowered himself into the chair. The cushion was firm, providing a stark contrast to the softness he expected. He gripped the arms of the chair, his knuckles whitening again. The room was quiet, save for the distant hum of the building’s systems and the faint, rhythmic beep of a monitor in the adjacent room.

The nurse pulled a stool closer to his side. She didn't sit. She stood, leaning slightly, her hands clasped in front of her. Her eyes moved over him, assessing. They checked his skin, his posture, the color of his lips. She was looking for signs of distress, or perhaps just signs of the drug’s effect.

"How are you feeling?" she asked. The question was formulaic, but her tone was different from the receptionist's. There was a slight undercurrent of inquiry, a request for data rather than a social pleasantry.

Adrian opened his mouth. The word "fine" hung in the air, ready to be released. It was the truth, in a sense. He was not in pain. He was not dying. He was just... emptied. Hollowed out.

"I'm okay," he said. The words tasted like ash.

The nurse nodded slowly. She didn't buy it, but she didn't press. She reached into her pocket and pulled out a small clipboard. "Dr. Thorne will be with you in a few minutes. In the meantime, I need to take your vitals again. It’s standard protocol for the second week."

Adrian nodded, relaxing his grip on the chair arms just slightly. He watched as the nurse retrieved a blood pressure cuff from a cabinet. The plastic of the cuff felt cold against his forearm when she wrapped it. The inflation was slow, a gentle squeeze that tightened around his bicep. He could feel the pulse in his own vein, a steady, thumping rhythm that seemed too loud, too insistent.

The nurse checked the gauge, her eyes narrowing slightly. She adjusted the cuff, tightening it a fraction more. Adrian flinched, the pressure feeling like a vice. He said nothing. He just sat there, enduring the sensation, watching the needle on the gauge climb.

When she finished, she wrote something on her clipboard. She didn't look at him as she did. She simply moved to the next task, checking his heart rate with a stethoscope. The cold metal diaphragm pressed against his chest, right over the center of his ribcage. He could hear his own heartbeat, a frantic, galloping sound that seemed to echo in the small room.

The nurse listened for a long moment. Her expression remained neutral, but her pen paused on the clipboard. She looked up at Adrian, her gaze steady and unblinking.

"Your heart rate is elevated," she said. It was a statement, not an observation. "And your blood pressure is higher than your last reading."

Adrian swallowed. The dryness in his throat was painful, a scraping sensation. "I know," he said.

The nurse tilted her head. "Do you know what's causing it?"

Adrian looked away, focusing on the red emergency button on the desk. It was a small, bright thing, a beacon of hope in the grey monotony of the room. "Stress," he said. "And the medication."

The nurse nodded again. She removed the stethoscope and hung it around her neck. "Dr. Thorne will be here in a minute. I'll leave you to collect yourself."

She turned and walked out of the room, the door clicking shut behind her. Adrian was alone. The silence rushed back in, filling the space where her presence had been. He took a deep breath, the air sharp and cold in his lungs. He could feel the tremor in his hands, a constant, low-level vibration that he could no longer hide from himself.

He looked at the desk. The papers were scattered, the consent forms, the daily logs, the medical charts. It was a bureaucracy of the body, a system of tracking and control. And he was a part of it. A data point. A variable.

He reached out and touched the red button. The plastic was smooth, cool to the touch. He traced the edge of it with his finger, the movement deliberate, precise. It was a small thing, a small piece of plastic, but it represented something massive. It represented the surrender of control. It represented the possibility of a failure so severe that the only option was to stop.

Adrian pulled his hand back. He sat upright, his spine straight, his face a mask of calm. He was ready. He had to be. He needed the money. He needed the trial. He needed the next payment to hit his account, to keep the lights on, to keep the food coming, to keep his life afloat.

He closed his eyes, listening to the hum of the room, the beating of his own heart. He was waiting.

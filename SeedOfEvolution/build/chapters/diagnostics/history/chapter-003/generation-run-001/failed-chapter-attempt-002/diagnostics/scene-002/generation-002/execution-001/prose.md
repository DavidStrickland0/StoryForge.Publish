The words swam. That was the problem. They weren’t static ink on a page; they were living things, drifting out of focus as his eyes tried to lock onto them. Adrian blinked hard, the motion sending a fresh wave of vertigo rolling through his skull. The paper trembled in his hands, the edges blurring into a gray smear. He forced his fingers to tighten, the skin of his palms slick with sweat that had nothing to do with the temperature in the room.

He looked up. Dr. Thorne was watching him.

She didn’t blink. Her pale eyes were fixed on the document in his hands, then on his face, then back to the paper. It was a clinical assessment, cold and precise. She waited. There was no impatience in her posture, no urgency. She simply occupied the space, a tall, lean silhouette against the sterile white of the clinic wall, radiating a stillness that felt heavier than any demand she could have voiced.

"Read it," she said. Her voice was low, stripped of inflection. "Take your time."

*Take your time.* The phrase was a lie. Adrian could feel the clock ticking behind his teeth, the same relentless, grinding rhythm that had driven him to this room in the first place. The rent notice on his desk. The empty fridge. The tuition invoice that glowed red in his email inbox, a digital scar he couldn’t scrub away. He had no time. He had never had time. Time was a luxury he could only buy in fragments, and right now, he was spending the last of his currency to keep from being evicted into the cold.

He looked back down at the page. The first paragraph was a blur of legalese, a wall of words designed to be read, not understood. *Full and unrestricted access.* *Experimental intervention.* *Life-threatening emergency.* The phrases repeated themselves, a mantra of surrender. He traced the line with his eyes, his brain fighting to parse the meaning behind the syntax. It was a legal trap, or perhaps a safety net. The distinction didn’t matter. What mattered was that his body was no longer his own.

A tremor started in his left hand, a violent, jerking spasm that sent the pen sliding across the table. He caught it, his fingers wrapping around the plastic shaft. His heart was hammering against his ribs, a frantic, arrhythmic pounding that matched the shaking in his legs. He could feel the heat in his cheeks, the prickling sensitivity in his skin, as if the air itself were touching him with a thousand tiny needles.

"Dr. Thorne," he started. His voice sounded thin, scraped raw.

She tilted her head. A fraction of an inch. "Yes."

"Does this... does this cover the medication?" He asked. "The side effects?"

The question hung in the air, fragile and desperate. He needed to know. If the drug was breaking him, if his body was starting to fail, was he allowed to die? Or was he still required to produce?

Thorne’s expression did not change. She didn’t soften. She didn’t offer a reassurance, a hint of hope, or a medical caveat. She simply looked at him, her gaze sharp and unyielding.

"The authorization covers any emergency intervention required to preserve your life," she said, her words measured and deliberate. "It is a prerequisite for continued enrollment. Without it, you cannot receive further doses of the investigational compound."

The silence that followed was deafening. Adrian stared at the pen in his hand. It was a cheap, black ballpoint, the kind the clinic provided in a stack on the desk. It felt heavy. It felt like a key.

He thought of the diner. The grease-stained apron he’d worn for three days straight. The way the manager had rolled his eyes when Adrian asked for an extra shift, the way the customers stared when he stumbled over the counter, his movements jerky and unnatural. He thought of his apartment, the peeling paint, the draft under the door, the silence that pressed against his eardrums until he couldn’t tell where the quiet ended and his own thoughts began. He thought of the money. The small, recurring deposit that hit his account every week. The only thing standing between him and the street.

He looked at Dr. Thorne again. She was waiting. She had all the time in the world. He had none.

The words on the page were still swimming, but the meaning had settled into a cold, hard block in his stomach. It wasn’t a choice, really. It was a transaction. His body for his survival. His autonomy for his rent. The logic was simple, brutal, and final.

He picked up the pen.

His hand shook as he lifted it, the tip hovering over the signature line. The paper trembled beneath his gaze. He could feel the sweat on his forehead, the heat in his chest, the terrifying speed of his own pulse. It was a terrifying thing, this surrender. It was a hole in the world, a gap in the wall of his own identity that he was willingly opening.

But the alternative was worse. The alternative was the dark. The alternative was the end.

Adrian took a breath. It rattled in his lungs, a shallow, rasping sound. He looked at the line. He looked at the pen. And then, with a steady, deliberate motion that fought against every shivering nerve in his body, he brought the tip down.

The ink bled into the paper. A dark, permanent mark.

He finished the name. *Adrian Vale.*

He set the pen down. It made a small, sharp click against the desk, a sound that seemed to echo in the sterile room. He let out a breath he didn’t realize he’d been holding. His hands were still trembling, but the shaking was different now. It was the shaking of a man who had just let go of something heavy, something he hadn’t known he was carrying until it was gone.

He picked up the document. It felt light, almost insubstantial. He slid it across the desk toward Dr. Thorne.

She reached out and took it. Her fingers brushed his, a fleeting, cool touch that sent a shiver up his arm. She took the pen from the table and placed it on the desk, aligning it with the edge of the paper. A small, precise gesture.

"Thank you," she said.

It was a simple sentence. It wasn’t warm. It wasn’t kind. It was an acknowledgment. A receipt.

Thorne turned to her computer, her movements efficient and fluid. She began to type, the sound of the keys a rapid, rhythmic clicking that filled the silence. Adrian sat back in the plastic chair. The legs of the chair squeaked slightly as he shifted his weight.

He watched her. He watched her pale hands moving over the keyboard, her head bent slightly over the screen. She was working. She was processing. She was filing him away.

The realization settled over him, a cold, heavy blanket. He was done. The bureaucracy was complete. The final hurdle was cleared. He was enrolled. He was part of the program. He was theirs.

And he was still starving.

The hunger was a dull, persistent ache in his gut, a reminder of the physical reality he was trying to escape. He looked at the clock on the wall. The second hand swept across the face, marking the passing of time. One minute. Two minutes.

He was safe. He was enrolled. He was alive.

For now.

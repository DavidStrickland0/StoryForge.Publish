The walk to the clinic had taken longer than it should have. Adrian’s legs felt like they were filled with wet sand, each step a deliberate negotiation with gravity that his body was losing. The city lights smeared into long, horizontal streaks as he blinked, a constant, annoying flicker at the periphery of his vision. By the time he reached the glass doors of the research facility, his chest was heaving in a way that had nothing to do with the mild evening air.

He pushed through the entrance, the automatic sensors sliding the heavy panels open with a soft hydraulic hiss. The lobby was bright, sterile, and aggressively white. The fluorescent lights hummed with a frequency that seemed to vibrate directly against the base of his skull. Adrian leaned against the wall for a second, his hand gripping the cool metal of a banister to steady his trembling fingers. He could feel the heat radiating from his own skin, a low-grade fever that the medication had settled into his system like a persistent guest who refused to leave.

At the front desk, the receptionist was typing on a keyboard with rhythmic, detached precision. She had gray eyes that seemed to look through people rather than at them. She didn’t look up as Adrian approached, but her voice cut through the quiet hum of the air conditioning.

"Mr. Vale. You're running a few minutes behind the scheduled window."

Adrian managed to keep his voice level, though it felt thin and reedy in his own ears. "Traffic was bad."

It was a lie. He had walked. But the truth—that his legs had nearly buckled twice on the sidewalk and that he was currently trying to keep his stomach from turning over—was too much to articulate to a stranger who cared only about the timestamps in her computer.

She finally looked at him. Her gaze swept over his pale face, the sheen of sweat on his forehead, and the way his shoulders were hunched as if he were trying to make himself smaller. She didn't comment on it. She simply typed a few more keys.

"Room 4. Dr. Thorne is expecting you. Try to keep the hallway traffic to a minimum; the other participants are in the waiting area, and I don't want them to see you looking like you're about to pass out."

The comment was clipped, professional, and devoid of sympathy. Adrian nodded, his throat tight. He turned and walked toward the elevator, his bag clutched in one hand. The straps dug into his fingers, a sharp, grounding pain that helped keep him anchored to the present moment.

The elevator ride was a blur of ascending numbers and the subtle rise in pressure in his ears. When the doors opened on the fourth floor, the hallway was empty. The carpet was a deep, institutional blue that seemed to swallow the sound of his footsteps. Adrian walked slowly, his eyes fixed on the middle distance to avoid the dizziness that came from focusing on the walls.

Room 4 was at the end of the hall. The door was slightly ajar. Adrian pushed it open, and the familiar, antiseptic scent of the clinic hit him. It was a smell he had come to associate with the only reliable source of income in his life, and for that reason, it was both comforting and nauseating.

Dr. Aris Thorne was standing at her desk, reviewing a tablet. She was tall, her dark bob cutting a sharp line against her pale skin. Her eyes, almost colorless, moved up from the screen to meet his. She didn't stand up. She didn't smile. She simply assessed him with the same cool, clinical detachment she had used during their first meeting.

"Sit," she said, gesturing to the chair opposite her desk.

Adrian moved to the chair. The plastic was cold against his back. He sat down, trying to keep his hands still in his lap. They were trembling, a fine, rapid vibration that he could feel in his bones. He closed his eyes for a second, inhaling through his nose, trying to force his heart rate down. It was no use. The medication was a chemical tide in his system, and he was just a small boat being tossed around by it.

Thorne turned back to her tablet. "How are you feeling today, Mr. Vale?"

The question was so direct, so stripped of conversational filler, that it felt like an accusation. Adrian opened his eyes. "Fine. Just a little tired from work."

Thorne’s pale eyes narrowed slightly. She looked at him for a long moment, her expression unreadable. Then she tapped the tablet, closing it with a soft click.

"We have a few administrative items to finalize before you can proceed with today’s sample," she said. Her voice was flat, carrying the weight of a procedure that had been performed on many others before him. "There is a final authorization form that requires your signature. It’s part of the broader experimental-medicine program enrollment."

Adrian’s stomach gave a painful lurch. He knew this was coming. He had seen the blank space in the initial consent form, the one that had been left open until the program’s legal team had cleared the final language. This was the piece that tied his body to the hospital’s emergency protocols.

Thorne reached into a drawer and pulled out a single sheet of paper. She placed it on the desk between them. It was a dense block of text, the font small and unyielding.

"Read it," she said.

Adrian reached for the paper. His fingers were stiff, the joints aching. He took the document, and the first thing he noticed was the weight of it. It felt heavier than a single sheet of paper should. He began to read.

The words blurred. The text swam on the page, the letters detaching from their lines and floating in a gray haze. He read the first paragraph, and then he read it again. The meaning was there, but it was slippery, escaping his grasp the moment he tried to hold onto it. He could feel the cognitive fog settling in, a thick, cottony substance that lined the inside of his skull.

*In the event of a life-threatening emergency, the participant hereby grants the institution full and unrestricted access to their medical history, biological samples, and physical body for the purpose of experimental intervention...*

Adrian stopped. He looked at the word *unrestricted*. The letters seemed to pulse on the page. He tried to focus on the next line, but his vision narrowed to a tunnel. The edges of the paper dissolved into a white blur. He could feel the tremors in his hands intensifying, the paper rattling slightly against his palms.

The air in the room felt thin. Adrian’s breath was shallow, each intake a small effort. He looked back down at the document. The text was still there, a wall of legal obligation that he had to decipher with a mind that felt like it was being slowly erased. He could feel the tremors in his legs, a deep, rhythmic shaking that made it difficult to keep his posture straight.

He was seated in Room 4, the document in his hands, the words swimming before his eyes. The decision to sign, to surrender the final piece of his autonomy to the hospital, was right there on the page, waiting for him. But his body was already beginning to fail, the medication’s grip tightening, threatening to pull him under before he could even finish reading the first page.

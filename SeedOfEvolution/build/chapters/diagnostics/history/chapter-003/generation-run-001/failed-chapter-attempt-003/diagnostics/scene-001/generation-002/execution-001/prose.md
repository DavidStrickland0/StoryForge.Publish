The walk to the clinic took longer than it should have. Adrian’s legs felt like they were filled with wet sand, each step a deliberate negotiation with gravity that drained more energy than the movement itself required. The city lights smeared into long, vibrating streaks across his vision, the neon signs of the shops he passed bleeding into one another until the street became a tunnel of indistinct color. He kept his eyes fixed on the concrete ten feet ahead of him, counting the cracks in the pavement to keep his mind anchored to something tangible. One, two, three. The numbers helped, in a way. They gave his thoughts a place to land before they dissolved into the grey static that was starting to hum behind his eyes again.

By the time he reached the clinic, the headache had moved from a dull throb to a sharp, jagged spike at the base of his skull. Every time his heartbeat thudded against his eardrums, the pain flared, a syncopated rhythm that made it difficult to breathe. He leaned against the glass doors for a moment, his palms flat against the cool surface. The glass was smooth and hard, a reassuring solid reality in a world that had started to feel like it was made of water. He waited until his breathing slowed, until the trembling in his hands subsided enough that he could grip the door handle without spilling the imaginary contents of his own blood.

Inside, the air conditioning hit him like a physical blow. It was too cold, too dry, stripping the moisture from his skin and making the faint, raw sensitivity of his arms tingle with a painful clarity. The lobby was quiet, the kind of sterile silence that seemed to amplify the sound of his own pulse. He walked to the reception desk, his movements slower than he intended, each step deliberate to avoid the dizzy spells that had been plaguing him since the library.

The receptionist was typing on her keyboard, her gray eyes fixed on the screen. She looked up when he approached, her expression neutral, professional. She did not acknowledge the way his shoulders hunched, the way his face was pale and drawn, or the fact that he was still wearing the same clothes from his diner shift, the uniform slightly wrinkled and smelling faintly of grease and coffee. To her, he was just another participant, another line item in a spreadsheet.

"Adrian Vale," she said, her voice flat. "You're late by three minutes."

He blinked, the words taking a second to register. He hadn't checked the time. He had been too focused on keeping his balance. "I'm sorry," he said. His voice sounded thin, reedy in the quiet room. "Traffic."

It was a lie, but it was a small one, and she didn't seem interested in the truth. She gestured to the computer screen. "Sign the log. Room 4 is ready."

He reached for the pen, but his hand shook. The metal barrel slipped from his fingers and clattered onto the desk, rolling toward the edge. He caught it just before it fell, his heart hammering in his chest. He could feel the heat rising in his cheeks, a flush of embarrassment that clashed with the cold air of the room. He set the pen back down, his movements careful, controlled. He signed his name, the letters shaky but legible. He forced his hand to remain still, focusing on the tip of the pen, ignoring the tremors that tried to take over.

The receptionist didn't look at him. She was already turning to the screen, clicking the mouse. "Thank you. Don't touch anything in the exam room until Dr. Thorne is done with her current patient."

He nodded, turning toward the hallway. The floor was polished linoleum, reflecting the overhead fluorescent lights. The lights buzzed, a low, electric drone that seemed to drill into his temples. He walked slowly, his hand trailing along the wall for support. The wall was cool and smooth, a steady presence against his fingertips. He kept his eyes down, watching his feet, the pattern of the tiles blurring and sharpening as the static in his head wavered.

Room 4 was down the hall, past the closed door of Room 3, where he could hear the muffled sounds of another exam. He knocked on the door, the sound sharp and hollow. There was no answer. He knocked again, harder, the wood thudding against his knuckles. The pain was dull, a reminder that his body was still real, still present.

The door opened. Dr. Thorne stood in the doorway, her dark bob neat and severe, her pale eyes scanning him with a clinical assessment that felt like it was stripping away his defenses. She stepped aside, letting him enter.

The room was small, sparsely furnished. The examination table was covered in fresh paper, the plastic crinkling when she adjusted it. The red emergency call button sat on the desk, bright and incongruous against the grey wall. Adrian looked at it, his gaze drawn to the color, the promise of help that he was terrified of needing.

"Sit down," Thorne said, her voice calm, devoid of judgment.

He sat. The plastic chair was hard, the cold of the room seeping into his back. He folded his hands in his lap, interlacing his fingers to keep them still. The tremor was worse now, a fine vibration that ran through his bones. He could feel his heart beating in his throat, a frantic, bird-like flutter that he tried to suppress.

Thorne sat at her desk, picking up a clipboard. She didn't look at him immediately. She wrote something down, the scratch of the pen on paper loud in the quiet room. When she looked up, her eyes were steady, unreadable.

"Your vitals are elevated," she said. "Heart rate is high. Your skin temperature is slightly above baseline."

Adrian said nothing. He kept his hands in his lap, his fingers white-knuckled. He could feel the sweat beading on his forehead, the cold air of the room doing nothing to dry it.

"I'm fine," he said. The words felt hollow, a shield he was holding up with shaking arms.

Thorne studied him for a long moment. She didn't challenge him. She didn't ask him to take his jacket off, or to show his hands. She simply nodded, a small, efficient movement. "I'll start with the standard checks. If you feel any dizziness, or if the room starts to spin, you press the button. Do you understand?"

He nodded. He understood. He also knew that pressing the button would mean an explanation, a record, a potential removal from the trial. It was a choice he didn't want to make.

Thorne stood up, moving to the examination table. She began to lay out the instruments, the metal tools clicking together with a precise, mechanical rhythm. Adrian watched her hands, the steady, confident movements that contrasted sharply with his own trembling. He felt a sudden, sharp pang of envy, a desire for the kind of control she possessed, the ability to function in a world that was trying to break him.

He looked down at his hands. They were still shaking, a subtle, persistent tremor that he couldn't stop. He closed his eyes, focusing on his breathing, trying to slow his heart rate, trying to quiet the noise in his head. But the noise didn't stop. It only grew louder, a rising tide of static that drowned out the sound of the instruments, the hum of the air conditioning, the beating of his own heart.

He opened his eyes. Thorne was waiting for him, her expression neutral, her eyes fixed on his face. He could see the question in her gaze, the one she hadn't asked. He knew the answer. He knew that he was lying. He knew that he was in danger. But he also knew that he couldn't afford to stop. Not yet. Not until he had secured his future, until he had signed the final piece of the puzzle that would keep him alive.

He looked at the red button on the desk. It was so close. He could reach out and press it. He could end this, end the pain, end the fear. But he didn't. He sat there, in the cold room, with his hands shaking and his head spinning, and he waited for her to speak.

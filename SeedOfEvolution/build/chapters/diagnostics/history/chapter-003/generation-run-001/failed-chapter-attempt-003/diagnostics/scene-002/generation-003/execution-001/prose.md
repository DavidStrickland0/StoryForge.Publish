The hum of the air conditioning unit in the corner of Room 4 was a low, persistent drone that Adrian’s sensitive ears interpreted as a physical pressure against his temples. He sat rigid in the hard plastic chair, his fingers intertwined so tightly in his lap that the skin between his knuckles had turned a pale, translucent white. The tremor in his hands had migrated to his elbows, a vibration that traveled up his forearms and settled deep in his chest, rattling against his ribs. He could feel the sweat prickling on the back of his neck, a fine mist that felt cold as it dried in the sterile air.

Dr. Thorne did not look at him while she arranged the instruments on the stainless steel table. She moved with a quiet, economical precision, sliding a blood pressure cuff into alignment with a stethoscope, then setting down a penlight and a small, flat tray of vials. The click of metal against metal was sharp and clear, a sound that seemed to echo in the hollow of his skull. He watched the tip of her pen as she uncapped it, the movement slow and deliberate. He knew he should be afraid. The consent forms had warned him about side effects—nausea, fatigue, a general sense of unease—but they had not warned him about the way his own nervous system had begun to feel like a broken instrument being played by someone who didn’t know the key.

"Your heart rate is elevated," Dr. Thorne said. Her voice was calm, devoid of inflection, a flat line of professional detachment. She turned her back to him for a moment to adjust a monitor on the wall, the screen glowing with a steady, rhythmic pulse that did not match the frantic, erratic thumping Adrian felt in his ears. "And your skin temperature is running slightly above baseline. Are you experiencing any acute pain?"

Adrian opened his mouth. His throat felt like it had been packed with dry sand. He swallowed, the muscle in his neck pulling tight, and forced the words out. "No," he said. The word was smaller than he intended, a whisper that he had to push back up from his lungs. "Just a little tired. The shift at the diner ended late."

It was a lie. It was the largest, most dangerous lie he had ever told. The headache at the base of his skull was not fatigue; it was a jagged, throbbing pressure that made the fluorescent lights above flicker and smear. The tremor in his hands was not exhaustion; it was a neurological stutter, a signal from his brain to his limbs that had gone wrong. He could feel the medication humming in his blood, a foreign, electric current that made the air in the room feel thick and heavy, as if he were breathing underwater.

Dr. Thorne turned back to face him. She did not look at his face, but rather at the monitor, where his biometrics were scrolling in a rapid, unsteady stream. Her pale eyes were distant, focused on the data rather than the man. "I see," she said. The tone was neutral, but there was a slight pause in her speech, a fraction of a second longer than necessary. "The compound is still being mapped. Side effects can vary significantly from participant to participant. Skin sensitivity, for instance, is a new observation we are tracking."

Adrian stared at her. The mention of skin sensitivity made the hairs on his arms stand up. He remembered the way the sleeve of his diner uniform had felt like it was scraping against his bare skin, a raw, burning friction that had made him want to peel the fabric away. He had thought he was overreacting, a simple irritation, but her calm, clinical delivery suggested that his body was doing something that the doctors had not fully anticipated.

"You should report any symptoms that feel severe or persistent," Dr. Thorne continued, her gaze finally drifting up to meet his. Her eyes were clear and unblinking, a mirror that reflected his own desperate, terrified state. "If you experience faintness, or if the tremors become difficult to control, you must press the red emergency button on the desk. Do you understand?"

Adrian looked at the desk. The red button sat there, a small, glossy dome of plastic that seemed to pulse in the dim light. It was a simple, mechanical thing. A switch. A choice. If he pressed it, the door would open. A nurse would come in. They would take his blood, check his pupils, and ask him questions he did not know how to answer. And then he would be removed. His name would be taken off the list. The payment would stop. The tuition would become an impossible mountain, and the rent would collect its due. He would be back to discount noodles and the cold, empty silence of his apartment, watching the days slip away while his body failed him from the inside out.

He looked back at Dr. Thorne. She was waiting. The silence in the room stretched, thin and taut. He could feel the vibration in his hands intensifying, the tremor becoming a shudder that traveled up his arms and into his jaw. He clenched his fists, digging his nails into his palms, using the sharp, stinging pain to anchor himself to the chair, to the room, to reality.

"I understand," he said. His voice was steady. He did not know where the steadiness had come from, whether it was a last flicker of his own will or a cold, hard edge of the medication’s influence. It did not matter. He had made his choice. He had weighed the cost of his body against the cost of his survival, and he had decided that he could afford the former, if only for a little while longer.

Dr. Thorne held his gaze for a long moment. She did not nod, nor did she offer a reassurance. She simply reached into the drawer of her desk, the metal sliding open with a smooth, oiled whisper, and pulled out a single, folded sheet of paper. She placed it on the desk between them, smoothing the corners with two fingers.

"Before we proceed with the standard checks," she said, "we need to finalize the emergency-intervention authorization."

Adrian looked at the paper. It was blank, a pristine white rectangle that seemed to mock the chaos raging in his system. He could feel the weight of it before he even touched it. This was the final piece. The last barrier. He reached out, his hand still trembling slightly, and took the paper. The texture was smooth and cool against his fingertips.

"Read it," Dr. Thorne said. She stepped back, giving him space, her arms crossed over her chest. She stood by the examination table, a tall, silent figure in the sterile light, watching him with the detached patience of a scientist observing a reaction that was taking a little too long to complete.

Adrian unfolded the document. The text was dense, legal, and impenetrable. He scanned the lines, his vision blurring, the words swimming on the page as the cognitive fog rolled in thick and heavy. He could barely retain the meaning of the sentences, the legal jargon dissolving into a stream of noise. But he did not need to understand every word. He only needed to understand the final line.

He found it. The signature line. The date. The box to check.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was still watching him. He looked at the red button on the desk. It was so close. He could reach out and press it. He could end this, end the pain, end the fear. But he didn’t. He sat there, in the cold room, with his hands shaking and his head spinning, and he waited for her to speak.

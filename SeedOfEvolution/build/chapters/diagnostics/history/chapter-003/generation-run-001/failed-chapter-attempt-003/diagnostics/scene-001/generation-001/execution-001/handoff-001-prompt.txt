The clinic lobby smelled of antiseptic and stale coffee, a scent that had become the olfactory anchor for Adrian’s last week of sanity. The automatic doors slid open with a soft hydraulic hiss, admitting him into the sterile, fluorescent-lit waiting area. It was 6:45 PM. The reception desk was empty, the computer monitors glowing with the blue, indifferent light of a system in standby mode.

Adrian leaned against the wall, his shoulder blades pressing into the cold tile. His hands were shaking. It wasn’t the subtle, high-frequency vibration of caffeine jitters; it was a deep, structural tremor that seemed to originate in his bones and radiate outward. He clenched his fists, forcing his fingers into the palms of his jacket, trying to lock the joints into place. The pain was a dull, throbbing ache behind his eyes, a pressure that made the overhead lights look like they were bleeding into the white walls.

He had walked here. He had to remember that he had walked here. The distance from the diner to the clinic was three blocks, a route he had taken a dozen times before. Usually, it took twelve minutes. Today, it had taken twenty-five. He had stopped twice, leaning against brick walls, breathing through his mouth to keep his heart rate from spiking into the red zone. The medication, the experimental compound that was supposed to stimulate his reproductive cells, had turned his body into a hostile environment. Every step was a negotiation with his own physiology.

The receptionist’s chair was unoccupied. Adrian looked at the clock on the wall. 6:52 PM. The trial required him to remain in the clinic for at least one hour after medication administration, but the protocol for the evening sample collection was less rigid. He was supposed to be there, but the staff was likely off the clock. He needed to provide the sample. He needed the data. He needed the money.

He walked to the front desk. The surface was smooth, polished laminate, reflecting the distorted image of his face. He looked pale. Not the healthy, sun-kissed pallor of a student who spent too much time in a library, but the gray, translucent look of someone who hadn’t eaten a proper meal in two days. His skin felt tight, as if it had shrunk around his skull. He touched his cheek, and the sensation was electric, a sharp prick of sensitivity that made him flinch.

"Hello?" he called out. His voice sounded thin, reedy. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Hello?"

The interior door to the staff area opened. The receptionist stepped out. She was a woman in her thirties, with gray eyes that held a professional, detached calm. She wore a white coat over a dark blouse, and her hair was pulled back in a neat bun. She didn’t look surprised to see him. She looked tired.

"Adrian Vale," she said. It wasn’t a question. She checked a tablet in her hand. "You’re early. Or late, depending on how you look at it. The window for the evening sample is 7:00 to 9:00. Dr. Thorne is in Room 4. She’s expecting you."

"I’m here," Adrian said.

"I can see that." She gestured to the hallway behind her. "Room 4 is down the hall, second door on the left. Don’t wait in the lobby. It’s cold."

Adrian nodded. He turned and walked down the hallway. The floor was linoleum, patterned with a faint, blue grid that seemed to move under his feet. His vision blurred for a second, the grid lines merging into a single, wavy line. He closed his eyes, took a deep breath, and opened them again. The world was still there. It was still solid.

He reached Room 4. The door was open. The light from the interior spilled out into the hallway, a warm, yellow glow that contrasted sharply with the blue fluorescence of the corridor. He stepped inside.

The room was small, furnished with a desk, a chair, and a medical bed. Dr. Aris Thorne was sitting at the desk, her back to the door. She was looking at a computer screen, her dark bob framing her face. She didn’t turn around.

"Close the door," she said. Her voice was low, steady.

Adrian closed the door. The latch clicked shut, a small, definitive sound. He turned to face her.

Dr. Thorne looked up. Her pale eyes swept over him, assessing. She didn’t smile. She didn’t frown. She simply looked, with the same clinical detachment she had shown during the intake.

"Sit down," she said, pointing to the chair opposite her desk.

Adrian sat. The chair was hard, plastic, and cold. He placed his hands on his lap, interlacing his fingers to stop the trembling. He watched her. She was typing something into her computer, her fingers moving quickly over the keyboard.

"How are you feeling?" she asked.

The question was simple. It was also a trap. If he said he was fine, he was lying. If he said he was in pain, he was admitting that the medication was having effects that were not on the list. He knew that reporting side effects led to removal from the trial. He knew that removal meant no more money. No more money meant no rent. No rent meant the street.

"I’m fine," Adrian said.

Dr. Thorne stopped typing. She turned her chair to face him fully. Her eyes were steady, unblinking.

"Your heart rate is elevated," she said. "Your skin temperature is lower than baseline. And you’re shaking."

It wasn’t an accusation. It was an observation. She had seen it. She had seen him trembling in the hallway, or perhaps she had checked his vitals remotely. The clinic was a machine, and he was a part of it.

"I’m tired," Adrian said.

"Tiredness is a listed side effect," she said. "Tremors and cognitive impairment are not."

Adrian looked away. He stared at the red emergency call button on the desk. It was bright, vivid, a stark contrast to the muted colors of the room. He had been instructed to press it if he experienced severe or persistent pain or faintness. He hadn’t pressed it. He couldn’t press it. If he pressed it, they would stop the medication. They would run tests. They would find something wrong. And then they would take the money away.

"I need the sample," Adrian said.

Dr. Thorne looked at him for a long moment. The silence stretched, thin and tense. Then she turned back to her computer.

"The sample cup is on the counter," she said. "You have ten minutes. Don’t press the button. I don’t want to file an incident report for a tremor."

Adrian stood up. His legs felt weak, like they were made of wet paper. He walked to the counter, where a sterile plastic cup sat on a tray. He picked it up. It was cold, smooth, and heavy. He looked at it, and his vision blurred again. The cup seemed to vibrate in his hand, a small, rhythmic pulsing that matched the tremor in his own bones.

He walked to the privacy screen. He pulled the curtain closed. The world outside the room disappeared, replaced by the cramped, enclosed space of the examination area. He was alone.

He stood there, holding the cup. His hands were shaking so badly that he nearly dropped it. He caught it, his fingers slipping on the plastic surface. He took a deep breath, and his lungs burned. The air in the room was dry, stale.

He closed his eyes. He thought about the money. He thought about the rent. He thought about the empty apartment, the silence, the hunger. He thought about the library, the books he couldn’t read, the words that swam before his eyes like fish in a dark tank. He thought about the diner, the coffee, the plates, the faces of the customers who looked through him as if he were invisible.

He opened his eyes. He looked at the cup.

He began.

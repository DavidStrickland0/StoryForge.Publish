The walk to the clinic took twelve minutes, a duration that stretched in Adrian’s mind like taffy. His legs felt filled with wet sand, each step a deliberate negotiation against the gravity that seemed to have doubled since he left the diner. He kept his bag strap tight against his shoulder, the leather digging into the skin of his left deltoid. It was a good pain. Sharp, localized, and real. It anchored him to his body when the rest of him felt like it was dissolving into the humid evening air.

He passed the corner of 4th and Main, where the streetlights buzzed with a low, electric hum that he could feel in his teeth. A bus rumbled past, its headlights sweeping across the wet pavement, and Adrian flinched, his hand coming up to shield his eyes. The light didn't hurt, exactly, but it seemed to penetrate the darkness behind his eyelids, leaving a lingering afterimage that took ten seconds to fade. He blinked rapidly, forcing his vision to clear, and watched the bus disappear into the night.

His hands were trembling again. He shoved them deep into his pockets, gripping the seams of his jeans to keep them from shaking visibly. The tremor was a fine, high-frequency vibration that traveled from his fingertips up to his elbows. It made his fingers ache, a dull, throbbing pain that he had to focus on to ignore the nausea rising in his throat.

The clinic building was a low, concrete structure that blended into the urban landscape, its windows glowing with a sterile, white light. It looked like a bank, or a government office, or a place where you went to sign away pieces of your life in exchange for a check that would bounce before the month was out. Adrian stood on the sidewalk, looking at the glass doors, and took a breath that didn't reach his lungs.

He straightened his jacket. He smoothed his hair. He checked his watch, noting the time with a precision that felt performative. 8:42 PM. He was late. The trial required him to be there by 9:00 PM for the sample collection, and he knew that being late was a flag. A yellow flag. If he showed up too early, he was anxious. If he showed up late, he was unreliable. Unreliable participants were dropped. Dropped participants didn't get paid.

He pushed through the glass doors.

The lobby was cool, the air conditioning humming with a quiet, mechanical efficiency that made his sweat-dampened skin prickle. The reception desk was a curved expanse of white laminate, illuminated by a strip of LED light that cast a blue-white glow on the papers stacked neatly in front of the receptionist. She was typing on a keyboard, the click-clack of her fingers a rhythmic, soothing sound that Adrian found he wanted to mimic. He wanted to be part of that rhythm. He wanted to be the keyboard, the desk, the air conditioning. Anything that was fixed and stable.

"Evening," he said. His voice came out smoother than he expected. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Adrian Vale. I'm... I'm here for my appointment."

The receptionist looked up. Her gray eyes were sharp, missing the slight sheen of sweat on his forehead, the way his pupils were dilated even in the bright light. She smiled, a practiced, professional curve of the lips that didn't reach her eyes.

"Welcome back, Adrian. You're a bit past your scheduled window. Is everything okay?"

"Traffic," Adrian said. The word felt absurd. He had walked. He had walked for twelve minutes. But traffic was a reason that made sense. It was a reason that implied he was a normal person, a person with a car, a person who dealt with delays and inconveniences, a person who wasn't currently feeling like his internal organs were vibrating at a frequency that could shatter glass.

"Understood," she said, her fingers moving over the keyboard. "Let me pull up your file. Nurse Miller will be with you in a moment."

Adrian leaned against the wall, his back pressing against the cool concrete. He closed his eyes for a second, focusing on the sensation of the wall behind him. Solid. Unyielding. He opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling, counting the tiles. One, two, three. The numbers were clear. He could hold them. He could hold them for now.

A door opened behind the reception desk, and a woman in a white uniform stepped out. She was tall, with dark hair pulled back in a tight bun. Her eyes were quick, scanning Adrian from head to toe in a single, fluid motion.

"Adrian Vale?" she asked. Her voice was neutral, devoid of warmth or judgment.

"Yes," Adrian said.

"Come with me. Room 4."

He followed her, his steps measured and even. He didn't look at the other patients waiting in the lobby, a handful of people sitting in plastic chairs, their faces lined with the same exhaustion and anxiety that he felt. The tactile sensitivity was worse here, the fabric of his shirt feeling like sandpaper against his neck. He rolled his shoulders, trying to stretch the tension out of his muscles, but the movement only seemed to make the tremor in his hands worse.

Nurse Miller walked ahead of him, her heels clicking on the tile floor. She didn't speak. She didn't need to. The silence was a test. She was watching him. She could see the way he favored his left leg, the way his fingers twitched in his pockets. She could see the fear in his eyes, the way he kept checking his reflection in the dark windows of the passing rooms.

They reached Room 4. The door was open, the light spilling out into the hallway. Adrian stopped at the threshold, his heart hammering against his ribs. He could see Dr. Thorne’s desk, the red emergency button, the sterile white light. He could see the future, or at least the next ten minutes of it.

"Go in," Nurse Miller said. Her tone was soft, almost gentle, which was worse than if she had been harsh.

Adrian stepped into the room. The door clicked shut behind him, the sound final and absolute. He stood in the center of the room, his hands still in his pockets, his breathing shallow and controlled.

Dr. Thorne was sitting at her desk, a tablet in her hands. She looked up, her pale eyes meeting his. She didn't smile. She didn't frown. She simply looked at him, assessing, categorizing, filing him away in the database of her mind.

"Adrian," she said. "You're late."

"I know," Adrian said. "I'm sorry."

She set the tablet down. "Let's begin."

The next twenty minutes were a blur of clinical efficiency. Adrian sat on the examination table, the paper rustling under his weight. He felt the cold of the metal scale beneath his feet, the sharpness of the stethoscope against his chest. He answered the questions about his diet, his sleep, his stress levels, keeping his voice steady, his eyes focused on a point on the wall behind Dr. Thorne’s head.

He lied. He said he was sleeping well. He said he was eating enough. He said he felt fine. The words came out of his mouth like stones, heavy and cold. He felt them in his throat, the taste of deception.

When it was his turn to provide the sample, he walked to the small partitioned area in the corner of the room. The door was thick, soundproof. He closed it, and the world outside ceased to exist. There was only him, the cup, and the trembling in his hands.

"Thank you, Adrian," she said. "You can go."

The walk to the clinic took twenty minutes. Adrian counted the steps as a way to keep his hands from shaking. One, two, three. The pavement was cool under his worn sneakers. The air smelled of exhaust and rain-washed asphalt. His stomach churned, a hollow, acidic ache that no amount of willpower could ignore. He had eaten a single granola bar at the diner, the kind they kept behind the counter for staff, but it had felt like chewing on cardboard.

He reached the clinic building. The glass doors slid open with a soft pneumatic hiss, releasing a wave of conditioned air that felt too cold against his sweat-dampened shirt. The interior was sterile, bright, and quiet. The fluorescent lights hummed at a frequency that seemed to drill into his temples.

The receptionist sat behind the high desk, her gray eyes fixed on a monitor. She looked up as he approached, her expression neutral, professional. She did not smile. She had never smiled, not really. Not in the way people smiled when they were happy or pleased. Her smile was a mechanism, a default setting for customer service.

"Adrian Vale," she said, her voice flat and efficient.

He nodded, his throat tight. "Vale."

She turned to her screen, fingers moving rapidly over the keyboard. "Checking in for the 6:30 PM session. You’re running late."

The words were not a judgment. They were a fact, stated with the same tone she would use to report the weather or the time. Adrian felt a spike of irritation, a hot, prickly sensation that started in his chest and radiated out to his fingertips. He wanted to snap back, to explain that he had been working, that the medication made him feel like he was wading through mud, that the world was slightly out of focus and the lights were screaming at him. But he knew better. He knew that explaining was useless. To her, he was a row of data in a database. A participant ID. A compliance metric.

"I had a shift," he said, his voice sounding rougher than he intended.

"Understood," she said. She didn’t look at him. She was already typing, already processing his arrival into the system. "Dr. Thorne is reviewing your logs. Please take a seat."

She pointed to a row of plastic chairs arranged against the far wall. The chairs were hard, molded, and uncomfortable. Adrian sat in the middle one, the one that seemed least likely to be broken. He placed his bag on the floor beside him and rested his forearms on his knees.

He waited.

The waiting room was empty. No other patients. No families. No children crying or elderly people shuffling with canes. Just him, the hum of the lights, and the distant, muffled sound of the city outside. The silence was heavy, pressing against his eardrums.

He looked at the clock on the wall. 6:42 PM.

He closed his eyes. The darkness behind his lids was not restful. It was textured, flickering with static. He could feel his heart beating, a sluggish, heavy thud that seemed out of sync with the rest of his body. His hands were trembling again, a fine, high-frequency vibration that he tried to suppress by clenching his fists.

He thought about the diner. The grease on the floor. The smell of burnt coffee. The customer who had asked him if he was okay. He had smiled, forced the smile, and said, "Just tired." It had been a lie, but a small one. A harmless one.

Here, in the clinic, the lies were larger. More dangerous. He had signed a document that waived his right to privacy, his right to sue, his right to know the full extent of what they were doing to him. He had signed it because he needed the money. He had signed it because he was desperate. And desperation made a person willing to accept terms that a healthy, wealthy person would never consider.

He opened his eyes. The receptionist was still typing. Her cursor blinked on the screen, a steady, rhythmic pulse. She was checking his compliance. Checking that he had taken the medication. Checking that he had provided the sample. Checking that he had not skipped a day. Checking that he was still useful to them.

Adrian felt a cold knot form in his stomach. He looked at the red emergency button on the desk in the corner of the room. It was small, unassuming, red plastic against the gray laminate. He had been told to press it if he experienced severe pain, faintness, or any symptom that felt life-threatening.

He had not pressed it. He did not want to press it. If he pressed it, they would stop. They would pull him out of the trial. They would deny him the money. He would fail.

He looked at the button, then away. His hands were still shaking. He let them shake. He let the tremor move through his arms, his shoulders, his neck. He let it exist. He did not fight it. He did not hide it. He simply let it be part of him, a physical manifestation of the lie he was living.

The door to Room 4 opened.

Dr. Thorne stepped out. She was tall, lean, her dark bob cut sharp and precise. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they moved over Adrian with a quick, assessing sweep. She did not smile. She did not frown. She simply looked at him, taking in his posture, his skin, the slight pallor of his face.

"Adrian," she said. Her voice was calm, clinical. "Come in."

Adrian stood up. His legs felt weak, unsteady. He gripped the edge of the chair to steady himself, then let go. He walked toward the door, his steps measured, deliberate. He did not look at the receptionist. He did not look at the red button. He looked at the door, at the white frame, at the handle.

He pushed the door open.

The room was dark, lit only by the glow of the monitors on Dr. Thorne’s desk. The air was cool, scented with antiseptic and something else, something faintly metallic. Adrian stepped inside, and the door clicked shut behind him.

He stood in the doorway, breathing through his nose, waiting for her to speak.

The fluorescent lights in the intake office hummed with a low, electrical drone that Adrian felt in his teeth. He had walked three blocks from the diner, his legs feeling like they were filled with wet sand, and now he was seated in a plastic chair that offered no support for his lower back. The chair was molded, blue, and cold against his jeans.

He kept his hands resting on his knees. His fingers were trembling, a fine, persistent vibration that he could not stop. He pressed his palms flat against his thighs, using the friction and the pressure to ground himself, to keep his hands from shaking visibly. It was a small trick, one he had learned during his years of waiting tables, but it did not silence the ringing in his ears.

Across the room, the receptionist was typing on a keyboard. The sound was rhythmic, mechanical, and entirely indifferent to the fact that Adrian felt like he was dissolving. She did not look up. Her gray eyes were fixed on the monitor, her cursor blinking in a steady, patient rhythm.

"Vale, Adrian," she said, her voice flat and procedural. She slid a clipboard across the polished surface of the desk. "Check in."

Adrian stood up. The motion was slower than he intended. The room tilted slightly, a subtle shift in perspective that his brain tried to correct but could not fully stabilize. He reached for the clipboard. His grip was secure, though his skin felt tight, as if it had shrunk around his bones.

He signed the sheet. The pen felt heavy in his hand, the ink flowing too quickly, leaving a dark, wet line on the paper. He signed his name, the letters looking alien and jagged to his own eyes. He had written this name ten thousand times, but today it looked like a scribble made by a stranger.

He handed the clipboard back. The receptionist took it, her fingers brushing the paper with a casual efficiency that made Adrian’s stomach turn. She typed the time, 8:02 AM, into the system.

"Dr. Thorne will be with you in a moment," she said. She looked up, her gaze sweeping over him for a fraction of a second. It was a quick, clinical assessment, the kind of look a butcher might give a cut of meat to check for spoilage. Then she looked back at her screen. "Please wait."

Adrian sat back down. The plastic chair creaked under his weight.

He closed his eyes for a second, just to let the darkness wash over his vision. The headache was a dull throb behind his left eye, a steady pulse that matched his heart rate. He could feel the blood moving through his veins, a hot, rushing stream that seemed to bypass his brain and flow directly into his extremities. His hands were still shaking.

He opened his eyes. The waiting area was small, a rectangular space with beige walls and a single window that looked out onto a concrete alleyway. There was a magazine rack in the corner, filled with glossy papers that advertised health spas, local restaurants, and insurance plans. Adrian looked at the cover of a magazine titled *Wellness Weekly*. The image showed a woman stretching by a pool, her skin glowing with a healthy, radiant sheen.

Adrian felt a sudden, sharp spike of irritation. He looked away, staring at the beige wall instead. The paint was peeling slightly in the corner, a small flake of white curling away from the surface. He focused on that flake, on the way the light caught its edge. It was a tiny, insignificant detail, but it was real. It was there.

He remembered the diner. The smell of grease and burnt coffee. The way the customer had looked at him, with that curious, prying interest that made Adrian want to disappear. *Rough day?* the customer had asked. Adrian had smiled, a tight, practiced smile that did not reach his eyes. *Just tired,* he had said. *Long shift.*

It was the truth, and it was a lie. He was tired, yes. But he was also something else. He was a container for a chemical reaction that he did not understand and could not control. The medication was in his blood, saturating his cells, doing whatever it was designed to do. And it was changing him.

He could feel it in the way his skin felt, raw and sensitive to the air conditioning blowing from the vent above. He could feel it in the way his thoughts moved, sluggish and thick, like syrup in a cold glass. He could feel it in the way his body wanted to move, to shake, to release the tension that had built up in his muscles.

The door to the hallway opened.

Dr. Thorne walked in.

She was tall, her dark bob cut sharp and precise. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they moved over Adrian with a quick, assessing glance. She did not smile. She did not offer a greeting.

"Mr. Vale," she said. Her voice was clear, devoid of any inflection. "Come with me."

She turned and walked toward the door at the end of the office. Adrian stood up, his legs feeling unsteady. He followed her, keeping a respectful distance. The hallway was carpeted in a dark green color, the fibers worn smooth in the center where people walked. The walls were lined with framed posters, medical diagrams of the human body, the circulatory system, the nervous system.

Adrian looked at the posters as he walked. The lines were clean, precise, scientific. They showed the body as a machine, a collection of parts that could be measured, analyzed, and understood.

He looked down at his own hands. They were still trembling.

He did not stop walking. He did not look up. He followed Dr. Thorne down the hallway, his footsteps silent on the carpet, his mind a blur of static and noise. He was waiting for her to speak, to tell him what to do, to tell him what was wrong with him.

But she did not speak. She simply walked, her heels clicking on the floor, a steady, rhythmic sound that echoed in the empty hallway.

They reached the door to Room 4. It was a standard office door, white and unmarked. Dr. Thorne reached out and opened it with her keycard.

The room was small, much smaller than the intake office. It had a desk, a chair, and a single window that looked out onto the city. The desk was cluttered with papers, a computer monitor, and a red emergency button that sat on a small pedestal in the corner.

Adrian had seen that button before. He had seen it when he signed the consent form. He had read the instructions, the small, printed text that explained what the button was for. *Press in case of severe pain, faintness, or emergency.*

He had not thought much of it then. It had seemed like a standard piece of medical equipment, a safety feature, a precaution.

Now, as he looked at it, it seemed different. It seemed like a threat.

Dr. Thorne walked to her desk and sat down. She placed her hands on the keyboard and began to type. The sound of the keys was loud in the quiet room.

"Sit down," she said.

Adrian sat in the chair opposite her. The chair was softer than the plastic chair in the waiting area, but it did not feel any more comfortable. He sat with his back straight, his hands resting on his lap. He waited.

Dr. Thorne continued to type. Her fingers moved quickly, efficiently, over the keys. She did not look at him. She did not speak.

The silence stretched out, long and heavy. Adrian could hear the hum of the computer, the faint buzz of the lights, the sound of his own breathing. He could hear the blood rushing in his ears, a loud, roaring sound that filled the room.

He waited for her to speak. He waited for her to ask him how he was feeling. He waited for her to ask him about his symptoms, his side effects, his pain.

But she did not ask. She simply continued to type.

Adrian stared at the red button in the corner. It was small, bright, and vivid against the beige wall. It looked like a target.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was still typing. Her face was expressionless, her eyes fixed on the screen.

He looked back at the button.

He felt a surge of fear, cold and sharp, in his stomach. It was a physical sensation, a tightening of his chest, a quickening of his pulse. He felt like he was standing on the edge of a cliff, and all he had to do was take one step to fall.

He did not take the step.

He sat in the chair, his hands trembling, his eyes fixed on the red button. He waited.

Dr. Thorne stopped typing. She leaned back in her chair, her hands resting on the armrests. She looked at Adrian.

"Mr. Vale," she said.

Her voice was calm. It was the same voice she had used when she had administered the medication, the same voice she had used when she had collected the sample. It was a voice that did not care, a voice that did not feel.

"Your compliance logs are complete," she said.

Adrian did not speak. He waited for her to say more.

"Are you in pain?" she asked.

The question was simple. It was direct. It was the first time she had asked him anything since he had walked into the room.

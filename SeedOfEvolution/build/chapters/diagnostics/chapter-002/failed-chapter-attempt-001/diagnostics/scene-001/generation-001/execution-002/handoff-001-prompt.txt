David swiped his thumb across the tablet screen, the glass making a soft, frictionless sound. The document scrolled upward, revealing dense blocks of text in a font size that seemed designed to discourage reading rather than encourage it. Adrian leaned forward, his knuckles white where they gripped the edge of the reception counter. The wood was cool under his fingers, a grounding sensation against the tremor that had settled into his hands.

"Here," David said, his voice mild and practiced. He tapped a line in the middle of the page. "Section four. Participant Obligations."

Adrian’s eyes tracked the words, though his brain moved sluggishly through the legalese. *Daily administration of the investigational compound. Strict adherence to dosing schedule. Mandatory daily biological sample collection for analysis. Prohibition on external sexual activity unless pre-approved by the Medical Monitor.*

The last line stopped him. He blinked, reading it again. The words were clear, printed in the same dry, clinical font as the rest. *Prohibition on external sexual activity.*

David watched him, his expression neutral. He didn’t offer a smile, didn’t offer a shrug. He just waited, his posture relaxed, one hand resting on the back of his chair.

"Does that mean what it says?" Adrian asked. His voice sounded thin in the quiet lobby, stripped of the usual resonance. He cleared his throat, trying to find the bottom of it.

"It means what it says," David replied. "The compound is unstable in the presence of certain enzymatic interactions. To ensure the data integrity of the trial, we require that participants do not engage in sexual activity that could introduce external biological variables. It’s a standard protocol for trials involving reproductive tissue."

"Standard," Adrian repeated. The word felt heavy in his mouth. He thought of the empty apartment, the silence that filled it when he wasn’t working, the way he had spent the last three weeks sleeping on a mattress that didn’t quite fit the frame. He thought of the fact that he hadn’t had a relationship in four years, not because he was shy, but because he had been too busy surviving to remember what it felt like to want something from another person.

"And if I break it?" Adrian asked.

David’s eyes flicked to the line again. "Then you are disqualified from the trial. Any accrued compensation is forfeited, and the agreement states that the Institute is not liable for any financial losses resulting from your removal from the program."

The implication hung in the air, cold and sharp. Forfeited compensation. For a man with no savings, no family, and a lease that required a check to be written by Friday, that wasn’t a penalty. It was an execution.

Adrian looked down at his hands. The skin was translucent, the blue veins beneath visible like cracks in dry earth. He flexed his fingers, watching the tendons move. He was nineteen years old. He should have been worried about grades, or finding a job that didn’t involve cleaning up other people’s messes, or figuring out how to eat three meals a day without counting the calories. Instead, he was standing in a lobby that smelled of floor wax and antiseptic, negotiating the terms of his own bodily autonomy for the price of rent.

"What happens during the sample collection?" Adrian asked. He kept his voice flat, keeping the panic out of it. Panic was expensive. Panic made you make mistakes.

David scrolled down, his thumb moving with practiced ease. "You come to the clinic. You take the medication. Then you proceed to the collection room. It’s a private room. You provide the sample in a designated container. It’s a straightforward process."

"Private," Adrian echoed. "Just me and the cup?"

"Exactly."

"And I do this... every day?"

"Every day, unless you are instructed otherwise by the Medical Monitor. The schedule is flexible. You can choose your time slot within the operating hours. Most participants find it easiest to come in early, so it’s out of the way for the rest of the day."

Adrian looked at the tablet. The screen was bright, the text crisp. The numbers at the bottom of the page were large, bold, and undeniable. *$1,500 per week. Medical insurance included. Tuition assistance available upon request.*

It was enough. It was more than enough. It was exactly enough to keep the lights on, the food in the fridge, and the eviction notice out of his mailbox.

He looked at David. The coordinator was watching him, his face a mask of professional patience. There was no judgment in his eyes. There was no pity. He saw a participant, a data point, a problem to be solved. And Adrian, for the first time in weeks, felt a strange sense of relief. It was easier to be a data point than a person. A person had to worry about dignity, about shame, about the fact that he was selling his body to a corporation because he was too poor to afford a better life. A data point just had to follow the instructions.

"I need to read this," Adrian said.

"Take your time," David said. He slid the tablet across the counter. The glass surface was cool under Adrian’s palm.

Adrian picked it up. His hand shook, just slightly, but he steadied it. He scrolled back to the top of the document. *Participant Agreement. Experimental Medicine Program. Apex Research Institute.*

He read the first paragraph. *By signing this agreement, the Participant acknowledges that they are voluntarily enrolling in a clinical trial...*

Voluntarily. The word was a joke. He was not choosing this. He was being forced into it by the simple, brutal arithmetic of his own life. He had no choice. He had never had a choice. He just had a deadline.

The letters appeared on the screen, sharp and black. He looked at them, wondering if they looked like the name of a man who had just sold his future for a paycheck.

"Five business days," Adrian repeated. That was too long. But he didn’t say it. He put the stylus back on the counter and capped it.

Adrian looked at the tablet, then at David, then at the glass doors behind him. The streetlights outside were fully on now, casting long, orange shadows on the pavement.

He turned away and began to walk. His legs felt heavy, the fatigue deep in his bones. But his mind was clear. He had a plan. He had a deadline. And for the first time in weeks, he had a reason to wake up tomorrow.

He walked faster, the cold air filling his lungs, the city noise rising around him. He didn’t look back. He didn’t need to. He knew where he was going.

David set the tablet on the counter, the screen glowing with a dense wall of text. He didn’t look up immediately; instead, he tapped the screen twice, adjusting the font size before turning it toward Adrian. The movement was smooth, practiced, the kind of efficiency that made Adrian’s stomach turn over with a hollow ache.

“Here’s the participant agreement,” David said. His voice was level, devoid of the breathless excitement he’d shown when discussing the compensation figures an hour ago. “And here’s the medical consent form. I recommend you read both in full. I can stay here, or you can take them back to your dorm if you prefer the privacy.”

Adrian looked at the glass surface of the tablet, then at his own reflection. He looked thinner than he remembered, the shadows under his eyes more pronounced than the fluorescent lighting of the lobby had suggested. His hand trembled slightly as he reached out to touch the edge of the device. He didn’t take it. Not yet.

“I’ll read here,” he said. The words felt heavy in his mouth.

David nodded, stepping back to give him space but remaining within the circle of the desk’s ambient light. He leaned against the receptionist’s partition, arms crossed, watching Adrian with the patient attention of a man who had seen this exact hesitation a dozen times before.

Adrian scrolled to the bottom of the first page. The legal language was dense, filled with references to indemnification and waiver of liability. He skimmed past the standard boilerplate, searching for the parts that mattered. The compensation structure was clear: a weekly stipend deposited directly into a bank account, plus a monthly bonus for perfect attendance. The numbers were real. They were enough to cover the rent, the tuition balance, and enough food to keep his hands from shaking so badly he couldn’t hold a pen.

He scrolled further.

Section 4.2: Data Collection and Biological Monitoring.

Adrian stopped. He read the first line again. *Participants are required to submit a daily biological specimen for analysis to track the efficacy of the compound and monitor for adverse effects.*

The words were sterile. The words were clinical. They didn’t carry the weight of the reality they described.

He scrolled to the appendix, where the procedural guidelines were outlined in plain language. There it was. *Participants must masturbate into a provided specimen cup to collect the sample. This process must be completed on-site at the Apex Research Institute facility during the designated collection window.*

The air in the lobby seemed to thicken. Adrian stared at the sentence, the cursor blinking at the end of the line like a slow, indifferent heartbeat. The humiliation of it wasn’t the sex of the act; it was the procedure. The clinical detachment. The way it reduced a private, messy, human function into a data point, a checkbox in a spreadsheet. It was a transaction he didn’t know he could make, where the currency was his dignity and the price was his survival.

His throat tightened. He could feel the heat rising in his cheeks, a flush that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room. He looked up at David, expecting to see judgment, or perhaps pity.

David’s expression was neutral. He wasn’t looking at Adrian with disgust. He wasn’t looking at him with curiosity. He was looking at him with the same professional detachment he had applied to every other applicant who had ever walked into this lobby.

“Is there a problem with the procedure?” David asked. His tone was gentle, inquiring, but it didn’t soften the implication.

Adrian shook his head. He couldn’t find his voice. He looked back down at the screen, tracing the words with his eyes until they blurred. *The screen displayed the word Masturbate. The screen displayed the word Specimen. The screen displayed the word Cup. On-site.*

“I just want to make sure I understand the schedule,” Adrian managed. His voice sounded thin to his own ears, stripped of its usual quiet confidence. “How long does the collection window last? Do I have to wait in a room?”

“The window is one hour,” David said. “You will be assigned a private collection room. It’s clean, private, and equipped with everything you need. No one will be in the room with you. The samples are analyzed in the lab immediately after collection to ensure integrity.”

Adrian nodded, though the motion felt mechanical. One hour. A private room. A sterile environment where he would have to perform this act, alone, under the implicit authority of the institution that paid him.

“And if I’m not… if I can’t do it that day?” Adrian asked. The question was harder to ask than he expected. It felt like admitting a failure before it had even happened.

“Missing a sample constitutes a breach of the agreement,” David said. He paused, letting the statement hang in the air. “We understand that biological functions aren’t always on demand. If you have a medical issue preventing collection, you should contact the medical team immediately. But for the purposes of the trial, consistency is key. The data needs to be regular.”

Adrian felt a cold knot form in his stomach. He was being asked to maintain consistency. He was being asked to maintain consistency and regularity. The language of a machine. He was being asked to become a machine, a reliable source of data points.

He looked at the bottom of the agreement. His signature line was already formatted, waiting. The ink was imaginary, but the commitment was real. He thought of his landlord, who had given him until Friday. He thought of the empty fridge in his apartment, the dust motes dancing in the sunlight from the single window. He thought of the feeling of hunger, that constant, grinding ache that had become his companion over the last three weeks.

He could leave. He could walk out of this lobby, back into the street, back to the uncertainty that threatened to swallow him whole. He could preserve his pride. He could keep his body his own.

But he would be homeless. He would be expelled. He would be nothing.

Adrian took a breath. It was a shallow, rattling breath, barely enough to fill his lungs. He looked at David, who was still watching him with that calm, expectant stillness.

“I need to see the withdrawal clause,” Adrian said.

David didn’t hesitate. He swiped his finger across the screen, navigating back to the main body of the agreement. He tapped a section near the end.

*“Participant may withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Upon withdrawal, all payments cease immediately. Any previously collected data remains the property of Apex Research Institute.”*

Adrian read it twice. *Without penalty.* It was a lifeline, a thin rope thrown into a dark ocean. He could cut it at any time. But if he did, the ocean would take him.

He looked at the numbers one last time. The compensation. The medical care. The freedom from the immediate, crushing weight of his poverty.

It was a terrible deal. It was degrading. It was a violation of every instinct he had about autonomy and dignity.

But it was the only door that opened.

Adrian reached into his pocket and pulled out a pen. It was a cheap, ballpoint thing, the kind he’d bought from a convenience store and kept in his jacket pocket for months. He uncapped it, the plastic clicking in the quiet lobby.

His hand was still trembling. He could feel the sweat on his palms, the stickiness of it against the glass of the tablet. He pressed the tip of the pen to the screen, hovering over the line where his signature was expected.

He didn’t sign it. Not yet.

He needed to do one more thing. He needed to ask the question that had been burning in his chest, the one that tied the knot of anxiety and necessity together.

“What happens if I fail a side-effect check?” Adrian asked. He didn’t look up. His eyes were fixed on the digital line, the cursor blinking its endless, patient blink. “If the compound… if it does something to me that the tests flag?”

David’s voice was soft. “We monitor for adverse effects daily. If a side effect is detected, the medical team will assess it. If it’s severe, we will discontinue the medication and provide treatment. If it’s minor, we may adjust the dosage. But the priority is your safety, Adrian. We don’t want to hurt you.”

*Safety.* Adrian almost laughed at the word. Safety was a luxury. Safety was having a roof that didn’t leak, having food that didn’t taste like cardboard, having a body that didn’t feel like it was slowly falling apart from lack of sleep and nutrition.

He looked at the pen in his hand. It was a simple tool, designed for a simple purpose. Signing a name. Marking a contract. Accepting a fate.

He brought the pen down.

The scratch of the plastic tip against the glass was loud in the quiet room. He wrote his name, the letters slightly jagged, the ink dark and permanent.

*Adrian Vale.*

He capped the pen and set it on the counter. He looked up at David, his face a mask of exhausted resignation.

“I accept,” he said.

David’s face broke into a small, professional smile. It was a genuine expression, or at least it appeared to be. He picked up the tablet and tapped the screen, finalizing the process.

“Welcome to the program, Adrian,” David said. “I’ll have Sarah set up your profile and schedule your first collection appointment. You’ll receive a confirmation email with the details. The first payment will hit your account within twenty-four hours.”

Adrian nodded. He felt a strange, hollow lightness in his chest, as if a weight he had been carrying for weeks had finally been set down, only to be replaced by a heavier one.

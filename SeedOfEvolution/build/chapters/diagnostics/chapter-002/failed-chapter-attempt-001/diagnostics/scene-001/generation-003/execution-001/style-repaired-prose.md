David tapped the screen, bringing the text into sharp focus. The font was clean, sans-serif, efficient. It lacked the warmth of a handwritten note or the complexity of a legal contract, which was a relief. This was a form. A very detailed, very long form.

"Let’s start with the logistics," David said. He didn't look up from the tablet, but his tone shifted. The eager recruiter vibe softened into something more procedural, more tired. He was used to this. He had been waiting for someone to say *yes* to the fertility study for so long that the relief had likely curdled into routine. "The compensation structure is weekly. You get paid every Friday for the previous week’s participation. The base rate is two hundred and fifty dollars per week. There’s a completion bonus of fifty dollars for each full week where you have no missed appointments and no protocol deviations."

Adrian did the math in his head. Two hundred and fifty, plus the bonus, was three hundred dollars a week. Fourteen hundred dollars a month. He had roughly four thousand dollars in immediate debt. He would need to do this for three months, minimum, to clear the backlog and have enough breathing room to buy actual food instead of expired rice.

"Missed appointments," Adrian said.

"Count them as a zero for the bonus, but the base pay remains if you make it up within forty-eight hours," David clarified. "Side effects? Report them. Minor ones don't impact pay. Major ones trigger a medical review. If you’re hospitalized, the base pay continues, obviously. We don't want to discourage people from coming in when something feels wrong."

Adrian nodded. He wrote down the numbers on the back of a receipt he had salvaged from his pocket earlier. It was a receipt from a laundromat he hadn't been able to afford to use. The ink was smudged, the numbers slightly blurred, but they were there.

"Now, the medical component," David said. He scrolled down. The document was long. Adrian could see the scroll bar at the bottom was barely moved. "You’ll be taking a daily oral medication. It’s a synthetic compound designed to stimulate spermatogenesis. We call it VRX-904. It’s in Phase II trials for male factor infertility. Your role is to provide the data points that tell us if it’s safe and effective for men who aren't already infertile."

Adrian felt a strange, hollow sensation in his stomach. Hunger, mostly. But also a prickly anticipation that made the back of his neck itch. "And the sample collection?"

"Daily," David said. "Every day. Monday through Friday, minimum. Weekends are optional but encouraged for data consistency. You come in, you take the pill, and you provide a specimen."

"Into a cup?" Adrian asked. He hated how his voice sounded. His voice sounded thin. His voice sounded reedy. Like a boy who hadn't learned how to use his own lungs properly.

"A sterile container," David corrected gently. "You have a private room. You have time. You can lock the door from the inside. There are no cameras in the sample collection room. That’s a standard privacy protocol."

Adrian let out a breath he didn’t realize he’d been holding. He had been picturing a glass room, perhaps. Or a nurse standing in the corner with a clipboard. The idea of being watched had been the part of the mental checklist that made his skin crawl. Privacy was the one thing that made the indignity bearable. If he could do it behind a locked door, it was just a chore. A gross, biological chore. Like picking his teeth. Like getting a haircut.

"What if I… what if I can’t?" Adrian asked. The question hung in the air, pathetic and direct. "Physically?"

David looked up then. His eyes were the color of wet slate. He studied Adrian for a moment, taking in the thinning of the hair, the way the collar of his shirt hung loose around his neck, the tremor in the hand that held the receipt.

"The medication is intended to increase viability and count," David said. "It’s not a performance enhancer. But generally, the side effects include mild fatigue, occasional headaches, and… increased libido. It’s a common complaint. Or a common benefit, depending on your perspective."

Adrian blinked. "Libido?"

"The compound works by stimulating the reproductive system. It increases blood flow, hormonal activity, that sort of thing. Some participants find it makes the… process… easier than they expect. Others find it overwhelming. It varies."

Adrian tried to smile. It felt like a grimace. "So it’s a double-edged sword."

"Metaphorically speaking, yes," David said. He went back to the tablet. "Now, the withdrawal clause."

He highlighted a section in bold. Adrian leaned in, his shoulder aching from standing too long.

"Participants may withdraw from the trial at any time, for any reason, without penalty. There is no lock-in period. Once you withdraw, you simply stop taking the medication and stop attending the clinic. You do not owe us any further data. We do not ask why. We do not call. We close your file."

Adrian stared at the bold text. *Without penalty.* It was a strange thing for a corporation to offer. It felt like a trap with no teeth. Or perhaps it was the opposite. A genuine freedom, offered with a smile.

"Is it really that simple?" Adrian asked.

"Legally and ethically, yes," David said. "It’s a requirement for our approval. If you feel unsafe, uncomfortable, or simply decide it’s not for you, you walk out. We don’t chase people. We don’t want people who are forced. We want people who are willing. And we want to make sure they know they can leave."

Adrian looked at the screen. The words were simple. The offer was absurd. The need was absolute.

He thought about the laundromat receipt in his hand. He thought about the stack of unpaid bills in his mailbox. He thought about the way his legs shook when he stood up too fast, the way the world blurred at the edges when he hadn’t eaten in twenty-four hours. He thought about the look on his mother’s face when she had handed him the key to his college apartment, trusting him to be someone who could handle it. He had failed that trust. He had failed himself.

This was not dignity. Dignity was having a job that paid fairly, having a body that worked when you asked it to, having a future that didn't require you to sell a part of your biology to survive.

But this was survival.

Adrian pulled a pen from his pocket. It was an old ballpoint, the cap loose, the ink dry. He clicked it. It squeaked.

"I need to see the liability waiver," he said. His voice was steady now. The trembling had stopped, replaced by a cold, hard focus. "And the medical consent forms. I need to know exactly what I’m consenting to."

David nodded, his expression softening with a professional respect that Adrian recognized. It was the respect accorded to a customer who was about to buy something expensive and dangerous.

"Of course," David said. He scrolled down, the screen glowing with the next page of text. "Take your time. I’ll be right here."

Adrian began to read. The words blurred, then sharpened. He read the first paragraph, the second, the third. He read until the meaning sank into his bones, until he knew exactly what he was doing.

He was selling his time. He was selling his body’s fluids. He was selling his privacy.

In exchange, he was buying his life.

He reached for the pen again. His hand did not shake. He signed his name.

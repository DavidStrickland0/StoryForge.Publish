David cleared the previous document from the tablet’s screen, a gesture that wiped away the general overview and replaced it with a dense block of text. The font was small, crisp, and unyielding. Adrian leaned forward, his stomach giving a hollow, cramping twist that had nothing to do with the sterile air conditioning blowing from the vents above. He tried to ignore the sensation, focusing instead on the lines of legal prose scrolling slowly down the glass.

"Section Four," David said, his voice maintaining that same smooth, practiced cadence. He tapped the screen with a stylus, highlighting a paragraph in pale blue. "This outlines the daily protocol. You’ll be administering the compound, K-92, once every twenty-four hours. The dosage is fixed. You won’t need to measure anything; the pill is pre-calibrated."

Adrian nodded, though the movement felt stiff in his neck. "And the sample?"

"Also every twenty-four hours," David replied. He didn’t look up, keeping his eyes on the screen as if reading a script. "The clinic operates on a rolling schedule. You can pick a window that works for your classes. Morning, early afternoon, or evening. But the interval must be consistent. Deviation of more than two hours requires a prior notification to your study coordinator."

Adrian scanned the highlighted text. *Consistent interval.* *Prior notification.* *Protocol adherence.* The words were clinical, stripped of any human context. He looked for a clause about flexibility, for an exception for exams or emergencies, but the language was tight. It accounted for everything except the fact that he was nineteen and terrified.

"What if I miss a day?" Adrian asked.

David finally looked up. His expression didn’t change. It remained pleasant, professional, and utterly devoid of judgment. "If you miss a day, the data point is lost. If you miss three days in a row, you are suspended from the study. If you miss a week, your contract is terminated, and you forfeit any accrued compensation pending final audit."

The air in the lobby seemed to thin. Adrian’s throat tightened. He thought of the email from the housing office, the red bold text of the payment deadline. He thought of the granola bar he’d been saving for three days, the way his hands trembled when he tried to type his password on his cracked phone. Forfeiting the compensation meant losing the rent. Losing the rent meant losing the apartment. Losing the apartment meant losing the only place that felt like his.

"Okay," Adrian said. The word came out flat, stripped of breath.

David swiped to the next page. "Section Seven. Medical Monitoring."

This was the part Adrian had braced for, the part that lived in the back of his mind as a cold, hard stone. He watched the text scroll. *Blood draws.* *Urine analysis.* *Genetic sampling.* *Quarterly cardiac evaluations.*

"You’ll be in and out of the clinic for about twenty minutes each day," David explained. "The blood draw is standard. The urine test is for hydration levels and metabolic markers. The genetic sampling is a one-time procedure, done during intake. It’s a cheek swab and a blood sample. Nothing invasive."

Adrian frowned, his eyes snagging on a line that didn’t match the verbal description. He squinted, the blue light from the tablet burning into his retinas. "David, look at paragraph three. It says 'manual collection of seminal fluid for microscopic analysis.' It says 'on-site.'"

David’s pen hovered over the screen. He glanced at the line, then back at Adrian. "Yes. That is the core requirement of the fertility trial. We need the specimens in a controlled environment to ensure chain-of-custody and immediate viability testing. Home collection is unreliable for the specific markers we’re tracking."

Adrian felt a flush of heat crawl up his neck, burning into his cheeks. He could feel the eyes of the receptionist behind the desk, could feel the weight of the empty lobby pressing in on him. The idea of doing that—standing in a white-tiled room, closing the door, performing that act while a camera recorded the timestamp—felt like a violation of a different order. It wasn’t just physical; it was administrative. It was reducing his body to a data source, a faucet for a specific liquid that needed to be measured, weighed, and cataloged.

"Is... is it private?" Adrian asked, his voice sounding strange to his own ears, thin and reedy.

"Absolutely," David said. "The rooms are soundproofed. The cameras only record the timestamp and the completion of the task. They do not record the act itself. The technicians are outside the door. You lock it from the inside."

Adrian looked down at his hands. They were pale, the blue veins prominent beneath the skin. He remembered the warehouse job, the way his shoulders had screamed after a single shift of lifting crates. He remembered the bar, the way the manager had looked at him with pity mixed with annoyance when he’d spilled the tray of shots. This was different. This was cleaner. This was paid. But it was also a cage, and the key was made of glass and legal jargon.

"What happens if I get sick?" Adrian asked. It was a stupid question, he knew, but he needed to hear the answer.

"Then you follow the medical protocol," David said. "You report the symptoms. If the symptoms are severe, you are removed from the study until cleared by the medical board. You are compensated for the days you are absent due to medical necessity, up to a limit of ten days per quarter."

Ten days. That was a lot of money. That was a lot of survival.

Adrian scrolled back to the top of the document. There was a signature block. There was a date field. There was a line for "Participant Name."

"Can I take this home?" Adrian asked.

David shook his head slowly. "The agreement needs to be signed in person. We need to witness the signature to ensure there’s no duress. Once you sign, we run your basic biometrics to confirm identity, and then we can process your first payment. It usually hits your bank account within the hour."

The first payment. The number David had mentioned earlier. Enough to cover the rent. Enough to buy food. Enough to keep the lights on.

Adrian felt a strange, heavy calm settle over him. It was the calm of exhaustion. He was so tired of being hungry, so tired of being afraid, that the fear of humiliation had begun to pale in comparison to the relief of having a solution. It was a terrible, degrading solution. It required him to give a part of himself every single day to a corporation that viewed him as a variable in an equation. But it was a solution.

He picked up the stylus David had set on the desk. It was cold and smooth in his hand.

"I have one more question," Adrian said.

"Go ahead."

"When I withdraw," Adrian said, "do I have to explain why?"

David smiled, a small, genuine smile that broke through the corporate veneer for a brief second. "No, Adrian. You can withdraw at any time, for any reason, or no reason. That’s a core tenet of the program. Your body belongs to you. We are just renting it for a while."

*Renting it.* The word stuck in Adrian’s mind. Renting his body. It sounded exactly like what it was.

He looked at the screen, then at David, then at the glass doors leading out to the street where the city was waking up to the evening. He thought about his parents, who had died leaving him nothing but a name and a debt. He thought about the empty fridge in his apartment. He thought about the way his hands shook when he tried to hold a pen.

Adrian moved the stylus to the signature line. His hand was steady now. The trembling had stopped, replaced by a rigid, determined stillness. He signed his name.

"Done," he said.

David nodded, taking the tablet back. "Excellent. I’ll get Sarah to bring the biometrics scanner. We’ll have you set up in ten minutes."

Adrian sat down on the plastic chair, the legs scraping against the tile floor. He waited, his hands folded in his lap, feeling the weight of the decision settle into his bones. He had found a way. A terrible, degrading, necessary way. But he had found it.

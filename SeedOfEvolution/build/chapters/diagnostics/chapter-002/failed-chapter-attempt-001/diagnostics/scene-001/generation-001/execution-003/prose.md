The tablet screen glowed with a stark white light, the text crisp and unyielding against the dimming lobby. David tapped the glass, scrolling down with a practiced ease that suggested he ran this show daily.

"Here is the compensation structure," he said, his voice smooth, devoid of the friction Adrian had expected. "Weekly deposit of two thousand dollars. Direct to your bank account. You also receive full coverage for medical expenses related to the study, and a stipend for any missed academic days, up to five per month."

Adrian stared at the numbers. Two thousand. A week. His rent was four hundred. Tuition was a monthly drain he could barely keep ahead of, but this... this was a different scale of existence. It was the difference between surviving and living. It was the difference between eating when he was hungry and eating when he was full.

"Is that... is that real?" Adrian asked, the words feeling thin in his mouth. His stomach gave a low, hollow growl, a reminder of the granola bar he had eaten two days ago, a sad, stale crumble that had sustained him through the worst of his exhaustion.

"It is standard for Phase I and II trials with high retention rates," David said, not looking up. "We value consistency. If you miss a day without a valid medical excuse, the weekly payment is prorated. If you withdraw, you keep the money for the days you participated. There are no clawbacks."

Adrian felt a strange lightness in his chest, a phantom relief that clashed with the heavy, leaden weight of his fatigue. He looked down at his hands. They were pale, the blue veins prominent under the skin, the fingers slightly tremulous. He tucked them into the pockets of his hoodie, trying to hide the evidence of his body’s slow starvation.

"What are the requirements?" he asked. He needed to know. He needed to see the cost before he paid it.

David turned the tablet so Adrian could read. The font was small, legalistic, dense. Adrian squinted, the words swimming slightly in his vision. His eyes burned, a dull ache behind the sockets that sleep deprivation had made a permanent resident.

"Daily administration of the study compound," David read, his finger hovering over the screen. "Oral, once a day, at a consistent time. You will be provided with the medication and a log to track intake. You must report any adverse side effects immediately. And..." He paused, a micro-hesitation that Adrian caught. "And daily sample collection."

The air in the lobby seemed to thicken. The hum of the HVAC system, previously a background whisper, now sounded like a drone. Adrian looked at the line on the screen. *Daily sample collection.*

"What kind of sample?" Adrian asked. His voice sounded distant, as if it were coming from someone else.

"Biological fluid," David said, his tone remaining professional, neutral. "Semen. We are tracking sperm count, motility, and morphological health. The compound is designed to stimulate production and improve quality. To measure that, we need a baseline and daily monitoring."

Adrian felt a heat rise in his neck, a prickling embarrassment that was immediately drowned out by a colder, sharper fear. He looked around the lobby. It was empty, save for the receptionist, Sarah, who was typing on her computer, her back to them. She didn't look up. She didn't need to. This was business. This was data.

"Every day?" Adrian asked.

"Every day," David confirmed. "You will come to our clinic, which is on the third floor, for a private appointment. The process is quick, usually under ten minutes. You will be given a private room, a specimen cup, and instructions. You can use your phone, a magazine, whatever helps you relax. We are not judging. We are collecting data."

*We are not judging.* The phrase hung in the air, sterile and cold. Adrian wanted to laugh, to say something witty, to break the tension with a joke. But the words stuck in his throat. He was nineteen. He was broke. He was alone. And he was being asked to sell his body, piece by piece, for rent money.

"And if I... if I can't?" Adrian started, then stopped. It was a stupid question. He could do it. Everyone did it. It was a biological function. But the clinical nature of it, the fact that it would be logged, measured, and analyzed by a team of scientists in white coats, made it feel alien. It felt like a violation of a privacy he had always assumed he owned.

"Can't do what?" David asked, his brow slightly furrowed. "The medication is a simple pill. The sample collection is a standard medical procedure. If you have a physical inability to perform, we can discuss alternatives, but statistically, that is rare."

Adrian looked at his hands again. The tremor was worse now. He was shaking. Was it the cold? The hunger? Or the sheer, absurd weight of the situation? He was standing in a glass building, surrounded by the quiet efficiency of a private research institute, and he was being asked to become a data point. A number in a spreadsheet. A source of fluid.

"What if I change my mind?" Adrian asked. "After I start?"

"You can withdraw at any time," David said, and this time, his voice softened, just a fraction. A human note in the corporate script. "No questions asked. No penalty. We respect your autonomy. But we do need the data. If you leave, we can't use the samples collected after that date."

Adrian nodded slowly. He understood. He understood the transaction. He was trading his dignity, his privacy, his bodily autonomy, for two thousand dollars a week. It was a bad trade. It was a terrible, degrading, necessary trade. But it was the only trade he could make.

He looked at the tablet again. The numbers were still there. Two thousand. A week. He thought about his apartment, the peeling paint, the leak in the ceiling that he had to catch with a bucket every night. He thought about the empty fridge, the silence of the room, the way his body felt like it was slowly dissolving into the air.

"Where do I sign?" Adrian asked.

David smiled, a small, satisfied expression. He tapped the screen, pulling up a digital signature pad. "Right here. Just your name and date. And here, a brief medical history form. Nothing major, just allergies, current medications, family history."

Adrian took the tablet. His hand was steady now, the tremor gone, replaced by a strange, cold clarity. He looked at the lines on the screen, the blank spaces waiting to be filled. He thought about his parents, who were gone, who had left him with nothing but a name and a debt. He thought about the future, which was usually a terrifying, blurry concept. But now, for the first time in months, the future had a price tag. And he could afford it.

He placed his finger on the screen. The ink flowed, black and sharp against the white background. *Adrian Vale.* It looked official. It looked real.

He signed the medical history next, filling in the blanks with a quick, efficient hand. No allergies. No medications. No family history of heart disease or cancer. Just a blank slate. A clean canvas.

When he was done, he handed the tablet back to David. The coordinator checked the entries, nodded, and tapped a few buttons. A chime sounded, a soft, melodic note that seemed out of place in the sterile lobby.

"Welcome to the program, Adrian," David said. "Sarah will take you to the third floor for your initial screening. It’s a quick blood draw and a physical exam. Then, you’ll be good to go."

Adrian felt a wave of nausea, a sudden drop in his stomach that had nothing to do with hunger. He looked at Sarah, who was now standing up, a polite, professional smile on her face.

"This way," she said, her voice quiet.

Adrian followed her out of the lobby, into the elevator. The doors slid shut, sealing him in a small, mirrored box. He looked at his reflection. He looked thin. He looked tired. He looked like a boy who had just sold his soul, and the price had been surprisingly reasonable.

The elevator began to rise. The numbers on the display ticked up. 1. 2. 3.

He closed his eyes. He was going to do this. He was going to sign the papers, take the pills, and do the... things. He was going to survive.

And for the first time in a long time, that was enough.

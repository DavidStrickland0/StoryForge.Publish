The clinic’s lobby smelled of antiseptic and stale coffee, a scent that had become as familiar to Adrian as his own skin. He stepped through the automatic doors, the glass sliding shut behind him with a soft hydraulic sigh that felt too loud in the quiet afternoon. His legs felt like they were filled with wet sand, each step a negotiation between his will and the trembling muscles beneath his skin. The fluorescent lights above the reception desk hummed, a low, persistent vibration that seemed to resonate in his teeth.

He walked to the front desk, where the receptionist, a woman with gray eyes and a posture of rigid efficiency, looked up from her computer screen. She didn’t smile. She never did. It wasn’t cruelty, Adrian realized; it was simply that she had long ago categorized the men who walked through this door as data points rather than people.

"Adrian Vale," she said, her voice flat. "You are fifteen minutes past your scheduled window."

"I was late from work," Adrian said. His voice sounded thin to his own ears, stripped of the projection he usually managed to muster. He placed his bag on the counter, the fabric scratching against the laminate. "Traffic was bad."

It was a lie. He had walked. He had walked for forty minutes in the cool evening air, his breath coming in short, ragged gasps, because he could not afford the bus fare and because the movement of his legs had been the only thing keeping his consciousness tethered to the present. But the receptionist didn’t look at him with suspicion. She looked at the clock, then at the screen, and the interaction ended before it had truly begun.

"Room 4," she said. "Dr. Thorne is expecting you."

Adrian nodded, his hand shaking as he lifted his bag from the counter. The tremor in his fingers was a constant companion now, a fine, rapid vibration that made holding a pen or a phone feel like trying to hold a live bird. He kept his hands in his pockets as he walked down the hallway, the beige carpet muffling his footsteps. The walls were plain, the windows overlooking a parking lot where a few stray leaves skittered across the concrete.

He reached the door to Room 4 and pushed it open. The room was smaller than the intake office, sparsely furnished with a desk, two chairs, and a medical examination table. Dr. Aris Thorne was seated at her desk, her dark bob catching the light from the overhead panel. She was reviewing a file, her pale eyes scanning the pages with the detached focus of a surgeon reading an X-ray.

She looked up as he entered. Her gaze moved over him, lingering for a fraction of a second on the slight pallor of his skin, the way he stood slightly hunched to conserve energy. Then it returned to the file.

"Sit down, Adrian," she said. Her tone was clinical, devoid of the warmth that might have suggested concern. It was the tone of a machine confirming a process.

Adrian pulled out the chair and sat. The plastic was cold against his thighs. He kept his hands in his lap, interlacing his fingers to hide the tremor. The air in the room was dry, recycled through vents that whirred softly in the corner. He could hear the hum of the computer on Thorne’s desk, the distant sound of traffic from the street outside, and the rapid, thudding beat of his own heart in his ears.

"You look tired," Thorne said. It was a statement, not a question. She turned a page in the file. "How have you been managing the side effects?"

Adrian swallowed. The word felt heavy in his mouth. "Fine," he said. "Just tired."

Thorne’s eyes flicked up to meet his. There was no judgment in them, only a precise assessment. She had seen enough of him, enough of the data he generated, to know that "fine" was a lie. But she let it stand. In the world of the trial, honesty was only required when it served the data.

"Understood," she said. She closed the file and slid it to the side. "We need to collect your daily sample. Please proceed to the examination table."

Adrian stood, his legs protesting the shift in weight. He walked to the table, the metal surface cold under his palm as he adjusted his shirt. The procedure was mechanical, a series of steps he had performed with the dull efficiency of a factory worker. He sat on the edge of the table, his hands gripping the sides, feeling the vibration in his muscles.

Thorne remained at her desk, observing. She did not look away. This was the part of the job that stripped away the last remnants of dignity, the reduction of a human being to a biological function. Adrian closed his eyes, focusing on the rhythm of his breathing, on the distant hum of the city outside. He performed the task, his body moving on autopilot while his mind retreated to the dark, quiet place where the pain and the hunger lived.

"Thank you," Thorne said. Her voice was steady. "Please wash your hands and return to the chair."

Adrian stood, his knees buckling slightly. He caught himself on the edge of the table, his breath coming in short bursts. He walked to the sink, the water running cold over his fingers. The sensation of the cold water was sharp, almost painful, a reminder that he was still physical, still present. He dried his hands on the paper towel, his movements deliberate, controlled.

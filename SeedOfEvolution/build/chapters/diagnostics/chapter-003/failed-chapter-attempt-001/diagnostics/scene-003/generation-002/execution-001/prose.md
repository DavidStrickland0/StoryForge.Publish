The pen tip touched the paper.

Adrian’s hand was steady for exactly the duration of the stroke. He drew his name in the white space, the letters sharp and deliberate despite the tremor that had been fighting for control of his wrist. He lifted the pen, the nib clicking softly against the plastic barrel as he set the instrument down on the desk’s surface.

Dr. Thorne reached across the desktop. She did not look at him as she collected the document. Her fingers were dry, her movements efficient. She aligned the Emergency Intervention Clause with the other forms, the paper sliding with a faint, dry whisper against the laminate. She picked up her own pen, a different one, darker, and signed her name in the adjacent column.

“Enrollment is complete,” she said.

Her voice was flat. It carried the same weight as the notification that the examination room’s door was unlocked or the ventilation system had cycled to a lower setting. She filed the pages into a manila folder, the motion practiced and devoid of ceremony.

Adrian let out the breath he had been holding in his chest for the last two minutes. His lungs expanded, the air moving through a throat that still felt tight and raw. The physical sensation of the signature sinking into the paper was gone, replaced by a dull, throbbing ache behind his eyes. The medication had settled deeper into his system, the chemical pressure building against his temples.

He stood up. The chair legs scraped against the floor, a brief, harsh sound that seemed too loud in the quiet room. He felt unsteady on his feet, the muscles in his legs twitching under his jeans. He adjusted the strap of his bag, his fingers clumsy and slick with sweat.

Thorne was already turning her attention to a file on her desk, her back to him. She pulled out a clipboard and checked the time, a small, mechanical confirmation of the protocol’s rhythm.

“You are to return tomorrow at 8:00 AM,” she said, her gaze still fixed on the clipboard. “The medication schedule remains unchanged. Report any changes in your physical state immediately upon arrival. The red button on the desk is for acute distress. Do not hesitate to use it if you experience pain, vision loss, or inability to stand.”

“I understand,” Adrian said. His voice sounded thin to his own ears, stripped of its usual quiet confidence.

Thorne nodded once, a sharp, jerky motion. She did not offer a greeting, a word of encouragement, or a reminder to eat. She closed the clipboard and set it aside.

Adrian moved toward the door. The hallway outside the examination room was cool, the air conditioning blowing a steady stream of chilled air over his skin. The sensation was sharp, almost painful against the feverish heat radiating from his body. He walked to the intake office, the linoleum floor clicking under his shoes. The receptionist was typing on a keyboard, her gray eyes briefly flicking up as he passed, then returning to her screen. She did not ask how the appointment had gone. She did not offer a smile. She simply logged his departure on her terminal, a small, digital checkmark in a long list of obligations.

The automatic doors slid open, releasing him into the late afternoon light. The city noise rushed in, a wash of traffic, distant voices, and the low rumble of engines. Adrian stepped onto the sidewalk, the concrete warm beneath his soles. He pulled his phone from his pocket, the screen bright against the glare of the sun. He checked his banking app, the numbers refreshing with a soft chime. The deposit was there. The balance was sufficient for the week, perhaps a little more if he was careful with food.

He stood on the curb, watching the stream of people passing by. They moved with a purpose he could no longer fully relate to, their bodies steady, their minds clear. Adrian felt the tremor in his hands again, a fine vibration that traveled up his wrists and into his forearms. He tucked his hands into his pockets, hiding the evidence of his condition. The relief of the signed contract was a heavy, suffocating blanket. He had bought his way through the next semester, but the price was written in the aching lines of his own body. He turned and began the walk back to his apartment, the city noise fading into a distant, meaningless hum as he focused on the next step, and the one after that.

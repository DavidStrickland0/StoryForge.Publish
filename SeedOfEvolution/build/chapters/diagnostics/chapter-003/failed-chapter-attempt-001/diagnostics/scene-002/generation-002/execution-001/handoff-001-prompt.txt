Thorne did not wait for him to settle his trembling hands. She reached across the desk, her movement efficient and precise, and slid a single sheet of paper toward him. It was not the thick stack of consent forms he had signed days ago, nor the lengthy protocols detailing the daily specimen collection. This was one page, dense with small, uniform text, bordered by a thin black line that seemed to cut the rest of the world away.

"Before we proceed with the next phase of data collection," Thorne said, her voice level and stripped of any conversational warmth, "there is an additional authorization required for enrollment in the broader experimental-medicine program. I need you to review this document."

Adrian stared at the paper. The words swam slightly at the edges, a persistent blur that made reading a struggle against his own physiology. He adjusted his grip on the chair’s armrests, his knuckles whitening as he fought the tremor that had started in his fingers and settled deep in his chest. The skin on his forearms felt too tight, as if the medication was pulling the moisture out of his epidermis, leaving a dry, raw sensation that made the fabric of his remaining shirt feel like sandpaper against his neck.

He picked up the pen Thorne had placed beside the document. The click of the cap was loud in the quiet room, echoing off the sterile walls.

"Section two," Thorne said, pointing a slender finger at a paragraph in the middle of the page. "And the final clause. Read them carefully. I cannot expedite the review process."

Adrian forced his eyes to focus on the text. The language was clinical, cold, and absolute. *In the event of a life-threatening medical emergency... the participant hereby grants the research team and affiliated medical staff the authority to override standard consent protocols... to administer any and all interventions deemed necessary to preserve life... including experimental treatments, surgical procedures, and pharmacological agents currently in the pre-clinical or early clinical stage.*

He read it once. The words felt hollow, abstract. He read it again, tracing the lines with his eyes, trying to understand the weight of what he was being asked to sign. It was a surrender of control. If something went wrong—if the medication failed, if his body rejected the compound, if he collapsed in the hallway or on the street—the people in this room would have the legal right to open him up, to inject him with whatever untested substance they had on hand, and to make those decisions without his input. They could do it to keep him alive, they said. But the fine print, the legal scaffolding that supported that benevolent intent, made it clear that his body would become their property in that moment.

"Does this apply to off-site incidents?" Adrian asked. His voice sounded thin, scraped raw by the effort of speaking while his heart hammered against his ribs.

Thorne did not look up. She was organizing a small stack of forms in a tray, her movements rhythmic and detached. "The authorization covers any emergency where you are under the care of the hospital’s trauma or emergency response team. If you are brought in by paramedics, the protocol activates. If you are in a research facility, the protocol activates. It is designed to ensure that time-sensitive interventions are not delayed by bureaucratic consent delays."

"Even if I haven't requested them," Adrian said.

"Especially then," Thorne replied. "If a participant is unconscious or incapacitated, there is no one to request them. The authorization bridges that gap."

Adrian looked down at the document. The ink was black, sharp and definitive. He thought of the empty refrigerator in his apartment, the stack of utility bills on the counter that he had been avoiding for three days, the tuition payment that was due next week and for which he had no funds. He thought of the hunger that was a constant, gnawing presence in his gut, a physical ache that made it difficult to concentrate, let alone read.

He thought of the alternative. If he did not sign this, he was out. The daily payment would stop. The clinic would no longer be a source of income, just a place where he had been told he was a liability. The financial safety net, however thin, would vanish. He would be back to scraping by on diner tips and discount noodles, his body still broken by the medication but without the money to pay for the consequences.

The tremor in his hands worsened. The pen slipped slightly in his grip, and he had to tighten his fingers around it to keep it from falling. The vibration traveled up his arm, into his shoulder, a reminder that his body was currently failing him, even as he sat here in the sterile light of Room 4.

"This is a standard requirement for the experimental program," Thorne said. It was not a question, and it was not an apology. It was a statement of fact, delivered with the same tone she would likely use to note a blood pressure reading. "Your participation in the fertility trial is contingent upon your enrollment in the broader program. This authorization is a prerequisite for that enrollment."

Adrian looked at her. Her pale eyes were steady, devoid of any judgment or concern. She was waiting for him to sign. She was waiting for him to choose between his autonomy and his survival.

He looked back at the page. The words blurred again, the cognitive fog rolling in like a tide. He could feel the edges of his vision softening, the focus slipping. He had to make the decision now, while he still could.

He picked up the pen.

He signed his name at the bottom of the page. The letters wavered slightly, the 'V' in Vale a little too sharp, the 'A' in Adrian a little too flat. He signed the date. He signed the witness line, even though Thorne had not moved to sit next to him.

He set the pen down on the desk. The click of the cap was gone; he had left it uncapped. He let his hand rest on the table, watching the tremor subside, or perhaps just fade into the background of the room's hum.

Thorne picked up the document. She scanned the signature, checking it against the intake forms she had in her hand. She nodded, a single, minimal movement of her head.

"Thank you," she said.

She placed the signed authorization into a folder, closed it, and slid it into a drawer. The click of the drawer locking was the only sound in the room.

"Your enrollment is now fully active," Thorne said. She turned back to her computer, the screen glowing with a soft blue light that reflected in her eyes. "There are no further documents to sign at this time. The data collection schedule remains unchanged. You are to report here at 8:00 AM daily."

Adrian sat in the chair, his hands still resting on his knees. The relief he had expected to feel was absent, replaced by a hollow, exhausted weight in his chest. He had given them the right to take his body apart if it served their research. He had traded his last bit of control for the next month's rent.

"Is that everything?" he asked.

Thorne did not look up from her screen. "That is everything for today."

Adrian stood up. His legs felt unsteady, the floor tilting slightly beneath him. He reached for his shirt, his movements slow and deliberate, aware of the stiffness in his joints. He pulled the fabric over his head, the material cool against his overheated skin.

He walked toward the door, his steps quiet on the linoleum. He did not look back at Thorne, who remained seated at her desk, her fingers moving across the keyboard with a steady, rhythmic precision. The door was open, the hallway beyond it quiet and empty. He stepped through it, the click of the latch behind him marking the end of the appointment, and the beginning of the next day's obligation.

Thorne didn’t wait for a nod. She slid a fresh document across the laminate surface of her desk, the paper catching the overhead fluorescent light with a faint, sterile sheen. It was thicker than the intake forms, the font smaller, the paragraphs dense.

“Before we proceed with today’s protocol, there is a final authorization I need you to review,” she said. Her voice was steady, devoid of the performative warmth one might expect from a healthcare provider. It was the tone of a clerk handling a transaction that required no small talk. “The Emergency Intervention Clause.”

Adrian stared at the sheet. The edges of his vision were already softening, the words swimming slightly in a blur that seemed to be deepening with every passing second. He forced his eyes to focus on the first line, his hands resting flat on his thighs to anchor himself against the tremor that had been ticking up his forearms since he left the diner. The chair’s plastic was cold against his bare skin, a sensation that felt overly sharp, as if the air itself had developed a texture he hadn’t noticed before.

He reached for the paper. His fingers curled around the edge, and the movement sent a jolt of static electricity through him. He pulled the document toward him, his gaze tracking the text in choppy, uneven intervals. The words blurred together, then snapped back into focus, then blurred again. It was like reading through water that was constantly shifting viscosity.

He read the first paragraph. *In the event of a critical medical emergency arising from the administration of the investigational compound, or any complication thereof, the participant hereby grants the clinical team full authority to initiate emergency medical procedures...*

Adrian stopped. He blinked, hard, trying to clear the grit that felt like it was accumulating in the corners of his eyes. He looked up at Thorne. She was already looking at him, her pale eyes unblinking, her posture rigid and expectant. She wasn’t fidgeting. She wasn’t leaning forward. She was simply waiting, her hands resting on the desk, fingers straight and still.

“Does this cover surgery?” Adrian asked. His voice sounded thin in his own ears, a reedy strain that betrayed the exhaustion he was trying to hide.

“It covers any intervention deemed necessary to preserve life,” Thorne replied. “This includes surgical procedures, blood transfusions, and the administration of experimental treatments not yet approved for general public use, if standard protocols are insufficient.”

Adrian looked back down at the page. The phrase *experimental treatments* sat there, plain and unadorned. He had assumed that meant the fertility drug he was already taking. He had assumed that was the limit of the unknown.

He continued reading, his lips moving slightly as he tracked the lines. The physical effort of keeping his eyes on the text was becoming a distinct, draining task. His neck felt tight, the muscles knotted in a way that made turning his head a conscious decision. The room felt too bright. The hum of the ventilation system in the corner of the room was louder than it had been an hour ago, a low, steady drone that seemed to vibrate in his teeth.

*...waiving any right to refuse treatment during the acute phase of the emergency...*

Adrian’s hand tightened on the paper. The fibers of the sheet crinkled under his grip. He thought about the empty refrigerator in his apartment, the stack of unpaid bills on the counter, the final warning notice from the university registrar tucked into his pocket. He thought about the rent due at the end of the month, a sum that would remain unpaid if he dropped out of the trial now. He thought about the fact that he had no savings, no family to call, and no other source of income that required such a specific, invasive surrender of bodily autonomy.

The authorization didn’t just give them permission to treat him. It gave them permission to override his consent in a moment where he might not be in a position to give it. It meant that if his body failed, if the drug caused a hemorrhage or an organ shutdown, they would have the legal and ethical standing to cut him open, to run unknown compounds through his veins, to make decisions about his survival that he could not make for himself because he was unconscious, or incapacitated, or simply too far gone to understand what was happening.

Adrian read the last line of the clause. *By signing below, the participant acknowledges that this authority is irrevocable and takes effect immediately upon enrollment.*

He looked up again. Thorne was still watching him. Her expression hadn’t changed. She didn’t offer an explanation. She didn’t ask if he had questions. She simply waited, her gaze fixed on his face with the detached precision of a machine monitoring a data stream.

Adrian felt a spike of nausea rise in his chest, a hot, heavy pressure that pushed against the back of his throat. He swallowed it down, his jaw clenching. The smell of the clinic—antiseptic, cold air, the faint, metallic tang of the cleaning supplies—was suddenly overwhelming. It seemed to coat the inside of his nose, a physical weight in his sinuses.

He looked at the document again. The words were still there, immutable and final. He knew what it meant. He knew exactly what it cost him. It was a piece of himself, sliced off and handed over to a clinical team that viewed his body as a container for data. And he knew, with a clarity that cut through the fog of his exhaustion, that he could not afford to keep it.

He reached for the pen lying on the desk, his hand steady only through sheer force of will. He picked it up, the plastic smooth and cool in his sweaty palm. He moved the paper so the signature line was positioned to his left, aligning it with the edge of the desk. He didn’t write. He didn’t sign. He held the pen, the tip hovering over the paper, his body trembling in a rhythm that matched the thumping of his own heart.

Thorne didn’t move. She didn’t lean in. She didn’t soften her voice. She simply sat there, her pale eyes fixed on the pen in his hand, waiting for the next step in the protocol. The silence in the room was heavy, filled with the sound of Adrian’s breathing and the persistent, drilling hum of the ventilation system. He was still. He was holding the pen. He was waiting.

The walk from the diner to the clinic took twenty minutes, a span of time that felt like it had been stretched thin over a drum. Adrian’s legs felt like they were filled with wet sand. Every step required a conscious effort to push through the tremors in his knees, a vibration that seemed to originate from deep within his bones rather than his muscles. The city lights blurred into streaks of amber and white as his vision swayed, the world tilting slightly to the left and then correcting itself. He kept his eyes fixed on the pavement, counting his steps to keep his rhythm, but the numbers dissolved before he could reach ten.

He was hungry. The hunger was a physical presence, a hollow ache in the pit of his stomach that made his hands shake. He had skipped lunch to save time, a decision that now felt like a personal insult. His body was demanding fuel he didn’t have, and the medication in his system was demanding something else entirely, a energy he couldn’t name. His skin felt too tight, the air against his face registering as a series of sharp, prickling sensations that made him want to flinch.

When he reached the clinic, the glass doors slid open with a hiss that sounded too loud. The interior was cool, the air conditioning cutting through the heat that radiated from his own skin. The lobby was quiet, the kind of sterile silence that seemed to absorb sound rather than reflect it. Adrian moved toward the reception desk, his bag hanging heavy in his hand. The strap dug into his shoulder, a sharp pain that he welcomed for a second before the rest of his body reminded him that pain was a relative concept.

The receptionist looked up from her computer. Her gray eyes scanned him, pausing on his pale face and the way he was gripping the edge of the desk to steady himself. She didn't ask how he was. She didn't ask why he looked like he was about to faint. She simply pointed to the waiting area.

"Room 4," she said, her voice flat and procedural. "The nurse will be with you shortly."

Adrian nodded. He couldn't speak. His throat felt dry, the muscles in his neck tight and unresponsive. He walked toward the waiting area, a small alcove with three plastic chairs that were too hard and too close together. The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed, a low-frequency hum that seemed to drill into his temples. He sat down, his legs trembling beneath him. He pressed his palms flat against the armrests, trying to anchor himself to the chair, to the floor, to anything that wasn't moving.

A few minutes passed. Or maybe it was an hour. Time had lost its linear quality, stretching and compressing in ways that made no sense. Adrian stared at the wall, watching a small crack in the paint that looked like a vein. He tried to remember the name of the professor in Intro to Econ, but the name slipped away, replaced by a blank white space in his mind. He tried to remember what he had eaten for breakfast, but the memory was equally empty.

The door to Room 4 opened, and the nurse stepped out. She was tall, with short hair and a clipboard tucked under her arm. She looked at Adrian, her eyes moving over his face, his hands, his posture. She didn't say anything. She just waited.

Adrian forced his legs to move. They resisted, protesting the command, but he stood up. He walked toward the door, his steps uneven. The nurse turned and walked back into the room, gesturing for him to follow.

The room was small, dominated by the examination table and the desk where Dr. Thorne usually sat. The red emergency button on the desk caught Adrian’s eye. It was bright, vivid, a stark contrast to the pale walls. He looked away, focusing on the floor.

The nurse moved to the corner of the room, where a small sink and a set of medical supplies were stored. She opened a cabinet and took out a thermometer and a blood pressure cuff. She turned to Adrian, her expression neutral.

"Please remove your shirt," she said.

Adrian hesitated. The command was simple, but it felt like an order to dismantle himself. He looked at the nurse, searching for a cue, a hint of sympathy or urgency. She looked back at him, her face a mask of professional indifference. She was waiting.

He reached for the buttons of his shirt. His fingers were clumsy, the tremors making it difficult to find the small buttons. He fumbled with them, the fabric sticking to his skin due to the sweat that had begun to bead on his forehead. He managed to unbutton the first one, then the second. The third button resisted, and he had to use his teeth to pull it free.

The nurse stepped closer. She didn't touch him, but her presence was a physical weight. She held the thermometer, her hand steady.

"Under the arm," she said.

Adrian placed the thermometer under his arm, closing his hand around it. The cold metal felt shocking against his skin. He waited, his mind drifting again, the edges of his perception blurring. He heard the hum of the lights, the distant sound of traffic outside, the sound of his own breathing.

The nurse checked her watch. She looked at Adrian, then at the thermometer. She waited another minute. Then she took the thermometer from his arm and read the display.

"98.6," she said.

It was a normal temperature. A healthy temperature. Adrian felt a brief surge of relief, followed immediately by a wave of nausea. His body was lying to him, or perhaps he was lying to himself. He didn't know which was true anymore.

The nurse wrapped the blood pressure cuff around his left arm. She pumped the air into it, the pressure building until it felt like his arm was being crushed. She watched the gauge, her eyes focused.

"130 over 85," she said.

It was slightly elevated. Adrian knew that. He had checked his blood pressure at the diner before his shift, and it had been similar. His heart was beating fast, a rapid thumping that he could feel in his ears. He tried to slow his breathing, to calm his body down, but it was like trying to stop a river with his hands.

The nurse unwrapped the cuff and set it aside. She looked at Adrian, her eyes searching. She saw the tremors in his hands, the pale color of his skin, the way he was gripping the edge of the examination table to keep himself upright. She saw it all. And she said nothing.

"Dr. Thorne will be with you in a moment," she said.

She turned and walked out of the room, closing the door behind her. The click of the latch was the only sound in the room. Adrian sat back down on the edge of the examination table, his legs still shaking. He looked at the red button on the desk. He didn't press it. He couldn't afford to press it.

He thought about the money in his account. He thought about the rent due at the end of the month. He thought about the tuition that was already overdue. He thought about the empty fridge in his apartment, the silence of his room, the loneliness that had become a constant companion.

He thought about the consent form. He remembered the words, the fine print, the waivers. He had signed them because he had to. He had signed them because he had no choice.

Now he sat in the room, waiting for the doctor who held his financial survival in her hands. He waited, his body trembling, his mind foggy, his dignity stripped away layer by layer. He waited for the next step, the next obligation, the next cost.

The door opened, and Dr. Thorne walked in. She was tall, her dark bob cut sleek and sharp. Her pale eyes swept over Adrian, taking in his posture, his expression, his state. She moved to her desk and sat down, opening a folder. She didn't look up.

"Sit down," she said.

Adrian stood up and walked to the chair in front of her desk. He sat down, his hands resting on his knees. He looked at Dr. Thorne, waiting for her to speak.

She looked at him, her expression professional, her tone flat.

"Let's begin," she said.

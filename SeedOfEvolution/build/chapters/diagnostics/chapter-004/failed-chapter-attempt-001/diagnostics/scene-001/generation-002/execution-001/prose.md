The air outside the automatic doors hit Adrian’s skin like a slap. It was warm, humid, and thick with the smell of exhaust fumes and frying oil from the bodega across the street. He flinched, his shoulders hunching up toward his ears. The sensation was immediate and electric, a sharp sting that traveled from his forearms down to his wrists. He shook his hands, trying to dislodge the feeling, but the tremor in his fingers was relentless. They vibrated against the cracked screen of his phone, a fine, high-frequency buzzing that made it impossible to hold the device steady.

He looked at the time. 6:58 PM.

Two hours until his shift. Two hours to find something to eat, get back to the apartment, and sleep enough to be awake for the 8:00 AM appointment. The math was simple, and it was entirely impossible.

Adrian turned his head to look at the storefronts. The bodega had a neon sign that buzzed with a low, irritating hum. He could feel the vibration of the light in his teeth. He squinted, trying to read the menu board in the window, but the letters swam and blurred, the black ink bleeding into the white paper. He blinked hard, the motion sending a wave of nausea rolling through his stomach. There was nothing in there. No food, no energy, just the hollow, gnawing emptiness of a body that had been running on fumes and adrenaline for too long.

He turned away from the bodega and started walking.

The street was busy. A delivery truck idled at the curb, its engine a deep, rattling growl that seemed to vibrate through the pavement and up into the soles of his sneakers. A group of students walked past, laughing, their voices bright and sharp in the quiet of his head. Adrian kept his gaze fixed on the middle distance, focusing on a patch of brick on the building across the street to keep the world from spinning. His legs felt heavy, leaden, as if he were wading through deep water. Each step required a conscious effort to lift his foot, place it down, and shift his weight.

He needed to eat. He needed to sit down. He needed to stop shaking.

He turned the corner onto his street. The apartment building was a row of brown brick, the windows dark with the early evening. His door was the fourth one up. He fumbled for his keys in his pocket, his fingers clumsy and thick. The key slid into the lock with a satisfying click, and he pushed the door open.

The apartment was small, a single room with a kitchenette and a bathroom. It smelled of old dust and the faint, lingering scent of the dish soap he used. Adrian closed the door behind him and leaned against it, sliding down until he was sitting on the floor. The linoleum was cold against his back, a grounding sensation that helped anchor him.

He pulled the manila envelope from his jeans pocket. It was light, barely a few sheets of paper, but it felt heavy in his hands. He held it there, the cardboard rough against his trembling fingers. The payment confirmation. The final intake payment. It was processed, he knew. It would hit his account within twenty-four hours. It was enough for the rent. It was enough for the groceries. It was enough to keep the lights on.

But it was also a leash.

He looked at the envelope, then at the door. The door led to the street, to the bodega, to the diner. The envelope led to Room 4, to Dr. Thorne, to the medication that made his skin feel like it was being sandpapered from the inside out.

He didn't get up.

He stayed on the floor, his back against the door, his eyes closed. The silence of the apartment was a relief, a stark contrast to the noise and light of the street. His heart was still pounding, a rapid, thumping rhythm against his ribs. He could feel the blood rushing in his ears, a low, constant hiss.

He thought about the red button on the desk.

He had seen it. It was bright, aggressive red, sitting on the white surface of Dr. Thorne’s desk. A simple, plastic button. A single press would stop the protocol. It would stop the medication. It would stop the pain. And it would stop the money.

Adrian opened his eyes. The room was dim, the light from the street outside casting long, distorted shadows across the walls. He sat up slowly, his muscles protesting the movement. He walked to the kitchenette, the floor creaking under his feet. He opened the refrigerator. Inside, there was a half-empty bottle of water, a carton of eggs, and a container of leftover rice from three days ago.

He took the rice. It was cold and hard, the grains stuck together in clumps. He put it in the microwave, setting the timer for two minutes. While it spun, he washed his face with cold water. The water was shocking against his skin, a clean, sharp sensation that cut through the haze of his thoughts. He looked at himself in the small mirror above the sink.

His face was pale, drawn. His eyes were dark, hollowed out by fatigue. There was a sheen of sweat on his forehead, despite the cool air in the room. He looked like a man who had been running a marathon without stopping.

The microwave beeped.

He took the rice out. It was hot, steaming, the smell of cooked grain filling the small kitchen. He ate it straight from the container, using his fingers. The taste was bland, familiar, comforting. It grounded him. He ate quickly, the hot grains sliding down his throat, settling in his empty stomach.

He finished the container and washed it. He dried it and put it back in the cabinet. He took a long drink of water, the cool liquid soothing the dryness in his throat.

He checked the time. 7:45 PM.

He had an hour and fifteen minutes to get to the clinic.

He got dressed. He put on a clean t-shirt, a pair of jeans, and his sneakers. He brushed his teeth, the minty freshness in his mouth a small, clean victory. He combed his hair, his fingers moving through the strands with a steady, deliberate motion.

He looked at himself in the mirror again.

He was ready.

He walked out of the apartment, locked the door, and began the walk to the clinic.

The street was quieter now, the rush hour traffic having thinned out. The lights of the shops were on, casting pools of yellow and white light onto the sidewalk. Adrian walked with a steady pace, his eyes fixed on the path ahead. He could feel the tremor in his hands, a subtle vibration that he ignored. He could feel the heat in his skin, a constant, low-level burn that he suppressed. He could feel the emptiness in his stomach, a hollow ache that he ignored.

He was a participant. He was a subject. He was a person who needed the money.

He arrived at the clinic. The automatic doors slid open, and the cool, sterile air of the lobby rushed out to meet him. He stepped inside, the smooth floor beneath his feet.

He walked past the reception desk. The receptionist was typing on her keyboard, her face illuminated by the glow of the screen. She looked up, her gray eyes scanning him.

"Mr. Vale," she said, her voice flat. "Dr. Thorne is expecting you."

"Thank you," Adrian said. His voice was steady.

He walked down the hallway, the white doors passing by in a blur. He could see the reflection of his own face in the glass panels of the doors, a distorted, pale image staring back at him. He kept walking.

He reached Room 4.

He pushed the door open.

The room was white, clean, sterile. The light was bright, harsh, unforgiving. Dr. Thorne was sitting at her desk, her tall frame leaning forward, her dark bob framing her face. She was looking at a file, her pale eyes focused on the papers in front of her.

She looked up as he entered.

"Adrian," she said. Her voice was calm, professional. "Please, sit down."

Adrian walked to the chair opposite her desk. He sat down, his hands resting on his knees. His fingers were still, for the moment. He looked at Dr. Thorne, waiting.

Dr. Thorne closed the file in front of her. She reached into the drawer of her desk and pulled out a manila folder. She slid it across the desk toward him.

"I have the final authorization form for you to review," she said. "It is the last document required to complete your enrollment in the program."

Adrian looked at the folder. He reached out and took it, the cardboard cool and smooth in his hands. He opened it, the pages crisp and white.

He looked at the paper.

He was sitting in Room 4, physically present, but internally fraying. The emergency-intervention authorization form was placed before him, a blank sheet of paper waiting for his signature.

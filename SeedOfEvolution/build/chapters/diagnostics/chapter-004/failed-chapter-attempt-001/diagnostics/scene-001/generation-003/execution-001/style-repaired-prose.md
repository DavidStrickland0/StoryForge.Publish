The fluorescent lights in the intake office hummed at a frequency that Adrian felt in his teeth. He blinked, waiting for the afterimages to settle, but the light didn’t seem to dim. It just stayed there, a flat, white glare that washed out the colors of the waiting area until the blue plastic chairs looked like faded smudges of paint against the gray floor.

He checked the time on his phone. 7:42 AM. The battery icon was a red sliver, barely clinging to life, matching his own internal state. He tucked the device into his jacket pocket, the cracked screen pressing a cold line of pressure against his hip. The fabric of his jeans felt coarse, like sandpaper against the raw sensitivity of his skin. Every movement seemed to amplify the static charge running under his epidermis. He adjusted his collar, the cotton tag rubbing against the back of his neck, and he winced, a sharp, involuntary jerk that he quickly masked by clearing his throat.

The receptionist was already looking at him. Her gray eyes tracked his movement with a mechanical precision that made his stomach turn. She didn’t smile. She didn’t ask how he was. She just pointed a manicured finger toward the hallway on the left.

"Room 4," she said. Her voice was flat, devoid of the usual customer-service lilt. "Dr. Thorne is expecting you."

Adrian nodded, keeping his face blank. He knew the route. He had walked it yesterday, and the day before, but the clinic felt different today. The air conditioning was too cold, the smell of antiseptic too sharp, and the surveillance cameras in the corners of the ceiling seemed to track him with a persistent, silent judgment. He kept his head down, focusing on the tip of his shoes as he moved past the magazine rack. The pages were glossy and bright, offering articles about wellness and diet, a stark, mocking contrast to the gnawing hollow in his stomach.

He hadn’t eaten since yesterday’s lunch. It had been a sandwich made of stale bread and processed cheese, washed down with tap water. It hadn’t been enough. Now, the hunger had transformed into a low-grade nausea, a churning sensation in his gut that threatened to surface with every step. He gripped the strap of his bag, his knuckles white, fighting the tremor that was starting to shake his hand. His fingers were useless. They felt like they were filled with cotton, or perhaps just numb, the nerves firing off random signals that his brain couldn’t interpret.

He reached the hallway. The white doors were identical, unmarked, a row of blank faces staring back at him. He counted them, a grounding technique he’d picked up from a stress-management pamphlet he’d read on a bus ride. He counted the first door. He counted the second door. He counted the third door.

He stopped in front of the fourth door. His heart was hammering against his ribs, a frantic, irregular rhythm that echoed in his ears. He pressed his palm against the door. The metal was cool, but it didn’t stop the heat rising in his face. He knocked, the sound dull and muffled.

"Enter," Thorne’s voice called out. It was calm, measured, and utterly unbothered by the fact that Adrian was currently feeling like his organs were vibrating out of sync.

He pushed the door open and stepped inside.

Room 4 was smaller than the waiting area, but it felt more oppressive. The air here was thinner, drier. Thorne sat behind her desk, the manila binder closed, the filing cabinet standing sentinel beside her. She was looking at a screen, her pale eyes reflecting the blue light. She didn’t look up immediately.

"Sit," she said, gesturing to the chair opposite her desk.

Adrian moved to the chair. His legs felt like they were made of water. He sat down, the plastic creaking under his weight, and tried to straighten his spine. The movement sent a jolt of pain through his lower back, a deep, throbbing ache that he ignored. He placed his hands on his lap, interlacing his fingers to keep them from shaking.

Thorne turned from her monitor. Her gaze swept over him, clinical and detached. She wasn’t looking at Adrian as a person, or even as a patient. She was looking at a data point, a variable in an equation that needed to be balanced.

"Your vitals from the last check were elevated," she said. Her voice was neutral, stating a fact. "Heart rate one-twelve. Blood pressure ninety-eight over sixty. We need to address that before we proceed with the final documentation."

Adrian swallowed. His throat felt dry, the saliva thick and difficult to produce. "I’ve been having some headaches," he said, keeping his voice steady. He hoped it sounded convincing. He hoped it sounded like a man who was tired but functional. "Maybe a little dehydrated."

Thorne’s eyes narrowed slightly. She picked up a pen, turning it over in her fingers. "Dehydration is a common factor. However, the trial protocol requires us to rule out adverse reactions to the compound. You know the rules. Any significant deviation from the expected side-effect profile must be reported."

Adrian kept his gaze on her hands, on the way she held the pen, the precise, controlled grip. "I’m managing," he said. It was a lie. A small, desperate lie. He was barely holding it together. The world was blurring at the edges, the details softening into a haze of gray and white. He could feel the sweat beading on his upper lip, a thin, cold trail that he wiped away with the back of his hand, his motion clumsy and jerky.

Thorne watched him. She didn’t push. She didn’t offer sympathy or water or a pill for the nausea. She simply waited, letting the silence stretch until it became uncomfortable, heavy with the unspoken weight of his failure to maintain the facade.

"Very well," she said finally. She reached under her desk and pulled out a folder. It was thin, just a few sheets of paper. She placed it on the desk, sliding it across the polished surface until it stopped just within Adrian’s reach.

"This is the final authorization for the intake phase," Thorne said. "As discussed, the trial involves an expanded protocol for acute medical intervention. We need your signature to complete the file."

Adrian stared at the paper. The black text on the white page seemed to swim before his eyes. He could see the words, but they didn’t quite connect. *Emergency-intervention authorization.* *Life-threatening situations.* *Override patient consent.*

He knew what it meant. He had read it, or skimmed it, or let his mind slide over the legalese because he was too hungry, too tired, too scared to think about the consequences. Now, it was in front of him. A contract. A surrender.

He reached for the paper. His hand was shaking now, an uncontrollable tremor that he couldn’t hide. He gripped the edge of the folder, his fingers slipping slightly on the smooth paper. He forced his breath to slow, to deepen, to center. He focused on the coolness of the desk, the solid reality of the wood beneath his palm.

Thorne watched him, her expression unreadable. She didn’t look away. She didn’t offer a reprieve. She just waited, a statue of clinical detachment in a room that smelled of disinfectant and old paper.

Adrian picked up the pen from the desk. It was heavy, solid, a real object in a world that was starting to feel like a dream. He uncapped it, the click loud and sharp in the quiet room. He looked at the line where his signature was required. It was blank. It was waiting.

He was seated in Room 4, the form before him, the weight of the decision pressing down on his shoulders, physically present but internally fraying, the emergency-intervention authorization form placed before him by Dr. Thorne, the final barrier to the payment he needed, the price of his survival, written in black ink on white paper.

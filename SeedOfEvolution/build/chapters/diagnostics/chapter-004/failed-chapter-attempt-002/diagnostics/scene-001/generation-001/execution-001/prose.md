The automatic doors hissed open, exhaling a gust of conditioned air that smelled of floor wax and antiseptic. Adrian stepped into the lobby, the sudden drop in temperature hitting his skin like a slap. His fingers were still trembling, the fine tremor that had started in his hands during the walk from the bus stop refusing to settle. He gripped the strap of his bag tighter, his knuckles white, trying to force the muscles in his forearms to lock around the nylon.

He checked the time on his cracked phone screen. 7:42 AM. Eighteen minutes until he had to be in the waiting area. The clock on the wall above the reception desk ticked with a metronomic precision that seemed to mock the frantic rhythm of his own heart.

The receptionist looked up from her computer. Her gray eyes scanned him, lingering for a fraction of a second on the sweat darkening the collar of his t-shirt, before moving to the ID badge he held out. She didn’t ask how he was. She never did.

"Adrian Vale," she said, her voice flat and professional. "Room 4. Elevator on the left. Don’t linger in the halls."

He nodded, unable to speak without risking a stutter. The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed, a low-frequency hum that vibrated in his teeth. He moved toward the elevator, his steps careful, measured. The hallway was carpeted in a beige so neutral it was almost offensive, the walls painted a shade of white that seemed to absorb the light rather than reflect it.

The elevator doors slid shut, sealing him in a small, mirrored box. His reflection stared back at him: pale, thin, eyes hollowed out by exhaustion. He pressed the button for the fourth floor. The car rose with a smooth, silent glide, and he watched the floor numbers count up. 1. 2. 3. 4.

When the doors opened, the hallway was identical to the one on the ground floor. White doors. White walls. The air conditioning vent near the ceiling blew a steady stream of cold air, and Adrian flinched, pulling his jacket tighter around himself. The hyperesthesia made the cold feel like needles pricking the surface of his skin, a sharp, electric sting that traveled up his neck and settled behind his ears.

He walked to the end of the hallway. Room 4. The door was closed, a thin strip of light visible beneath it. He raised his hand to knock, but the movement triggered a spasm in his wrist, and his fingers curled inward, gripping the air. He took a deep breath, forcing his lungs to expand, forcing his muscles to relax.

He knocked. Two sharp raps.

"Come in," a voice called out. It was calm, measured, and utterly unbothered by the fact that Adrian was currently feeling like his organs were vibrating out of sync.

He pushed the door open.

Dr. Aris Thorne sat at her desk, a manila binder open in front of her. She was tall, lean, with a dark bob that framed a face of sharp, angular features. Her pale eyes were fixed on the documents in front of her, and she did not look up as he entered.

"Adrian," she said, her tone neutral. "Close the door."

He closed the door, the latch clicking softly in the quiet room. The space was small, sparse. A desk, a chair, a filing cabinet, and a red emergency button on the corner of the desk. The button was bright, almost aggressive in its color, a stark contrast to the sterile white of the room.

Thorne gestured to the chair opposite her. "Sit."

Adrian sat. The chair was cold, the plastic seat hard against his back. He placed his bag on the floor beside him, his hands resting on his knees. They were still shaking. He tried to hide them, tucking them under his thighs, but the tremor was persistent, a constant, low-level vibration that he could not stop.

Thorne looked up, her gaze sweeping over him. She didn’t ask how he was. She didn’t ask if he was feeling well. She simply looked at him, her expression unreadable, and then reached into the manila binder on her desk.

She pulled out a document. It was a single sheet of paper, printed on both sides, the text dense and small. She slid it across the desk toward him.

"Read this," she said.

Adrian stared at the paper. The words blurred before his eyes, the letters swimming on the page. He blinked, trying to focus, but the nausea that had been building in his stomach all morning rose again, a wave of heat that threatened to push the acid up into his throat.

He picked up the paper. The weight of it felt significant, heavy with implication. He began to read.

The title was bold, centered at the top of the page. *Emergency Intervention Authorization.*

His eyes scanned the text, the words jumping and dancing before his sight. He read the first paragraph, then the second. The language was legalistic, precise, devoid of emotion. It outlined the conditions under which the research team would be authorized to intervene in his care. It detailed the scope of their authority, the procedures they would follow, the criteria for their decision-making.

He read the third paragraph. And the fourth.

The nausea intensified, a dull ache behind his eyes. The words on the page seemed to lose their meaning, the sentences running together in a blur of black and white. He tried to focus, to understand, but his mind was foggy, his thoughts sluggish and slow.

He looked up at Thorne. She was watching him, her expression still neutral, her pale eyes steady and unblinking.

"There’s more," she said.

Adrian looked back down at the paper. His hands were shaking again, the tremor so severe that the paper fluttered in the air. He pressed it down with his palm, forcing it flat, forcing it still.

He continued to read.

The document outlined the specific protocols for emergency intervention. It described the types of procedures that could be performed, the range of treatments that could be administered, the extent of the research team’s authority. It was a comprehensive list of the actions they would be permitted to take, the decisions they would be empowered to make, the choices they would be allowed to dictate.

Adrian read the last paragraph.

He set the paper down on the desk. His hands were trembling, the tremor so intense that his fingers were blurred with motion. He took a deep breath, trying to steady himself, trying to find his footing.

Thorne watched him, her expression unreadable. She waited.

Adrian picked up the pen from the desk. It was a simple black ballpoint, unremarkable in its design. He held it in his hand, the weight of it familiar, comforting.

He looked at the document. Then he looked at Thorne.

He signed his name.

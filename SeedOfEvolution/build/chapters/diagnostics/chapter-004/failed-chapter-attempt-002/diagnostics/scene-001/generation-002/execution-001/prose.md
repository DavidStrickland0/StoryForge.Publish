The bodega was closed. The fast-food place across the street was open, but the line stretched out the door and into the evening light, and Adrian calculated the cost of the wait. He had two hours until his shift. He had no money for a meal that required standing in line for ten minutes. He had a cracked phone in his hand, the screen spider-webbing across the display, and a manila envelope in his jeans pocket that felt heavier than paper should.

He turned away from the storefronts. The street noise, the hum of the refrigerator cases, the distant traffic—it all pressed against his eardrums like a physical weight. The sun was still high, but it felt wrong, too bright, too sharp against the skin of his face. He could feel the heat radiating off the asphalt, a dull, throbbing pulse that matched the beat in his own chest. One hundred and twelve beats per minute. He knew the number because he had checked it on the monitor in Room 4, and he knew it because his body was currently operating at that exact frequency, a frantic, vibrating engine that refused to idle.

He needed to go back.

The logic was cold and absolute. The trial paid. The trial kept him enrolled. The trial was the only reason he was still a student, the only reason he wasn't already dropping out to work double shifts at the diner and sleeping on a friend's couch. The medication was making him feel like his nervous system had been stripped bare and left out in the rain, but the money was real. The money was not a hallucination. The money would cover rent. The money would cover food, eventually.

He walked back toward the clinic. The automatic doors slid open with a soft, pneumatic hiss, admitting him into the sterile, air-conditioned silence of the lobby. The air smelled of antiseptic and lemon cleaner, a scent that usually triggered a mild headache, but today it was a grounding force. It was a smell that meant structure. It meant rules. It meant that someone else was in charge of the chaos that was currently rioting inside his skull.

The receptionist looked up from her computer. She had gray eyes and a face that had seen a thousand similar arrivals. She did not smile. She did not ask how he was. She simply pointed a pen at the check-in sheet.

"Vale, Adrian," she said. Her voice was flat, professional. "You're early."

"Third day," Adrian said. His voice sounded thin, scraped raw by the tremor in his throat. He tried to swallow, but his throat muscles felt stiff, uncooperative. "I didn't want to miss the window."

It was a lie. He had missed the window. He had been standing on the street, unable to make a decision, unable to move his legs in a straight line. But the receptionist did not look at him with judgment. She looked at the sheet. She looked at the clock on the wall. She stamped the paper.

"Room 4," she said. "Dr. Thorne is expecting you."

Adrian nodded. He turned to leave the lobby, but his step faltered. His left leg had seized, a tight, electric knot forming in the calf muscle. He stopped, breathing through his nose, forcing the sensation to pass. He focused on the blue plastic chairs in the waiting area. He focused on the magazine rack, the identical white doors lining the hallway. He focused on the red emergency button on the desk in Room 4, a small, bright point of color in the monochrome world.

He reached the door to Room 4. His hand shook as he reached for the handle. The metal was cold, and the contact sent a jolt of hyperesthesia up his arm, a sharp, stinging pain that made him flinch. He gritted his teeth, forcing his fingers to curl around the handle. He turned it. The door opened.

Dr. Thorne was sitting at her desk. She was tall, lean, with a dark, sleek bob haircut and pale eyes that looked almost colorless in the clinical light. She was reading a file, her glasses perched on the bridge of her nose. She did not look up immediately. She let him stand in the doorway for a moment, letting him adjust to the room, to the silence, to her presence.

"Close the door," she said. Her voice was calm, measured. It was the voice of someone who had seen this before, of someone who knew that the shaking in his hands was not a choice.

Adrian closed the door. The latch clicked shut, a small, definitive sound. He stood in the center of the room, his back to the wall, his hands hanging at his sides. He could feel the vibration in his fingers, a constant, low-frequency buzz that made it difficult to keep his palms flat.

"Sit down," Dr. Thorne said. She gestured to the chair opposite her desk.

Adrian moved to the chair. His knees buckled slightly as he sat, the muscle in his thigh failing to support his weight for a fraction of a second. He caught himself, his elbows digging into his own legs to steady his torso. He placed his hands on his lap, interlacing his fingers to hide the tremor. The skin on his knuckles felt paper-thin, as if the bones were pressing against the surface.

"Vitals first," Dr. Thorne said. She reached for a blood pressure cuff on the side of her desk. She did not ask how he was. She did not ask if he was in pain. She simply began to wrap the cuff around his left arm.

The cold of the vinyl against his skin was a shock. He held his breath, counting the seconds in his head. One. Two. Three. The cuff inflated, squeezing his arm, cutting off the blood flow. He felt the pressure, the firm, unyielding grip of the device. He focused on the sensation, using it as an anchor. If he could feel the pressure, he could feel the room. If he could feel the room, he could remain present.

The cuff deflated. The needle on the gauge swung.

"112," Dr. Thorne said. "98 over 60."

She recorded the numbers in her chart. She did not comment on them. She did not look at him with concern or alarm. She simply filed the information away.

"Side effects?" she asked.

Adrian looked at her. His vision blurred for a moment, the edges of the room softening, the light from the window smearing into a white haze. He blinked, forcing the focus back. He felt the nausea rising in his stomach, a dull, rolling wave that made his hands sweat.

"Managing," he said.

The word felt small, inadequate, a lie that sat in his mouth like a stone. He looked at his hands, interlaced in his lap. He could feel the tremor, the tiny, rapid vibrations that he could not stop. He could feel the heat in his cheeks, the flush of blood rushing to his face. He could feel the absolute, terrifying fragility of his own body, the way it was falling apart, piece by piece, while he sat there, smiling, nodding, saying the right words.

Dr. Thorne watched him. Her pale eyes were unreadable. She leaned forward, resting her chin on her hand, and looked at him with an intensity that made him want to shrink back into the chair.

"Adrian," she said. "We need to discuss the next step."

He waited. The air in the room felt thick, heavy with the weight of what was coming. He knew what was coming. He had known it since he had signed the initial consent form. He had known it since he had sat in this chair, since he had felt the first dose of the medication enter his bloodstream. He had known that this moment would come, that the trial would demand more of him, that the cost of his survival would be paid in increments, each one slightly more expensive than the last.

"Take your time," Dr. Thorne said.

She reached into the manila binder on her desk and pulled out a document. It was a single sheet of paper, crisp and white, printed with dense, black text. She placed it on the desk in front of him. She did not push it across. She did not gesture to it. She simply left it there, a stark, rectangular object in the center of the wooden surface.

Adrian looked at the paper. He looked at Dr. Thorne. He looked at the red emergency button on the desk, a small, bright point of color that seemed to pulse in the silence. He looked at his hands, still trembling, still vibrating, still trying to hold himself together.

He picked up the pen from the desk. The metal was cold. He held it in his right hand, his fingers wrapping around the barrel, his grip tight, his knuckles white.

He was ready.

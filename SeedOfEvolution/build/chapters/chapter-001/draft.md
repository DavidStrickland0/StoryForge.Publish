The radiator in the corner clanked, a dry, metallic percussion that marked the seconds in the small, drafty studio. Adrian sat on the edge of his mattress, the fabric stretching taut over his hip bones. He held a can of generic brand soda, the tab pulled but the liquid untouched, its cold condensation beading on his fingers. He had bought it for the caffeine, but the price tag had cost him a dinner.

On the desk, the laptop screen glowed with the university’s student portal. The balance due was a bright red number, pulsing with a quiet, digital urgency. $4,200.50.

He clicked the link to the financial aid office’s FAQ. The text scrolled past in a blur of bureaucratic jargon. *Disbursement schedules. Verification of enrollment. Emergency hardship grants.* The words were sterile, detached from the physical reality of the hollow ache in his stomach. He had applied for the grant three weeks ago. The status was still *Processing*.

Adrian closed the laptop. The screen went dark, reflecting his own face back at him. He looked back, and the reflection looked back with the same tired, wary expression. He was nineteen, but in the dim light of the unlit room, he looked older. The shadows under his eyes were not just a lack of sleep; they were a structural feature of his life, etched there by months of skipping meals and working double shifts at the library.

He stood up. His joints popped, a small, dry sound in the silence. He walked to the window, pressing his forehead against the cold glass. The street below was wet from a recent shower, the rain washing the grime off the pavement into the gutters. The city lights smeared into long, horizontal streaks, orange and yellow, indifferent to the life happening inside the apartment.

He pulled his phone from his pocket. The battery was at twelve percent. He didn’t have a charger left after the last one had broken, a casualty of the constant shuffling between the library, the apartment, and the campus. He typed a message to Marcus, his roommate who had moved to Paris for a summer internship.

*Hey man, how’s that study thing going? You said you were looking into that hospital program?*

The text appeared on the screen, the cursor blinking at the end of the line. Adrian stared at it, the blue light illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air. He had mentioned it once, in passing, a throwaway comment about how he was looking for ways to make ends meet. He hadn’t expected Marcus to remember.

He deleted the message.

He didn’t want to talk about it. He didn’t want to explain the math that had led him here, the cold, hard calculus of survival. He didn’t want to admit that he had been looking at the hospital’s website for three days, reading the consent forms, the protocols, the disclaimers. He didn’t want to admit that he had printed out the application, sat at the kitchen table, and started to fill it in, only to stop when his hands had started to shake.

He had put the paper in the trash.

Now it was raining again. The sound was different, a steady, rhythmic drumming against the windowpane. Adrian watched the droplets race down the glass, leaving small, winding trails. They were like the paths of the insects that crawled across the library’s reading tables, the ones he had to carefully move out of the way of the students. He remembered the way the students would look at him, the way they would shift their weight, the way they would adjust their glasses. He was invisible to them, a ghost in the machine, a functionary of the institution.

He needed to be a functionary of something else.

He picked up the can of soda. The aluminum was cold, the condensation dripping onto his wrist. He took a sip. The sugar hit his tongue, a sharp, bright sweetness that cut through the dullness of the room. He drank half the can, the carbonation fizzing in his throat. He set the can down on the desk, the metal clicking against the wood.

He opened the laptop again. The red number was still there, waiting. He navigated back to the hospital’s website. The page loaded slowly, the text appearing in chunks. He found the link to the experimental medicine program.

The screen filled with text. It was dense, technical, full of acronyms and subheadings. *Phase I. Phase II. Phase III.* The words blurred together, a wall of white letters on a black background. He scrolled down, his finger moving across the trackpad.

*Male-Fertility Trial. Investigational. Low-Risk.*

He stopped reading. The words hung in the air, suspended in the silence of the room. He looked at the text, then at the window, then back at the text. The rain was getting heavier, the sound rising to a roar.

He started to fill out the form.

His fingers moved across the keyboard, clicking the boxes, typing his name, his address, his date of birth. The cursor blinked at the end of each line, waiting for the next input. He typed his medical history, his allergies, his current medications. The list was short. No medications. No allergies. No history of disease.

He reached the section on consent. The text was long, a block of black letters that stretched down the page. He read it slowly, his eyes moving line by line. He understood the words, but the meaning felt distant, abstract. It was a legal document, a contract, a binding agreement. It was the thing that would keep the door locked, the lights on, and his place in the lecture hall.

He signed his name.

The cursor blinked. The form was submitted.

Adrian leaned back in his chair, the spine creaking under his weight. He felt a strange sense of relief, a small, fragile bubble of hope expanding in his chest. He was going to do this. He was going to sign the papers. He was going to get the money.

He looked at the window. The wind was picking up, the rain starting to fall in thin, gray sheets. The city lights blurred into streaks of orange and yellow. He felt a cold weight in his chest, a fear that was hard to name.

He closed the laptop. The lid clicked shut with a finality that echoed in the small, drafty apartment.

The rain had stopped, leaving the windows streaked and the air in the apartment heavy with the scent of wet asphalt and old dust. Adrian sat in the chair, his back straighter than it had been in weeks, though his stomach still churned with a hollow, acidic ache. The silence of the studio was different now. It didn’t feel like an empty void anymore; it felt like a held breath.

He checked his phone. 11:42 PM. The battery icon was a thin red sliver. He plugged it into the wall outlet, the little light blinking green, and watched the percentage tick upward. One percent. Two. It was a small, mechanical comfort, the only thing in his life that moved in predictable, linear increments.

His application was in. The next step was confirmation.

He knew the drill, or at least the vague outline of it. He had read the FAQ on the hospital’s website until the words blurred together. *Eligibility screening. Preliminary health assessment. Consent briefing.* It sounded like a job interview for a position he wasn’t qualified for, except the job was his own biology and the pay was survival.

He stood up, his joints popping in the quiet room. The floorboards creaked under his weight, a sound that always made him feel like he was walking on thin ice. He walked to the small kitchenette, where the sink held the remnants of his dinner: a bowl with a film of grease on the surface and a fork resting against the rim. He didn’t have the energy to wash it, so he left it, pushing the bowl to the back of the sink where the drain gurgled softly.

He needed to prepare. Not just mentally, but physically. The intake appointment was tomorrow, ten in the morning. He had no car, no bus pass, and his feet were already aching from the long walk home. He would have to take the city bus, transfer once, and walk the last three blocks from the stop to the hospital’s main entrance. It would take an hour.

He pulled on a clean shirt, one that had been washed twice and hung on the back of the door to dry. It was a solid blue, free of stains, but the fabric was soft and thin, clinging to the sharp angles of his shoulders. He looked in the mirror above the sink. The reflection staring back was gaunt, with dark circles under eyes that were too wide, too alert. He looked like he hadn’t slept in a month. He hadn’t.

He brushed his hair back, trying to smooth the unruly strands. It didn’t help. He looked tired. He looked poor. He looked like a nineteen-year-old who was trying to sell his kidneys to pay for tuition.

The difference, he told himself, was that the hospital was offering to buy his time and his samples, not his organs. Well, sort of.

He lay down on the narrow bed, the mattress thin and lumpy. The ceiling was painted a pale, institutional white, peeling slightly at the edges. He stared at the water stain in the corner, a brownish map of a place he didn’t want to visit, and tried to sleep.

It took him a long time. The rain started again, tapping against the window glass, a rhythmic, insistent sound. He lay there, listening to the city breathe, feeling the cold seep through the cheap blanket. He was afraid. But he was also, for the first time in months, not entirely alone in his fear. He had a plan. A terrible, undignified, expensive plan. But a plan.

***

The hospital was a fortress of glass and steel, rising out of the gray morning like a monolith. The sky was overcast, a flat sheet of lead that made the building’s reflections look dark and distorted. Adrian stood on the sidewalk, his backpack heavy with a change of clothes and his wallet, which contained eleven dollars and a library card.

The lobby was vast and bright, the floors polished to a mirror sheen. The air smelled of antiseptic and expensive flowers. He felt small. He felt dirty.

He walked to the reception desk, where a woman with a name tag that read *ELISE* was typing on a keyboard. She didn’t look up immediately. When she did, her eyes swept over him, taking in his thin frame, his worn shoes, his anxious posture. Her expression didn’t change, but something in her eyes shifted, a subtle tightening.

“Can I help you?” she asked. Her voice was smooth, professional, devoid of warmth.

“I’m here for the intake appointment,” Adrian said. “Adrian Vale.”

Elise stopped typing. She looked at the screen, then back at him. “Do you have your confirmation email?”

Adrian nodded. “It’s on my phone.”

He pulled out his device, the battery now at forty percent, and showed her the screen. Elise read the message, her face impassive. She reached under the desk and pulled out a clipboard.

“Please fill this out,” she said, sliding it across the counter. “It’s a standard health history and liability waiver. It’s a lot of pages.”

Adrian took the clipboard. The paper was crisp, white, and expensive. He looked at the top of the first page. *Patient Information and Consent for Experimental Medicine Program.*

His hands trembled slightly as he uncapped the pen. The ink was black, sharp, and permanent. He started to write.

*Name: Adrian Vale.*
*Date of Birth: October 14, 2004.*
*Address: 42nd Street, Apt 3B.*

He filled in the details, his handwriting shaky. The questions were clinical, detached. *Do you have any chronic conditions? No. Are you currently taking any medication? No. Have you been diagnosed with any genetic disorders? No.*

He paused at the section on sexual health. *Are you currently sexually active? Yes. Do you use protection consistently? Yes.*

He felt a flush of heat rise up his neck. He looked around the lobby. There were other people there, mostly older, some in hospital gowns, some in business suits. They all looked like they had a reason to be here, a reason that made sense. He didn’t.

He finished the form and handed it back to Elise. She scanned it quickly, her eyes lingering on the sexual health section for a fraction of a second longer than the rest. She didn’t comment on it.

“Thank you,” she said. “I’m going to take you to the intake clinic. Please follow me.”

She led him through a set of double doors, into a hallway that was quieter, dimmer. The air here was colder, and the smell of antiseptic was stronger. They walked past several closed doors, each with a small window in the center. Through the glass, Adrian could see people sitting in chairs, waiting. Some were looking at their phones. Some were looking at the floor.

They stopped in front of a door labeled *Intake Room 1*. Elise knocked, then opened the door.

Inside, a man sat at a desk, reviewing a stack of files. He was middle-aged, with graying hair and glasses that sat low on his nose. He looked up as they entered, his eyes meeting Adrian’s.

“Mr. Vale?” the man asked.

“Yes,” Adrian said.

“I’m Dr. Aris,” the man said, standing up and extending his hand. “Please, have a seat.”

Adrian sat down on the edge of the chair, his legs stiff. Dr. Aris closed the door behind him, the click of the lock sounding final.

“Thank you for coming in today,” Dr. Aris said, his voice calm, measured. “I’ve reviewed your application. It was… thorough. And honest.”

Adrian didn’t know what to say to that. “Thank you.”

Dr. Aris sat down, leaning back in his chair. “Before we begin the physical examination, I want to walk you through the details of the trial. I know you’ve read the informational materials, but I want to make sure you understand exactly what you’re signing up for.”

He opened a folder on his desk and pulled out a single sheet of paper. He slid it across the table to Adrian.

“This is the core protocol,” Dr. Aris said. “The study is focused on male reproductive health. Specifically, we are testing a new compound designed to enhance sperm motility and count. It’s a low-risk study, but it does require daily participation.”

Adrian looked at the paper. The words were dense, filled with medical jargon. *Compound. Motility. Count.*

“Daily participation?” Adrian asked.

“Yes,” Dr. Aris said. “You will need to visit the clinic every day for the duration of the trial, which is twelve weeks. You will take a dose of the compound, and then you will provide a sample.”

Adrian felt a cold knot form in his stomach. “A sample.”

“A semen sample,” Dr. Aris said, his tone neutral, clinical. “It’s a standard procedure in reproductive medicine. We will provide a private room for you to do this. It will take about ten to fifteen minutes.”

Adrian stared at the doctor. His face was blank, professional. There was no judgment, no pity. Just facts.

“Is that… is that all?” Adrian asked.

“For the most part, yes,” Dr. Aris said. “You will also need to report any side effects, no matter how minor. And you will need to take the medication exactly as directed. No skipping doses, no altering the

“—altered the dosage. The compound is designed to work in a specific cycle. If you break that cycle, the data becomes useless, and we cannot verify safety or efficacy.”

Dr. Aris tapped the sheet of paper with a manicured fingernail, a gesture that seemed almost mechanical against the sterile white of the intake room. “You will receive a weekly stipend. It will be deposited directly into your account every Friday. In exchange, you are required to be here, in this building, once every twenty-four hours. The timing is flexible within a three-hour window, but the frequency is absolute. There are no weekends off. There are no holidays.”

Adrian looked down at the paper. The font was small, the language dense with legal disclaimers about bodily autonomy and experimental risk. He had read it twice already. The first time, he had scanned for the word *pain*. The second time, he had scanned for the word *death*. Neither was there. In their place were words like *motility*, *concentration*, and *specimen*.

He did the math in his head. It was a calculation he had been running for three weeks, ever since the university’s financial aid office had told him that his Pell Grant would cover tuition and books, but not room and board. The rent for his studio apartment was a fixed, unyielding line item. The cost of food was a variable that fluctuated with his blood sugar and his pride. He was currently sitting on eleven dollars and a library card.

The stipend was $200 a week. That was nine hundred dollars a month. That was enough for rent. That was enough for groceries. That was enough to keep the lights on without having to choose between heating the apartment and buying protein.

He looked up at Dr. Aris again. The doctor was waiting, a pen poised over a clipboard. The silence in the room was heavy, filled with the hum of the air conditioning and the distant, muffled sound of the hospital lobby.

“Is the room… private?” Adrian asked. His voice sounded thinner than he expected.

“Completely,” Dr. Aris said. “It is a standard clinical examination room. It has a lock on the door from the inside. You will be given a sterile cup and a lid. You will seal it, label it with your participant ID, and place it in the drop box. The technician will collect it. You will not speak to anyone. You will not be observed. It is a routine biological sample collection.”

Adrian nodded. He could feel the heat rising in his cheeks, a prickly embarrassment that settled low in his stomach. It was undignified. It was tedious. It was, in the grand scheme of human experience, profoundly mundane. He was about to trade his bodily fluids for rent money, one morning at a time, for three months.

He thought about the last time he had had to do something that felt this vulnerable. It had been in high school, when he had failed his driving test because he couldn’t keep his eyes on the road while the instructor sat in the passenger seat, breathing heavily. He had felt small then. He felt small now.

But he was hungry.

The hunger was a constant companion, a low-grade ache that lived behind his ribs. It was the kind of hunger that made the cafeteria food taste like cardboard and made the idea of buying a sandwich with a filling sound like a luxury he couldn’t afford. He was nineteen years old, and his life had become a series of logistical problems. How to get to class. How to get to work. How to eat. How to sleep.

This was a solution. It was a bad solution, but it was a solution.

“Do I need to bring anything?” Adrian asked.

“No. We provide everything. You just need to bring yourself. And your medication. You will pick up your daily dose from the pharmacy on your way out.”

Adrian stood up. His legs felt unsteady, as if the chair had been waiting for him to leave so it could collapse. He walked to the desk where the consent forms were waiting. The pen was heavy in his hand.

He looked at the signature line. *Adrian Vale*.

He signed his name. The ink bled slightly into the paper, a dark, permanent mark.

Dr. Aris took the forms, checked them, and slid them into a folder. “You are now enrolled in the trial,” she said. “Your first dose is in the pharmacy. I will walk you there.”

Adrian followed her out of the room. The hallway was bright and white, the floor polished to a mirror shine. He could see his own reflection in the glass doors of the labs that lined the corridor. The reflection looked gaunt, tired, and uncertain.

He walked faster.

The pharmacy was at the end of the hall. A man in a white coat stood behind the counter, looking bored.

“Vale,” Dr. Aris said. “This is Mr. Vale. He’s starting the daily regimen.”

The pharmacist nodded. “Take number 402. One capsule every morning. With food. Do not crush or chew.”

Adrian took the small, orange pill. It fit easily in his palm. He put it in his pocket.

“Thank you,” Adrian said.

He turned and walked back out into the lobby. The receptionist, Elise, was still sitting at the desk, typing on her keyboard. She looked up and smiled.

“Everything okay?” she asked.

“Yes,” Adrian said. “It’s fine.”

He walked out of the hospital and into the morning air. The sky was still gray, the clouds heavy and low. He took a deep breath. The air tasted of rain and exhaust fumes.

He had money. He had food. He had a future.

It was, he realized, the most boring future he had ever had.

And he was going to love every minute of it.

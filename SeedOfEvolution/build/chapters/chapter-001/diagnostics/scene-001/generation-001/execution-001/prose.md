The fluorescent lights in the Financial Aid Office hummed with a frequency that Adrian felt in his teeth. It was a low, persistent buzz, the kind that drilled into the base of the skull and refused to leave. He sat on the edge of a plastic chair, his knees pressed together, his hands resting flat on his thighs. His jeans had a hole at the knee, worn thin by months of walking across campus to save the bus fare. He could feel the draft from the HVAC unit through the fabric, a cold that had become a permanent resident of his joints.

On the desk before him, Officer Miller shuffled a stack of papers. The sound was dry, like dead leaves skittering across pavement. Miller was a heavy man with a receding hairline and a tie that looked like it had been selected by a random algorithm: bright blue, with small white dots that seemed to vibrate against his shirt. He adjusted his glasses, looking down at the document in his hand, then up at Adrian, then back down.

"Mr. Vale," Miller said. His voice was flat, stripped of any inflection that might suggest empathy or even boredom. It was the voice of a man reading a menu he had already decided not to order from. "I’ve reviewed your application for the Emergency Student Hardship Grant."

Adrian nodded. His stomach gave a low, hollow rumble. He had eaten nothing since yesterday afternoon, when a leftover slice of pizza from the cafeteria had disappeared into his stomach like a stone into a well. The taste of it was already fading, replaced by the metallic tang of hunger that coated the back of his throat. He kept his expression neutral. He had practiced this in the mirror of his dorm room, a small square of glass that reflected a boy who looked less like a student and more like a sketch of a student. Thin. Sharp-featured. Eyes that were too large for his face because the rest of him had shrunk around them.

"The grant," Miller continued, tapping a pen against the desk, "is designed for students facing sudden, unforeseen crises. Natural disasters, family emergencies, loss of employment due to no fault of their own."

"I lost my job," Adrian said. His voice was quiet. He had to be careful. If he spoke too loudly, it would sound desperate. If he spoke too softly, it would sound weak. He aimed for the middle, a tone of reasonable inquiry. "The bar closed down. The owner said he couldn't make the rent."

Miller looked at him over the rim of his glasses. "I see. And you are not currently employed?"

"No."

"And you have not applied for other forms of financial aid?"

"I applied for the standard scholarship. I applied for the work-study program. I applied for the emergency fund."

Miller sighed. It was a small, controlled sound, but it carried the weight of a door closing. "The standard scholarship requires a minimum GPA of 3.0 and a demonstrated financial need based on the FAFSA. Your FAFSA indicates that your parents' income is within the threshold, Mr. Vale. That is a matter of public record. Unless you can prove that your parents are unwilling or unable to support you, which requires a notarized affidavit and a detailed explanation of their financial insolvency, you are ineligible for the need-based scholarship."

Adrian’s jaw tightened. He didn't have parents. He had a father who had left when he was twelve and a mother who had died when he was fifteen. He had no one to sign an affidavit. He had no one to call. He had no one to blame.

"I don't have parents to support me," Adrian said.

Miller picked up a pen and made a small mark on his notepad. "I understand. However, the Emergency Hardship Grant also requires a co-signer for the repayment of funds. The university is a private institution, Mr. Vale. We do not lend money. We facilitate it. Without a co-signer with a credit score of 650 or higher, we cannot approve the disbursement."

Adrian looked at the pen. He looked at Miller’s face. Miller’s face was a mask of professional neutrality. He was not a bad man. He was not a cruel man. He was simply a man who worked in a system that had no room for exceptions. The system was a machine, and Adrian was a part that did not fit.

"I don't have a credit score," Adrian said.

"I know," Miller said. "I’m sorry."

The apology was automatic. It was the same apology a computer might give when it failed to load a file. It meant nothing. It changed nothing.

Adrian stood up. His legs felt unsteady. He gripped the edge of the desk for a second, his knuckles white, before releasing it. He straightened his shirt. He smoothed the hole in his jeans as best he could, though the fabric was too worn to hide the gap.

"Is there anything else?" Adrian asked.

Miller shook his head. "I’m afraid not, Mr. Vale. I recommend you look into off-campus employment opportunities. The Career Services office has a board with listings for part-time positions. There are also several private companies that offer summer internships, though those are usually competitive."

"Internships," Adrian repeated. The word sounded foreign. It belonged to a world where you had a network, a brand, a safety net. It belonged to a world where you could afford to be unpaid for a year in exchange for a line on your resume. Adrian could not afford to be unpaid for a day.

"Thank you," Adrian said.

He turned and walked out of the office. The hallway was long and carpeted in a dark, mottled blue that made him feel like he was walking through a bruise. He passed a row of glass doors, behind which students sat in clusters, laughing, drinking coffee, scrolling through their phones. They looked solid. Real. They had bodies that filled out their clothes, faces that were flushed with blood and energy. Adrian watched them for a moment, feeling a strange, detached curiosity. They were so loud. Their voices bounced off the walls, creating a cacophony of normalcy that seemed obscene in its volume.

He walked past the Career Services office. He did not go in. He knew what he would find there. A board covered in flyers. *Waiter. Server. Janitor. Data Entry. Customer Service Rep.* All of them requiring a background check, a reference, and a schedule that would conflict with his classes. He had tried. He had tried the week before, and the week before that. He had stood in front of the board, reading the same requirements over and over, until the words blurred and his head began to throb.

He walked to the main quad. The sun was high, but the air was cool. It was early autumn, and the trees were just beginning to change color. The leaves were a mix of green and yellow, a chaotic patchwork that reflected the state of his mind. He walked to the library steps and sat down on the concrete. He pulled his knees up to his chest and rested his chin on them.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He pulled it out. It was a text from the landlord.

*Rent is due on the 1st. Late fee is $50. Please make arrangements.*

Adrian stared at the message. He had thirty-four dollars in his checking account. He had twelve dollars in cash in his pocket. He had a bag of instant noodles in his backpack, and a half-eaten granola bar that had been in there since Monday. He had no idea what to do. He had no plan. He had no backup. He had no one to call.

He looked up at the sky. The clouds were thin and white, stretched out like cotton wool. They were beautiful, in a detached, indifferent way. They did not care about his rent. They did not care about his hunger. They simply existed, moving slowly across the blue expanse, following a path that was written in the laws of physics, not in the ledger of his life.

He stood up. His legs were still shaky. He took a deep breath, filling his lungs with the cool, crisp air. It did nothing for the hunger in his stomach, but it seemed to steady his hands. He walked back toward the dorms, his steps slow and deliberate. He had to eat. He had to find a way to eat. He had to find a way to pay the rent. He had to find a way to survive.

He reached the door of his dorm room. It was a small, single-occupancy space, barely large enough for a bed, a desk, and a chair. The walls were a pale, institutional yellow. The air smelled of old paper and dust. He dropped his backpack on the floor and walked to the desk. He opened his laptop. The screen lit up, casting a blue glow over his face.

He went to the university’s job board. He scrolled through the listings. *Waiter. Server. Janitor. Data Entry.* He closed the laptop.

He opened a new tab. He went to a website he had seen advertised in the local paper. It was a small, unassuming site, with a logo that was a simple blue circle with a white cross in the center. The name was *Helix Research Institute*.

He clicked on the link.

The page loaded slowly. When it finally appeared, it was stark and clean. White background. Black text. No images. No videos. No flashy animations. Just words.

The path narrowed as the manicured lawns of the campus gave way to the cracked sidewalks of the city. Adrian’s stomach gave a low, hollow rumble, a sound that seemed to resonate in his chest rather than his belly. He hadn’t eaten a proper meal in two days, and the granola bar he’d survived on this morning was a distant memory, its sugar long burned off by the sheer effort of keeping his legs moving.

He passed a row of small businesses that catered to the student population: a print shop, a used bookstore, a coffee chain with prices that made his head spin. His feet carried him forward on autopilot, his mind cataloging the few options he had left. He could drop courses, but that meant dropping out, which meant failing his father’s expectations and his own fragile hope for a future. He could work more shifts at the library, but the manager had already squeezed every available hour out of him.

He needed something immediate. Something that paid cash, or at least a check that cleared before Friday.

A flyer taped to a telephone pole caught his eye. *URGENT: Heavy Lifting Required. Immediate Pay. No Questions Asked.* The font was jagged, the paper slightly torn at the edges. Adrian stopped, his legs aching as he leaned against the pole to read it. The address was three blocks away, in a part of the district where the buildings were older and the air smelled of diesel and wet concrete.

He shouldn’t go. His body was a temple of exhaustion, his joints stiff with cold, but the alternative was sitting in his dorm room, staring at the eviction notice he hadn’t yet received but knew was coming. He needed the money. He needed it badly enough to ignore the warning bells ringing in the back of his mind.

The building was a warehouse with a roll-up door that was half-open, revealing a dim interior filled with crates and pallets. A man stood by the door, arms crossed, watching him with a critical eye. He was large, wearing a tank top despite the chill, and his face was a map of scars.

"You the guy from the flyer?" the man asked. His voice was rough, like gravel in a blender.

Adrian swallowed, his throat dry. "Yeah. I'm here for the lifting."

The man didn’t move. He just stared at Adrian, taking in his thin frame, his pale skin, the way his jacket hung loosely on his shoulders. "You look like a stiff wind would knock you over, kid."

"I can work," Adrian said, trying to sound more confident than he felt. "I'm fast."

The man snorted. "Fast doesn't matter when you're carrying two hundred pounds of engine parts up three flights of stairs. You sure you can handle that?"

Adrian looked at the man, then at the crates inside. He thought about the rent. He thought about the tuition. He thought about the feeling of shame that came with asking for help, and the greater shame of having to accept it.

"I can handle it," Adrian said.

The man stepped aside, gesturing for him to enter. "Name's Marcus. I'm the foreman. You get it done, you get paid. You mess up, or you slow us down, you're out. No complaints."

Adrian nodded, his heart pounding in his chest. He stepped inside, the air thick with the smell of oil and dust. He picked up a crate, his muscles screaming in protest, and began to carry it toward the stairs. The weight was immense, pressing down on his shoulders, his arms trembling as he climbed. He could feel his breath coming in short, sharp gasps, his vision blurring at the edges.

He made it to the second flight when he heard a noise behind him. He turned to see Marcus standing there, watching him with a mixture of amusement and disdain.

"You're not doing this right," Marcus said. "You're straining your back. You'll be done in five minutes, and then you'll be in the hospital."

Adrian gritted his teeth, his face flushing with heat. "I can do this."

"You can do what you want," Marcus said, stepping forward. He took the crate from Adrian’s arms, his movements easy and effortless. "But if you want to keep working, you need to know the rules. We don't do half-assed work. We don't do slow work. And we don't do work that hurts you. Because a hurt worker is a useless worker."

Adrian stared at him, his pride stinging. He had expected to be mocked, or ignored, but this directness was something else. It was a challenge, and he didn't like it.

"Then show me how," Adrian said, his voice tight.

Marcus smiled, a thin, sharp expression. "That's the spirit. Now, let's see if you've got the guts to keep up."

For the next hour, Adrian worked. He lifted, he carried, he moved. He felt the burn in his muscles, the ache in his bones, the sweat dripping down his face. But he also felt something else: a sense of purpose. For the first time in days, he wasn't just surviving. He was doing something. He was contributing.

When he was done, Marcus handed him an envelope. "Here's your pay. Five hundred bucks. And a tip: next time, don't try to impress me. Just do the job."

Adrian took the envelope, his hands shaking slightly. He looked at Marcus, expecting to see mockery or contempt, but all he saw was a nod.

"Thank you," Adrian said.

"Don't thank me," Marcus said. "Thank your legs. They're the only thing that matters right now."

Adrian walked out of the warehouse, the envelope clutched in his hand. The air was colder now, the sun setting behind the buildings, casting long shadows across the street. He felt a strange sense of relief, mixed with a lingering unease. He had made the money, but at what cost? His body was still exhausted, his mind still foggy. He had pushed himself to the limit, and he knew it.

He realized, with a sinking feeling, that this was not a solution. It was a bandage on a wound that was getting deeper. He could do this again, and again, but his body would eventually break. And even if it didn't, the work was degrading. It was physical, yes, but it was also exploitative. He was being used, just like before, just in a different way.

He needed something else. Something that didn't require him to sell his body, his dignity, his future.

He looked at the envelope, then at the city around him. The lights were coming on, the streets filling with people. He was one of them, but he was different. He was running out of time.

He turned and walked in the opposite direction, toward the university, toward the only other option he had left. It was a long shot, a desperate hope, but it was the only thing that didn't feel like a betrayal of himself.

The private research hospital was a gleaming white structure on the edge of the campus, a stark contrast to the older buildings around it. It looked clean, sterile, and expensive. Adrian stood in front of the entrance, his heart pounding. He knew what he was walking into. He knew what he would have to give. But he also knew that he had no other choice.

He took a deep breath, and walked inside.

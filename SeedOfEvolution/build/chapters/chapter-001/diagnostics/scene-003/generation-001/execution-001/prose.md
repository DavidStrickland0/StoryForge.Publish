The city center was a different animal than the university district. Here, the buildings were taller, the glass cleaner, and the air smelled faintly of ozone and expensive coffee. Adrian’s stomach gave a low, hollow growl that vibrated through his ribs. He ignored it. There was no point in acknowledging the void in his belly; it was just a fact of his existence, like gravity or the rising cost of tuition.

He turned a corner and almost walked into a revolving door. It was polished chrome, gleaming under the interior lights of a building that looked like it had been designed by someone who had never had to pay for rent. A sign above the entrance read: *Aethelgard Research Institute – Advanced Clinical Trials & Experimental Medicine*.

Adrian stopped. His legs felt like they were made of wet sand. He checked his phone. The battery was at 4%. He had one text message left to send, and he needed to know if this place was real. He remembered a flyer he’d seen on a campus bulletin board three weeks ago, tucked under a poster for a debate club and a missing cat. *Earn up to $500/week for your participation in low-risk studies. Medical coverage included.*

He had assumed it was a scam. Or a cult. Or both.

But the alternative was eating another granola bar and failing his midterms.

He pushed the door. It swung open with a hydraulic sigh, carrying with it a wave of conditioned air that was so clean it tasted like nothing at all. The lobby was vast, white, and silent. There were no plants, no clutter, no personal photos. Just sleek desks, digital kiosks, and a receptionist who looked up with the polite, detached curiosity of a museum guard.

"Can I help you?" she asked.

"I... I saw the flyer," Adrian said. His voice sounded thin in the large space. "About the... studies."

The receptionist’s expression didn’t change, but something shifted behind her eyes. A fraction of a second of assessment. She glanced at his clothes—his worn-out hoodie, his scuffed sneakers, his face which was likely pale from lack of sleep and worse.

"Experimental medicine," she clarified. "You’re looking for the intake coordinator. Please, take a seat. I’ll let them know you’re here."

Adrian sat on a bench that was too hard and too clean. He watched the people walking past. They were all dressed in business casual, carrying tablets, looking purposeful. They were the ones with safety nets. The ones with parents who could cover the bill. The ones who could afford to be sick without it being a crisis.

A woman approached. She was tall, dressed in a sharp blazer, with a tablet in her hand. She smiled, but it was a professional smile, the kind that didn’t reach her eyes.

"Mr. Vale? I’m Dr. Aris Thorne. I’m the intake coordinator for the experimental program. You must be here about the recruitment drive."

"Yes," Adrian said. He stood up, his knees popping. "I’m looking for work. I saw the flyer."

Dr. Thorne nodded. "Come with me. We can discuss the details in the conference room."

She led him through a set of glass doors into a room that looked like a spaceship. There were two chairs, a table, and a wall of screens displaying complex data visualizations. Adrian looked at the screens. They were graphs, mostly. Lines going up and down. He didn’t understand what they meant, but they looked expensive.

"Sit down, Mr. Vale," Dr. Thorne said. She sat across from him, tapping on her tablet. "I’ll be direct. Our program is designed to test new, low-risk medications. In return, we provide you with a weekly stipend and fully covered medical care for the duration of the trial. Is that what you were looking for?"

Adrian nodded. "Is it... safe?"

"Safe," Dr. Thorne repeated. The word seemed to hang in the air for a moment. "It is a clinical trial. We follow strict protocols. The medications are intended to treat common conditions. In your case, we are currently enrolling participants for a study on male reproductive health. Specifically, a new compound designed to improve sperm count and motility. It’s a tedious process, but the compensation is... generous."

Adrian blinked. "Reproductive health?"

"Yes. It’s a very specific study. We need healthy young men who are willing to commit to a daily schedule. It involves taking a medication and providing daily specimens for analysis. It’s... intimate, in a sense. But it’s also very important research. We believe this compound could be a breakthrough for male infertility."

Adrian felt a flush of heat rise up his neck. He looked down at his hands. They were shaking slightly. He thought about the warehouse job. Lifting crates. Breaking his back for eighteen dollars an hour. He thought about the bar. Pouring drinks for people who looked at him like he was furniture.

This was... different. This was degrading in a way that was hard to articulate. But it was also the first time anyone had offered him money that felt like it might actually be enough to survive.

"What’s the schedule like?" he asked.

"Every day," Dr. Thorne said. "You’ll come in for a brief check-up, take your medication, and provide a specimen. It takes about an hour. You’ll be paid weekly. And you’ll have access to our medical facilities for any other health needs you might have. It’s a comprehensive package."

Adrian thought about the hunger. The way his head ached every morning when he woke up. The way his eyes blurred when he tried to read his textbooks.

"And if I stop?" he asked.

Dr. Thorne’s smile didn’t waver. "You’re free to withdraw at any time. There are no long-term contracts. But I would encourage you to consider the benefits. It’s a unique opportunity to contribute to medical science while securing your financial stability."

Adrian looked at the screens on the wall. The lines went up and down, up and down. It looked like a heartbeat. Or maybe it was just data. He didn’t know. He didn’t know anything.

He thought about the flyer. *Earn up to $500/week.*

He thought about his bank account. *$12.43.*

He thought about the warehouse. The crates. The dust.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was waiting. She was patient. She was professional. She was offering him a way out of the hole he was in. It was a hole he had dug for himself, or maybe the world had dug for him, and he was just stuck in it.

"I’d like to know more," Adrian said.

"Of course," Dr. Thorne said. She opened a folder on her tablet. "I’ll give you a summary of the study. You can review it and think about it. We don’t require you to sign anything today. But I do suggest you make a decision soon. We have a limited number of spots."

Adrian took the folder. It was thin, with crisp white paper inside. He looked at the first page. It was full of technical terms. *Spermatogenesis. Motility. Genetic stability.*

He didn’t understand half of it. But he understood the bottom line.

He stood up. His legs felt a little steadier, or maybe it was just the adrenaline. He walked to the door, then stopped.

"Thank you," he said.

Dr. Thorne nodded. "Take your time, Mr. Vale. The door is always open."

Adrian walked out of the conference room, back into the sterile white lobby. The air was still clean. The silence was still heavy. But for the first time in weeks, he didn’t feel like he was drowning. He felt like he was standing on solid ground.

He walked out of the building, into the evening air. The sun was setting, painting the sky in shades of orange and purple. The streetlights were coming on, casting long, yellow shadows on the pavement.

He pulled the folder out of his pocket. He looked at it. Then he put it back.

He didn’t know if this was the right decision. He didn’t know if he could do it. He didn’t know if he would survive.

But he knew he had to try.

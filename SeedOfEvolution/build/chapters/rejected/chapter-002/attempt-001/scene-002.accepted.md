The waiting area was a study in beige and gray. The walls were painted a shade of off-white that seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it, and the plastic chairs were the kind that squeaked when shifted. Adrian kept his eyes closed, focusing on the rhythm of his own breathing. In, out. In, out. The hum in his blood was still there, a low-frequency vibration that seemed to originate from the base of his skull and radiate outward into his limbs. It wasn’t painful, exactly, but it was intrusive. It made the silence of the room feel heavy, pressurized.

He checked the watch on his wrist. 10:22 AM. Thirty-eight minutes.

Adrian opened his eyes and looked at the door. It was solid white, with a small square window near the top. Through the glass, he could see the sterile brightness of the hallway beyond. He could also see the reflection of his own face in the pane. He looked tired. His skin was pale, almost translucent under the fluorescent lights, and there was a faint sheen of sweat on his forehead. The tremor in his hands had subsided, replaced by a tight, coiled tension that made him feel like he was holding his breath underwater.

He stood up. His legs felt unsteady, as if the floor had softened slightly. He gripped the back of a chair for support, waiting for the dizziness to pass. It did, eventually, leaving behind a faint ringing in his ears.

He needed to move. Sitting still only made the sensation worse. He walked to the small table in the corner of the room, where a stack of brochures lay next to a glass of water. He picked up one of the brochures. It was a glossy pamphlet about the clinic’s outreach programs, filled with smiling stock photos of families and text blocks in a font so small it was nearly illegible.

Adrian scanned the text, but the words blurred together. He wasn’t reading; he was just looking at the black shapes on the white paper to anchor his gaze. He tried to focus on a single sentence, but his mind kept drifting to the math problem he had been working on in his head that morning. The integral had been simple, a basic calculus exercise for his freshman seminar, but he had lost his place halfway through. He had stared at the equation until the numbers seemed to swim, and then he had simply stopped. He had put the textbook away and driven to the clinic, his hands shaking on the steering wheel.

That was the problem. The trial was eating his time, but it was also eating his focus. He had spent an hour in the car just yesterday, sitting in the parking lot, unable to make himself get out. Not because he was afraid of the procedure, but because he was afraid of what it would cost him. Not in money, but in clarity. In the ability to think straight.

He put the brochure down. The paper crinkled under his fingers. He needed to study. He needed to keep his grades up. If his GPA dropped below a certain threshold, his financial aid would be revoked. He couldn’t afford that. He couldn’t afford anything right now.

He pulled his phone out of his pocket. The screen was cracked down the middle, a spiderweb of fractures that made the display flicker when he touched it. He opened his email app. There were three new messages.

One was from the university housing office, reminding him that his rent was due in five days.
One was from his mother, asking if he had eaten lunch. He hadn’t. He had skipped breakfast, and he hadn’t eaten since the slice of cold pizza he had found in the back of his refrigerator two days ago. The message was short, polite, and tinged with a worry he couldn’t bring himself to address. He didn’t have the energy for a conversation. He didn’t have the energy for anything that required more than a nod and a grunt.
The third was from his professor, Dr. Aris Thorne’s colleague in the biology department. It was an automated notification that his weekly quiz was due at 5:00 PM.

Adrian stared at the time. 10:25 AM. He had less than five hours left in the day to complete the quiz, and he was currently trapped in a clinical waiting room, waiting for his body to stop humming.

He felt a surge of frustration so sharp it made his chest ache. He wanted to throw his phone against the wall. He wanted to scream. Instead, he put the phone back in his pocket and sat down again.

He closed his eyes and tried to remember the formula for the integral. The numbers were there, floating in the dark behind his eyelids, but they were slippery. They slipped through his grasp every time he tried to hold onto them. He could feel the medication working, or perhaps it was just the stress, but his mind felt like it was being diluted. Like he was trying to think in a language he had forgotten.

He checked the clock again. 10:30 AM.

He stood up and walked to the window. Outside, the campus was quiet. Students were walking across the quad, carrying books and coffee cups, laughing and talking. They looked solid. They looked like they had places to be that weren’t defined by a clinical protocol. They looked like they had a future that wasn’t contingent on the stability of a chemical compound in their bloodstream.

Adrian pressed his hand against the glass. It was cool against his palm. He could feel the vibration of the building’s HVAC system, a low thrum that matched the hum in his blood. He wondered if the other participants felt it too. He wondered if they were also sitting in this room, staring at the wall, wondering if their minds were still their own.

He didn’t know if he would pass the quiz. He didn’t know if he would be able to finish the week. He didn’t know if he could keep doing this. But he knew that if he stopped, he would lose his tuition. And if he lost his tuition, he would lose everything.

So he stayed. He waited. He endured the hum, the tension, the creeping sense of his own mind slipping away. He did it because he had to. He did it because there was no other option.

The clock ticked. 10:45 AM.

Adrian sat down again. He picked up the brochure and read the same sentence for the fourth time. It was about the clinic’s commitment to patient care. The words were meaningless. They were just shapes on a page. He looked at the clock.

10:58 AM.

He stood up. His legs were steady now. The hum in his blood was still there, but it was quieter, a background noise he could almost ignore. He walked to the door and opened it.

The hallway was empty. The receptionist was at her desk, typing on a keyboard. She looked up and nodded at him.

"Ready for the sample?" she asked.

Adrian nodded back. He felt a strange mix of relief and dread. The waiting was over, but the real work was just beginning. He followed her down the hall, his footsteps echoing softly on the tile floor. He kept his eyes on the floor, focusing on the pattern of the tiles, trying to keep his mind sharp.

He was Adrian Vale. He was a student. He was a participant in a clinical trial. He was a person who needed money. And he was going to do what he had to do, because he had no choice.

The door to the examination room was open. The light inside was bright and white. Adrian stepped inside, and the door closed behind him.

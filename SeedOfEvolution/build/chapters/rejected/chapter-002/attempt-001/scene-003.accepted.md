The air in Room 4 was sterile, smelling of antiseptic and cold plastic. Adrian stood by the examination table, his hands gripping the edge of the metal surface to keep them from trembling. The door had clicked shut, sealing him in with the hum of the ventilation system.

Dr. Aris Thorne was already at her desk, typing on a keyboard. She did not look up. Her dark bob was perfectly styled, not a strand out of place, and her pale eyes remained fixed on the monitor screen.

"Sit down, please," she said. Her voice was flat, efficient.

Adrian moved to the stool. The plastic was cool against his thighs. He sat up straight, forcing his spine to align despite the dull ache in his lower back. The medication had been in his system for four days now. The side effects were no longer a sudden shock; they were a persistent background noise, a low-grade fever in his thoughts that made every word he read feel slightly slippery.

Thorne turned from the computer. She held a clipboard and a pen.

"Vitals first," she said.

She approached him with a blood pressure cuff and a stethoscope. The movements were mechanical, precise. She wrapped the cuff around his left arm. The rubber felt tight, constricting. She listened to his pulse for a moment, then checked his temperature with a digital thermometer. The beep was short and sharp.

"Blood pressure is normal. Heart rate is slightly elevated, but within expected parameters for the protocol." She wrote something down. "Any changes since yesterday?"

Adrian opened his mouth. He wanted to say *yes*. He wanted to describe the way his mind felt like it was moving through wet sand, the way the numbers in his calculus textbook blurred together if he stared at them for too long. He wanted to mention the persistent hunger that sat in his stomach like a stone, the way his appetite had doubled despite the fact that he had barely eaten a full meal since the weekend.

But he looked at Thorne. She was watching him with those colorless eyes, waiting for data, not for a confession.

"No," Adrian said. The word felt heavy in his throat. "No changes."

Thorne nodded. She did not probe further. She did not ask if he was sleeping well, or if the medication was causing him distress. She simply turned to the cabinet on the wall and pulled out a small, sterile cup.

"It’s time for your sample," she said.

The routine was becoming a ritual. Adrian stood up, his legs feeling unsteady. The humiliation of the first week had faded, replaced by a numb, procedural dread. He was a participant. He was a data point. He was a body that produced results in exchange for money.

He walked to the privacy screen Thorne had set up. It was a thin, translucent plastic divider that did little to shield him from the rest of the room. He could see Thorne’s reflection in the glass, a blurred, dark shape.

"Please close the door on your way out," Thorne said. It was not a command, but it carried the weight of one.

Adrian stepped behind the screen. The air was cooler there. He closed his eyes for a second, breathing in the scent of the antiseptic. He thought about the email from his professor. *Your grade in this course is currently a C-. To maintain financial aid eligibility, you must raise this to a B- by the end of the term.*

He thought about the rent due in five days. He thought about the empty fridge in his apartment. He thought about the fact that if he quit the trial now, he would be out of school by next month.

He opened his eyes.

The act was mechanical, devoid of pleasure or pride. It was a transaction. He performed his duty, his body responding to the chemical prompts of the medication, producing what the researchers needed. It was ugly, it was dehumanizing, and it was the only thing keeping him afloat.

When he was finished, he placed the cup on the tray. His hands were shaking now, the adrenaline of the act fading into a deep, bone-aching exhaustion. He washed his hands, the water cold and shocking, and dried them on the paper towels provided.

He stepped out from behind the screen. Thorne was already holding the cup, examining the contents with a detached curiosity. She sealed it in a biohazard bag and placed it in a labeled box.

"Good," she said. "That completes the protocol for today."

Adrian felt a surge of relief so intense it made his knees weak. It was over. For today, it was over.

"Thank you," he said, his voice raspy.

Thorne looked at him. For a second, there was a flicker of something in her expression—perhaps curiosity, perhaps assessment. Then it was gone, replaced by her clinical neutrality.

"You will receive your payment within twenty-four hours. Please remember to log any side effects in the online portal before midnight. Failure to report symptoms accurately is grounds for immediate removal from the study."

Adrian nodded. He understood. He had signed the waiver. He knew the terms.

He walked to the door. His legs felt heavy, as if he were walking through water. He pushed the door open and stepped into the hallway. The fluorescent lights were bright, harsh.

He paused in the doorway. He could hear Thorne typing again, the rhythmic clack of keys against the silence of the room. He felt a sudden, sharp pang of isolation. He was surrounded by people, by institutions, by rules and regulations, but he had never felt more alone.

He closed the door.

The hallway was empty. The other participants had already left, or had not yet arrived. Adrian leaned against the wall, closing his eyes. The hum in his blood was louder now, a persistent vibration that seemed to pulse in his teeth.

He had done what he had to do. He had secured his survival for another day. But as he looked at the clock on the wall, ticking steadily toward the end of his shift, he realized that the cost was higher than the money. It was his time. It was his mind. It was the part of him that still wanted to be more than just a source of data.

He pushed off the wall and began to walk toward the exit. His steps were slow, deliberate. He had a quiz to study for, a rent to pay, and a life to live. And he had to do it all with the weight of his own body hanging over his head, a burden he could no longer ignore.

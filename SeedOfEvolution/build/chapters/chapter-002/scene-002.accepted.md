The light was gone. Not dimmed, or softened, but extinguished. The window, which had admitted a weak, gray wash of afternoon, now offered only a dark rectangle framed in the deepening blue of night.

Adrian sat up.

The movement was stiff, his joints protesting the sudden shift in gravity. He blinked, the motion slow and deliberate, as if he were moving through water. His head felt tight, a band of pressure around his temples that the four eggs and a glass of water had not been enough to break. The room was cold. It had been cold before he slept, but now the chill seemed to have settled into the walls, into the thin fabric of his blankets, into the marrow of his bones.

He swung his legs over the side of the bed. His feet hit the hardwood floor, and the shock of the cold made him pull them back up, then drop them again. He needed to move. He needed to do something.

He shuffled to the bedside table. The phone lay there, screen dark, battery icon a hollow white square. He plugged it into the charger, the thin wire stretching just enough to reach the outlet behind the bed. The little red light turned on. He waited, watching the percentage climb: 2, 3, 4.

On the other side of the room, on the single desk that served as his workspace, his altar, and his prison, sat the participant handbook.

It was a thick, glossy document, bound in a blue plastic cover that reflected the dim light of the room. It sat there like a judgment. Adrian had signed the consent forms in the hospital’s sterile office, the air recycled and smelling of antiseptic and expensive coffee. He had shaken Dr. Aris’s hand, a firm, brief clasp, and walked out into the night, the weight of the orange capsule in his pocket feeling like a stone.

Now, the stone was in his pocket, and the handbook was on his desk.

He picked it up. The paper was heavy, high-quality, the kind used for financial reports or legal briefs. He opened it to the first page.

**Participant Schedule & Protocol Guidelines**
**Study ID: FERT-204-ALPHA**
**Principal Investigator: Dr. Elias Aris, M.D., Ph.D.**
**Primary Objective: Evaluation of Oral Androgenic Stimulant (Compound 7-B) on Sperm Viability and Count.**

Adrian read the first paragraph twice. It was written in a clinical, detached style that made him feel less like a person and more like a variable in an equation. *The subject will administer one (1) 50mg capsule of Compound 7-B orally each morning, between 08:00 and 09:00 AM local time. The subject will report to the Clinical Research Wing, Room 402B, for mandatory specimen collection daily between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM.*

He checked the time on the phone screen. 4:12 PM.

The red light on the charger blinked steadily. The battery reached 15%.

Adrian closed the handbook. The words blurred together, but the times were sharp, hard edges in a soft world. 08:00 AM. He had woken up at 6:45 AM yesterday, panicked by the silence of the phone, and scrambled to get ready. He had missed the window. Or had he? The handbook didn't say what happened if he missed it. It didn't say what happened if he was late. It just stated the requirements.

He opened the handbook again, flipping to the section marked **Exclusions and Termination Criteria**. His eyes scanned the bolded text.

*4.1. Failure to adhere to the dosing schedule for more than two consecutive days will result in immediate termination from the study and forfeiture of all pending payments.*
*4.2. Failure to provide a viable specimen on three (3) or more scheduled collection days will result in immediate termination from the study and forfeiture of all pending payments.*
*4.3. Any deviation from the protocol as defined by the Principal Investigator may result in data exclusion and potential termination.*

Termination. Forfeiture.

The words hung in the air, cold and absolute. He had assumed, in his desperation, that the trial was a safety net, a flexible arrangement where a missed morning could be made up, a late arrival forgiven. He had imagined Dr. Aris as a benevolent figure, a doctor who would understand the chaos of a college student’s life.

But the handbook suggested a machine. A machine that did not understand, or forgive, or care. It only measured.

Adrian felt a cold knot form in his stomach, a tight, physical reaction that had nothing to do with hunger. He had missed the window yesterday. That was one miss. Two consecutive days would mean he was out. If he missed today, that would be two. If he missed the specimen collection tomorrow, that would be two misses on that front. He was walking on a tightrope over a pit of fire, and he hadn't even put his feet in the water yet.

He looked at the orange capsule in his hand. It was small, unassuming, a tiny pill that held the promise of his future. If he took it now, at 4:15 PM, would it count? The protocol said "between 08:00 and 09:00 AM." Taking it at 4:15 PM was a deviation. A clear, documented deviation.

He put the capsule back in his pocket. It felt heavier now, like a bomb with a slow fuse.

He needed to know. He needed to ask.

He picked up his phone. The battery was at 22%. He opened the contacts. There was only one number saved, labeled **Dr. Aris (Clinical)**. It was not a personal number; it was the main line of the research hospital. Calling it at this hour would likely connect him to an automated system or a tired nurse on duty.

He hesitated. The fear of sounding desperate, of sounding like a failure already, made his thumb hover over the green button. If he called and they said he was out, his day was over. If he called and they said he was fine, he could breathe.

He pressed the button.

The phone rang. Once. Twice. Three times.

*This is the Meridian University Research Hospital. For clinical inquiries, please press one. For billing, press two. For administrative matters, please press three.*

He pressed one.

*Please hold for the on-duty clinical coordinator.*

Hold music played. A soft, ambient piano track that was designed to be calming but only served to highlight the silence of his apartment. Adrian stood up, pacing the small room. His footsteps sounded loud on the hardwood. He passed the window, then the desk, then the bed. The room was too small for anxiety.

The music stopped.

"Meridian University Research Hospital, this is Sarah speaking. How can I help you?"

Her voice was flat, professional, devoid of the warmth he had craved.

"I'm a participant in the FERT-204 trial," Adrian said, his voice cracking slightly. He cleared his throat. "I’m Adrian Vale. I... I have a question about the dosing schedule."

A keyboard clacked in the background. The sound was sharp, mechanical.

"Name, please, Mr. Vale."

"Adrian Vale. I enrolled last week."

Another pause. The clacking continued.

"I see you here. You’re in the alpha group. What’s the question?"

"I missed my dose yesterday," Adrian said. The words tumbled out, faster than he could stop them. "I woke up late. I didn't take the capsule until... well, I haven't taken it yet. Is... is that a problem?"

The clacking stopped.

"Mr. Vale, the protocol specifies administration between 8:00 AM and 9:00 AM. What time is it now?"

"It's 4:20 PM."

A silence followed. It was not a hostile silence, but it was a heavy one. Adrian could feel the weight of the question hanging in the air, a physical pressure against his eardrums.

"If you administer the dose now, you will be in violation of the protocol window," Sarah said, her tone careful, measured. "We will need to log this as a protocol deviation. Do you understand?"

"I... I don't want to be terminated," Adrian said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "I need this. I need the payments."

"Mr. Vale, I cannot make decisions regarding protocol deviations. That is the role of the Principal Investigator. However, I can put you through to Dr. Aris’s voicemail. He is not in the office, but he checks his messages every hour. You can leave a message. Or, you can submit a deviation report via the participant portal. Do you have your login credentials?"

Adrian closed his eyes. The portal. He had created an account when he signed up, but he hadn't used it. He didn't remember the password.

"I... I don't remember my password," he said.

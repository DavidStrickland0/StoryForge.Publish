===== ACCEPTED COMPLETED SCENE =====

The hum of the fluorescent lights was a constant, low-frequency drone that seemed to vibrate in Adrian’s teeth. He kept his eyes closed, his forehead pressed against the cool laminate of the desk. The plastic chair was hard, molded to support a spine that he was beginning to feel less of. His body felt like a loose collection of parts, held together by habit and the desperate need to keep the rent paid.

Ten minutes passed. Then twenty.

The door to Room 4 clicked open. It was a soft sound, barely audible over the air conditioning, but Adrian’s head lifted from the desk. He blinked, adjusting to the harsh overhead light. Dr. Thorne stood in the doorway, her clipboard resting against her hip. She was looking at the wall-mounted clock, then at him.

She stepped inside, the door sliding shut behind her with a pneumatic hiss. She didn’t speak immediately. Instead, she walked around the small perimeter of the room, her movements precise and efficient. She checked the thermometer on the shelf, though it wasn’t connected to him. She glanced at the logbook on the desk, where his signature was still fresh ink.

Adrian straightened his back, trying to make himself look less like a man who had forgotten how to sit. He was aware of the sweat beading on his upper lip. He was aware of the way his hands trembled slightly when he placed them on the desk. He was aware that he hadn’t eaten since a half-eaten granola bar he’d found in his jacket pocket the previous night.

“You’re shivering,” Thorne said. Her voice was flat, devoid of inflection.

Adrian pulled his jacket tighter around his shoulders. “I’m fine.”

She stopped in front of him. She was taller than him, and she used that advantage to hover, her pale eyes scanning his face. She looked at the pallor of his skin, the faint blue veins visible through the thin fabric of his shirt. She looked at the way his jaw was set, the tension radiating from his neck.

“Your heart rate is elevated,” she stated. It wasn’t a question. She had likely checked the monitor before entering, or perhaps the sound of his breathing had been enough.

“It’s a warm room,” Adrian said, though the air conditioning was set to a brisk sixty-eight degrees.

Thorne’s gaze dropped to the vial he had just ingested. It was an empty, clear glass tube, sitting on the desk next to his notebook. She picked it up, turning it over in her fingers. Her expression remained unchanged, but there was a micro-second of pause in her movement, a fraction of a hesitation that Adrian caught because he was watching her too closely.

“The compound is new,” she said, setting the vial down with a sharp click. “The side effect profile is still being mapped. Nausea is common. Fatigue is expected. Skin sensitivity is… noted.”

She said the last word with a slight emphasis, as if testing the weight of it.

Adrian looked at the vial. He thought about the burning sensation in his stomach, the way his skin had felt prickly and raw against the desk. He thought about the way his thoughts had become sluggish, heavy, as if he were wading through water. He had told her ‘no’ on the log. He had checked the box for ‘no adverse reactions.’ He had done it because he knew what happened to people who reported problems. They were removed. They were data points. They were replaced.

He didn’t want to be replaced. He needed the money. He needed to drop Bio 101 so he could keep the other courses, so he could stay enrolled, so he could survive.

“I’m managing,” Adrian said.

Thorne studied him for a long moment. The silence stretched, thick and heavy. Adrian could see the reflection of his own face in the dark glass of the monitor on the wall. He looked thin. He looked tired. He looked like someone who was running out of time.

Thorne’s eyes narrowed slightly. There was a flicker of something in her face, a quick, sharp movement that vanished before Adrian could name it. Concern, perhaps. Or calculation. It was difficult to tell the difference with her.

“Stay here,” she said. Her tone was sharper now, the clinical detachment returning with force. “I’ll check on you in an hour.”

She turned and walked toward the door. Her hand rested on the handle, but she didn’t open it. She paused, looking back at him.

“Adrian,” she said, using his name for the first time since the intake process.

He looked up.

“If you feel faint, or if you experience any pain that is severe or persistent, you are to press the button on the desk.” She pointed to the red emergency call button, a small, unassuming device that looked out of place among the papers and the plastic chair. “Do not wait. Do not try to endure it. Press the button.”

Adrian stared at the button. It was a simple thing, a piece of plastic and wire. But in her hand, it felt like a lifeline. Or a trap.

“Okay,” he said.

Thorne nodded once, a stiff, mechanical motion. She opened the door and stepped out, letting the pneumatic mechanism close the gap behind her. The latch engaged with a soft click.

Adrian sat in the silence. The hum of the lights continued. The clock ticked. He looked at the red button on the desk. He didn’t touch it. He didn’t need to. He could endure this. He had to.

He leaned back in the chair, stretching his neck. The pain in his lower back was a dull ache, a reminder of the hours he had spent sitting in the diner, serving coffee to people who didn’t know his name. He closed his eyes and focused on his breathing. In. Out. In. Out.

He thought about the payment. It was due at the end of the week. It would cover the rent. It would cover the electricity. It would cover the food, if he was careful. If he skipped meals. If he endured the hunger.

He opened his eyes and looked at the clock. Forty-five minutes left.

He picked up his notebook. The paper was soft under his fingers. He looked at the list of goals. *1. Pay rent.* *2. Eat.* *3. Study.* *4. Sleep.* *5. Drop Bio 101.*

He picked up his pen. He didn’t write anything new. He just held the pen, feeling the weight of it. He thought about the classes he had missed. The lectures he had skipped. The professor who had looked at him with a mixture of pity and annoyance when he had finally shown up, looking pale and shaken.

He thought about the trial. The vial. The button. The silence.

He put the pen down. He put his head back on the desk. He waited.

The minutes crawled by. The light from the window shifted, the sun moving lower in the sky. The hum of the lights seemed to get louder, or perhaps his hearing was sharper. He could hear the distant sound of traffic, the wail of a siren, the murmur of voices in the hallway.

He was still there. He was still alive. He was still hungry.

When the door opened again, it was Thorne. She carried a small tray. On it was a glass of water and a small, sealed packet of crackers.

“Eat,” she said.

Adrian looked at the food. He hadn’t expected it. He hadn’t asked for it. But it was there.

He picked up the cracker. It was dry, salty. He chewed it slowly, swallowing the dry crumbs with difficulty. He drank the water, the cool liquid soothing the burning in his throat.

Thorne watched him eat. She didn’t say anything. She just stood there, her hands clasped in front of her, her eyes fixed on him.

When he finished, he set the glass down. His hands were steady.

Thorne nodded. She picked up the tray and carried it out. The door closed.

Adrian sat in the silence. He felt a strange, hollow warmth in his stomach. It wasn’t satisfaction. It was just the absence of pain.

He looked at the clock. Five minutes left.

He took a deep breath. He straightened his back. He picked up his notebook.

He was ready.

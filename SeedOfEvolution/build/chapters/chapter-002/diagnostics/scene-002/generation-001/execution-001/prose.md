The number on the screen was a small, red, final verdict.

$412.

Adrian stared at the digits until they blurred, the cracked glass of his phone screen catching the sterile fluorescence of the lobby. Four hundred and twelve dollars. He did the math again, his thumb hovering over the bank app, the refresh icon spinning uselessly. Rent was due Friday. Tuition was due Friday. The gap between where he was and where he needed to be was a chasm that no amount of discount noodles or skipping meals could bridge. He could sell his textbooks. He could go back to the warehouse, lifting pallets until his shoulders screamed, but the hours clashed with his lectures, and his body was already a hollowed-out vessel, humming with a low-grade vibration that felt like static electricity trapped under his skin.

He locked the phone. The screen went dark, reflecting his own pale face back at him. He looked tired. He looked hungry. He looked like a boy who had run out of ways to survive.

David remained behind the desk, his posture a study in relaxed efficiency. He did not offer a tissue. He did not offer a word of comfort. He simply waited, his hands folded on the polished surface, watching Adrian with the calm detachment of a man who had seen this exact look in a hundred other pairs of eyes. It was a professional gaze, assessing the weight of the desperation, measuring the exact pressure required to make a decision.

Adrian let out a breath he didn’t realize he had been holding. His shoulders dropped, a sudden, heavy slump that seemed to drain the last bit of tension from his frame. He looked at the schedule David had printed out, the crisp white paper resting on the desk. Eight to eight. Fifteen minutes a day. A slot had opened up because a Mr. Henderson had left for personal reasons. The details were clinical, precise, and utterly devoid of the chaos that currently ruled Adrian’s life.

He picked up the schedule. The paper was cool to the touch. He read it again, tracing the lines with his eyes. It wasn’t a job. It wasn’t a sacrifice. It was a transaction. A daily, fifteen-minute transaction that would keep the lights on and the eviction notice away.

"Is there anyone else in the room?" Adrian asked, his voice sounding rougher than he intended.

David tilted his head slightly. "You mean in the collection room?"

"No." Adrian gestured vaguely around the lobby, towards the glass partition that separated the intake area from the rest of the facility. "Is there anyone else here? Today?"

David’s expression softened, just barely. A hint of something almost human flickered across his features. "We have a few participants who come in every morning. They usually wait in the lounge before their slot." He nodded towards a set of muted blue chairs near the window. "You might see them if you wait a moment."

Adrian looked towards the lounge. Through the glass, he could see three men sitting in the blue chairs. One was scrolling through his phone, another was reading a magazine, and the third was simply staring at the wall. They looked ordinary. They looked like people waiting for a bus, or a dentist, or a coffee to finish brewing.

"Can I... can I talk to them?" Adrian asked. The question felt absurd as it left his mouth. He was a stranger. He was a desperate, hungry boy standing in a sterile lobby. Why would he want to talk to strangers?

David didn’t hesitate. "Of course. Just introduce yourself. They’re all in the same boat."

Adrian straightened his thin jacket, feeling the fabric pull tight across his ribs. He walked toward the glass partition, his sneakers squeaking faintly on the polished floor. The air in the lobby was warm, recycled, and smelled faintly of lavender disinfectant. It was a clean smell. A safe smell. It was the opposite of the damp, mildew-scented apartment he lived in, where the pipes groaned in the walls and the heat was always turned down to save money.

He reached the glass and knocked lightly. The man reading the magazine looked up. He was older than Adrian, maybe in his thirties, with a neatly trimmed beard and a calm, unbothered expression. He wore a casual button-down shirt, the sleeves rolled up to his elbows. He looked like a teacher, or an accountant. Someone who had a life that made sense.

"Can I help you?" the man asked. His voice was low, steady.

"I’m... I’m new," Adrian said. He felt his face heat up, a flush of embarrassment creeping up his neck. He hated this. He hated the vulnerability of it, the need to seek out validation from people who didn’t know his name, who didn’t care about his parents, or his debts, or the way his hands sometimes shook when he was too tired to stop them. "I’m looking at enrolling. I just... I wanted to ask. How is it? Really."

The man smiled. It wasn’t a sympathetic smile. It was a practical one. "You mean the process?"

"I mean the... the daily thing," Adrian said, unable to bring himself to say the words *semen sample*. He gestured vaguely towards the back of the facility. "The collection. Is it... is it as bad as it sounds?"

The man laughed, a short, dry sound. "It’s a job, kid. You do the work, you get paid. The rest is just... plumbing." He leaned back in his chair, crossing his legs. "I’ve been doing it for three weeks. It’s the most consistent part of my week. More consistent than my actual job, if you ask me."

Adrian blinked. "You have a job?"

"Consulting," the man said. "But this pays better for the time commitment. And the medical coverage is decent. I haven't had a headache in three weeks. That alone was worth the hassle."

Adrian looked at the other two men. The one on his phone looked up, nodding. The third one, who had been staring at the wall, turned his head. He was younger, maybe Adrian’s age, with a nervous energy that Adrian recognized. He shifted in his chair, rubbing the back of his neck.

"Is it awkward?" the younger man asked, his voice quiet.

"Only if you make it awkward," the older man said. "I treat it like brushing my teeth. You do it, you move on. No one judges you. The nurses are used to it. They’re professional. They don't make it a big deal."

Adrian felt a strange, cold knot in his stomach loosen. The fear he had been carrying, the sense of isolation, the belief that he was the only one in this world willing to do this, the only one who had to sell his dignity for survival—it began to dissolve. It wasn't a unique sacrifice. It was a shared reality. A common trade.

He looked back at the older man. "Do you... do you regret it?"

The man considered the question. He took a sip from a paper cup he had been holding. "Regret? No. I have rent paid. I have food on the table. My back doesn't hurt. I sleep better. What’s there to regret?" He shrugged. "If it makes you feel better, I used to worry about it too. Now it’s just... Tuesday."

Adrian looked at his own reflection in the glass. He looked the same as he had a minute ago. Thin. Tired. But the tremor in his hands had stopped. The shaking was gone.

He turned and walked back to the reception desk. David was still waiting, his posture unchanged. But Adrian moved differently. The hesitation that had plagued him for the last hour was gone, replaced by a cold, clear resolve. He wasn't doing this for himself. He wasn't doing it for his pride. He was doing it for the money. For the survival. For the simple, undeniable fact that it worked.

"David," Adrian said, stopping in front of the desk.

The coordinator looked up. "Yes?"

"Let's do it," Adrian said.

David’s eyes narrowed slightly, a flicker of satisfaction crossing his face. He reached under the desk and pulled out a folder. It was thick, heavy, and smelled of new paper. He placed it on the desk, sliding it towards Adrian.

"Smart decision," David said. His voice was smooth, professional. "I’ll walk you through the final clauses. Then we can get you set up for your first slot."

Adrian picked up the folder. The paper was crisp, cold, and real. He could feel the weight of it in his hands. It was a contract. It was a promise. It was a way out.

He opened the folder. The first page was a list of terms, conditions, and legal disclaimers. He read the first line, his eyes scanning the text. It was dense, complex, and utterly binding.

He read it again.

And then he began to sign.

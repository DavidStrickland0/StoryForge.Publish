The hallway floor was cold. Adrian felt the chill through the thin soles of his sneakers, a dull ache that radiated up his shins and settled in his lower back. He adjusted the stack of forms in his left hand, the paper edges biting into his palm. The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed with a low, persistent hum that seemed to vibrate in his teeth.

He stopped walking.

The restroom was three doors down, past the supply closet and the break room where the glass door was permanently frosted. Adrian leaned against the wall, closing his eyes for a second. The nausea was still there, a low-grade swirl in his stomach that had nothing to do with hunger. It was a chemical thing, a side effect of the baseline exam, or maybe just the stress of having a stranger’s hands on his body. The side effects list had warned him: fatigue, nausea, changes in libido. He was already checking two of the boxes.

He opened his eyes and looked at the restroom door. The sign was a standard white plate with a blue male symbol. Below it, a small placard read: *No Food or Drink.*

Adrian let out a breath he didn’t realize he’d been holding. He pushed off the wall and walked toward it.

The restroom was small, tiled in a beige that had yellowed with age. There were two urinals, a sink with a paper towel dispenser that had been empty for days, and a mirror that was slightly warped. The air smelled of industrial cleaner and stale urine.

He locked the stall door. The latch clicked with a heavy, final sound.

Sitting down, Adrian felt a wave of relief so intense it made his knees weak. He let his head drop forward, resting his forehead against his crossed arms. The cold tile of the seat was uncomfortable, but it was solid. It was real. It wasn’t a doctor’s clipboard or a sterile examination table. It was just a toilet.

He sat there for ten minutes.

During that time, he did not think about the future. He did not think about tuition, or rent, or the fact that he had eaten a slice of cold pizza for dinner the night before. He simply existed in the quiet, sterile box, listening to the hum of the ventilation fan and the distant sound of the city traffic outside the clinic windows.

When he finally stood up, he felt a strange lightness. It wasn’t happiness. It was the absence of the pressure that had been building in his bladder for two hours. He washed his hands, the water running cold, and dried them on the paper towel he found hidden behind the dispenser. He looked at himself in the warped mirror.

His face looked the same as it had when he walked in. Thin. Tired. The scar on his shoulder was hidden under his shirt, but he could feel the faint, pale line of it against his skin.

He unlocked the stall and walked out.

The hallway was empty. The receptionist was still at her desk, typing. She didn’t look up. Adrian walked past her, his footsteps silent on the carpet. He needed to get to Room 4 again. The medication was waiting. The next step in the process was waiting.

He felt a sudden, irrational urge to turn around and run. To walk out of the clinic, to jump into the first bus he saw, to go back to his tiny apartment and sleep until the next shift at the diner. To forget that he had signed a document that bound him to this place, to this routine, to this specific, invasive procedure.

But he didn’t run.

He walked to the door of Room 4.

The door was closed. Adrian reached out and turned the handle. It was unlocked. He pushed it open and stepped inside.

Dr. Thorne was sitting at her desk, reviewing a file. She looked up as he entered. Her pale eyes scanned him, checking his posture, his expression, his compliance.

"Sit down," she said.

Adrian walked to the chair and sat. The plastic was cold again. He placed the stack of forms on the desk in front of her.

"Ready for the medication?" she asked.

"Yes," Adrian said.

Thorne reached into a drawer and pulled out a small, clear vial. It was filled with a liquid that looked exactly like water. She uncapped it and poured the contents into a small cup.

"Drink it," she said.

Adrian picked up the cup. The liquid was cold. He drank it in one go. It tasted like nothing. Like water. Like air.

"Good," Thorne said. She wrote something on her clipboard. "You will stay here for one hour. Do not leave the room. Do not eat or drink anything else. You will report any changes in your body to me immediately."

Adrian nodded. He placed the empty cup on the desk.

"Will I feel anything?" he asked.

Thorne looked at him. Her expression was neutral. "The medication is designed to stimulate reproductive cell production. Side effects are possible. Fatigue, nausea, changes in libido. If you experience any of these, report them. If you do not report them, you will be removed from the study."

Adrian swallowed. The words were simple. Clinical. Detached.

He understood.

He looked at the clock on the wall. It was 9:52 AM. He had to be there by 8:00 AM every day. He had to provide a sample every day. He had to take this medication every day. And he had to stay here for an hour every day.

It was a routine. A mechanical, repetitive, dehumanizing routine.

And it was his survival.

Adrian leaned back in the chair. He closed his eyes. He thought about his apartment. The thin mattress. The empty fridge. The silence.

He thought about the diner. The smell of grease. The sound of the coffee machine. The weight of the plates he washed.

He thought about the money. The payment that would cover his tuition. The safety net that was so thin it was almost transparent.

He opened his eyes.

"Dr. Thorne," he said.

She looked up.

"What happens after an hour?" he asked.

"You will be free to go," she said. "You will return tomorrow at 8:00 AM."

Adrian nodded. He looked at the wall. He looked at the door. He looked at the doctor.

He was waiting.

The hour began.

The first ten minutes were the hardest. Adrian sat in the chair, his hands folded in his lap, his eyes fixed on a spot on the floor. He tried to focus on his breathing. In for four counts. Hold for four. Out for four.

It didn’t work.

His mind started to race. He thought about the sample. The cup. The act of providing it. The way he had had to explain it to himself, to justify it, to make it a thing he did for a reason. A reason that was bigger than his own comfort, his own dignity, his own sense of self.

He thought about the hunger. The gnawing, persistent hunger that had been with him for days. The way his stomach growled, a low, rumbling sound that he could hear even in the quiet of the clinic.

He thought about the fear. The fear that he was doing the wrong thing. The fear that he was losing himself. The fear that he would never get it back.

Then, the nausea came.

It started as a slight tightness in his chest. A fluttering in his stomach. Adrian closed his eyes and tried to push it away. He focused on his hands. He looked at the veins in his wrists. He counted the hairs on his arms.

It didn’t work.

The nausea grew. It turned into a wave, a rolling, churning sensation that moved from his stomach to his throat. Adrian opened his eyes. His vision blurred.

He looked at Dr. Thorne. She was still sitting at her desk, reviewing her file. She hadn’t noticed.

Adrian forced himself to stay still. He forced himself to stay quiet. He forced himself to endure.

The nausea passed.

It was replaced by a strange, warm feeling. A tingling in his limbs. A lightness in his head.

Adrian looked at his hands. They were shaking.

He didn’t know what was happening. He didn’t know what the medication was doing to him. He didn’t know if this was a side effect, or if this was the beginning of something else.

He just knew that he had to stay.

He had to stay for the hour.

He had to stay for the payment.

He had to stay for the future.

Adrian closed his eyes and waited.

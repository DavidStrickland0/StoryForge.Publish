He did not feel the need to explain why he was doing this, or to justify his need for the money. He was nineteen years old, and he was broke, and he was going to sign a contract that would require him to do something embarrassing every single day for the next several months. The ink was dark and permanent. Adrian capped the pen and set it down. He looked at the document, the lines of text now binding him to the facility, to the daily visits, to the small, private humiliations that would become his routine. He felt a strange sense of relief, a tension releasing from his shoulders that he hadn’t realized he was carrying. It was a relief that felt almost guilty, as if he had just made a bargain he couldn’t take back, but one that was necessary.

He looked at David, who was already turning to the next task, his attention shifting to the next potential participant. The transaction was complete. Adrian was no longer a prospect; he was a participant.

David was on the phone, his voice low and steady, discussing something about a sleep disorder trial. The world had moved on, indifferent to Adrian’s decision. But Adrian had made his choice. He had signed his name. He had bought his survival.

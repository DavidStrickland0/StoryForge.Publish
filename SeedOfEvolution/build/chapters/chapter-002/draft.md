The elevator was out again. It had been out for three weeks, ever since the main shaft cable frayed and the building manager decided the repair bill was higher than the rent he collected from the two empty units. Adrian took the stairs.

His legs were leaden things, dragged along by gravity rather than will. The fourth floor was a long, grinding climb. He counted the steps, a dull, rhythmic mantra to keep his breathing steady. *One, two, three.* The air in the stairwell was stale, carrying the scent of boiled cabbage from the second floor and the metallic tang of rust from the fire exit door. By the time he reached the landing, his chest felt tight, a physical warning that his body was running on fumes.

He fumbled for his keys. His fingers were cold, stiff against the metal teeth of the keyring. The lock stuck, as it always did, demanding a specific, fussy combination of turns to release. Finally, the deadbolt slid home.

The apartment was a single room, barely larger than a studio, with a kitchenette alcove that felt less like a space and more like an afterthought. The walls were painted a shade of beige that had turned slightly yellow with age, and the carpet was a matted, gray shag that had long since lost its pile. A single window looked out onto a narrow alleyway where a dumpster sat under a perpetual film of grime.

Adrian dropped his bag on the floor. It made a heavy, dull thud. He stood in the center of the room, listening to the silence. It wasn’t true silence; there was the constant, low hum of the refrigerator, a sound that had become the backdrop to his life. There was also the distant, muffled thump of bass from the apartment below, and the occasional shriek of a siren passing on the street.

He walked to the kitchenette. The counter was cluttered: a stack of unpaid bills, a calculator, a pen, and a small, empty bowl. The refrigerator was small, a compact unit that held little more than a single carton of orange juice, a half-depleted carton of milk, and a plastic bag containing four eggs. He had checked the inventory twice that morning before leaving. He knew exactly what he had.

He opened the fridge door. The cold air washed over his face, a sharp, clean contrast to the stuffy heat of the room. He took out the eggs and the milk.

There was no rice in the cabinet. No noodles. No lentils. Just a single, crumpled packet of saltines that he had forgotten to open, sitting on the top shelf.

Adrian leaned against the counter, his hands resting on the edge. He felt the hunger then, a sharp, acidic ache in his stomach. It was a familiar companion, a constant presence that had become part of his identity. He hadn’t eaten since yesterday afternoon, a small portion of cafeteria soup that he had saved from his shift at the library. He had skipped breakfast to save the money for the bus fare, a decision that had left him hollow and trembling by the time he arrived at the hospital.

He didn’t feel proud of it. He didn’t feel dignified. He felt tired.

He took the eggs into his hand. They were small, pale brown things, cold to the touch. He cracked them into the bowl, his movements automatic, practiced. The shells broke with a sharp, dry sound. The yolk was bright yellow, the white clear and runny. He added a pinch of salt, just a pinch, from the small shaker on the counter.

He heated a small amount of water in the kettle, the electric coil glowing red. He poured the hot water over the eggs, letting them steep. It wasn’t cooking, not really. It was a way of softening them, making them easier to swallow.

He sat at the small table by the window. The light was gray, diffuse, filtering through the dirty glass. He ate the eggs in small, careful bites, chewing slowly, trying to extract every bit of sustenance he could. The taste was bland, metallic, but it was food. It was protein. It was enough.

While he ate, he took the orange capsule out of his pocket. It sat on the table, a small, bright spot of color in the drab landscape. He looked at it for a long moment. It was a simple thing, a plastic shell and a white powder. But in that powder was his future. Or at least, a small, manageable piece of it.

He took a sip of water, washing the last of the eggs down his throat. He felt a small, fragile sense of relief. He had done it. He had signed the papers. He had the medication. He had the money, or at least, the promise of it.

He put the capsule in his pocket, right where it would be easy to find. He would take it every morning, as instructed. He would visit the clinic every day. He would provide the samples. And in return, he would survive.

The apartment was quiet again. The hum of the refrigerator, the distant bass, the sound of his own breathing. Adrian sat at the table, his hands resting on the cool surface of the wood. He felt the weight of the day ahead of him, the long, tedious cycle of visits and tests and waiting. But for now, in this small, cramped room, he was safe. He was fed. And he had a plan.

He closed his eyes for a moment, letting the exhaustion wash over him. It was a heavy, physical thing, pressing down on his shoulders, settling into his bones. He allowed himself to feel it, to acknowledge the toll it had taken. He had been running on empty for so long, stretching himself so thin, trying to hold everything together with sheer willpower. It had worked, barely. But it had cost him.

Now, he had a safety net. It was a thin, fragile thing, made of promises and paperwork and small doses of medication. But it was there. And that was enough.

He opened his eyes and looked at the window. The gray sky was darkening, the clouds thickening. It was going to rain. He knew that. The air had that particular, heavy quality, that sense of impending pressure that meant water was coming.

He stood up, his legs stiff from sitting. He washed the bowl and the spoon, drying them with a rag that was already damp. He put them away. He wiped down the counter, removing the few crumbs that had fallen. He was a neat person, or at least, he tried to be. In a small space like this, mess was a threat. It grew, it spread, it consumed. He couldn’t afford to let that happen.

He checked his phone. 10:45 AM. He had time. He could sleep, just for a little while. Just to rest, to let his body repair itself, even if only for a few hours.

He lay down on the bed, pulling the thin blanket up to his chin. The mattress was old, the springs worn, the fabric thin and rough. But it was warm. And it was his.

He closed his eyes, and for the first time in days, he felt his breathing slow. The hunger was still there, a low, persistent hum in his stomach, but it was manageable. He was not starving. He was not dying. He was just tired. And tiredness, at least, could be cured by sleep.

He slept, deeply and dreamlessly, the gray light of the afternoon filtering through the window, casting long, soft shadows across the room.

The light was gone. Not dimmed, or softened, but extinguished. The window, which had admitted a weak, gray wash of afternoon, now offered only a dark rectangle framed in the deepening blue of night.

Adrian sat up.

The movement was stiff, his joints protesting the sudden shift in gravity. He blinked, the motion slow and deliberate, as if he were moving through water. His head felt tight, a band of pressure around his temples that the four eggs and a glass of water had not been enough to break. The room was cold. It had been cold before he slept, but now the chill seemed to have settled into the walls, into the thin fabric of his blankets, into the marrow of his bones.

He swung his legs over the side of the bed. His feet hit the hardwood floor, and the shock of the cold made him pull them back up, then drop them again. He needed to move. He needed to do something.

He shuffled to the bedside table. The phone lay there, screen dark, battery icon a hollow white square. He plugged it into the charger, the thin wire stretching just enough to reach the outlet behind the bed. The little red light turned on. He waited, watching the percentage climb: 2, 3, 4.

On the other side of the room, on the single desk that served as his workspace, his altar, and his prison, sat the participant handbook.

It was a thick, glossy document, bound in a blue plastic cover that reflected the dim light of the room. It sat there like a judgment. Adrian had signed the consent forms in the hospital’s sterile office, the air recycled and smelling of antiseptic and expensive coffee. He had shaken Dr. Aris’s hand, a firm, brief clasp, and walked out into the night, the weight of the orange capsule in his pocket feeling like a stone.

Now, the stone was in his pocket, and the handbook was on his desk.

He picked it up. The paper was heavy, high-quality, the kind used for financial reports or legal briefs. He opened it to the first page.

**Participant Schedule & Protocol Guidelines**
**Study ID: FERT-204-ALPHA**
**Principal Investigator: Dr. Elias Aris, M.D., Ph.D.**
**Primary Objective: Evaluation of Oral Androgenic Stimulant (Compound 7-B) on Sperm Viability and Count.**

Adrian read the first paragraph twice. It was written in a clinical, detached style that made him feel less like a person and more like a variable in an equation. *The subject will administer one (1) 50mg capsule of Compound 7-B orally each morning, between 08:00 and 09:00 AM local time. The subject will report to the Clinical Research Wing, Room 402B, for mandatory specimen collection daily between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM.*

He checked the time on the phone screen. 4:12 PM.

The red light on the charger blinked steadily. The battery reached 15%.

Adrian closed the handbook. The words blurred together, but the times were sharp, hard edges in a soft world. 08:00 AM. He had woken up at 6:45 AM yesterday, panicked by the silence of the phone, and scrambled to get ready. He had missed the window. Or had he? The handbook didn't say what happened if he missed it. It didn't say what happened if he was late. It just stated the requirements.

He opened the handbook again, flipping to the section marked **Exclusions and Termination Criteria**. His eyes scanned the bolded text.

*4.1. Failure to adhere to the dosing schedule for more than two consecutive days will result in immediate termination from the study and forfeiture of all pending payments.*
*4.2. Failure to provide a viable specimen on three (3) or more scheduled collection days will result in immediate termination from the study and forfeiture of all pending payments.*
*4.3. Any deviation from the protocol as defined by the Principal Investigator may result in data exclusion and potential termination.*

Termination. Forfeiture.

The words hung in the air, cold and absolute. He had assumed, in his desperation, that the trial was a safety net, a flexible arrangement where a missed morning could be made up, a late arrival forgiven. He had imagined Dr. Aris as a benevolent figure, a doctor who would understand the chaos of a college student’s life.

But the handbook suggested a machine. A machine that did not understand, or forgive, or care. It only measured.

Adrian felt a cold knot form in his stomach, a tight, physical reaction that had nothing to do with hunger. He had missed the window yesterday. That was one miss. Two consecutive days would mean he was out. If he missed today, that would be two. If he missed the specimen collection tomorrow, that would be two misses on that front. He was walking on a tightrope over a pit of fire, and he hadn't even put his feet in the water yet.

He looked at the orange capsule in his hand. It was small, unassuming, a tiny pill that held the promise of his future. If he took it now, at 4:15 PM, would it count? The protocol said "between 08:00 and 09:00 AM." Taking it at 4:15 PM was a deviation. A clear, documented deviation.

He put the capsule back in his pocket. It felt heavier now, like a bomb with a slow fuse.

He needed to know. He needed to ask.

He picked up his phone. The battery was at 22%. He opened the contacts. There was only one number saved, labeled **Dr. Aris (Clinical)**. It was not a personal number; it was the main line of the research hospital. Calling it at this hour would likely connect him to an automated system or a tired nurse on duty.

He hesitated. The fear of sounding desperate, of sounding like a failure already, made his thumb hover over the green button. If he called and they said he was out, his day was over. If he called and they said he was fine, he could breathe.

He pressed the button.

The phone rang. Once. Twice. Three times.

*This is the Meridian University Research Hospital. For clinical inquiries, please press one. For billing, press two. For administrative matters, please press three.*

He pressed one.

*Please hold for the on-duty clinical coordinator.*

Hold music played. A soft, ambient piano track that was designed to be calming but only served to highlight the silence of his apartment. Adrian stood up, pacing the small room. His footsteps sounded loud on the hardwood. He passed the window, then the desk, then the bed. The room was too small for anxiety.

The music stopped.

"Meridian University Research Hospital, this is Sarah speaking. How can I help you?"

Her voice was flat, professional, devoid of the warmth he had craved.

"I'm a participant in the FERT-204 trial," Adrian said, his voice cracking slightly. He cleared his throat. "I’m Adrian Vale. I... I have a question about the dosing schedule."

A keyboard clacked in the background. The sound was sharp, mechanical.

"Name, please, Mr. Vale."

"Adrian Vale. I enrolled last week."

Another pause. The clacking continued.

"I see you here. You’re in the alpha group. What’s the question?"

"I missed my dose yesterday," Adrian said. The words tumbled out, faster than he could stop them. "I woke up late. I didn't take the capsule until... well, I haven't taken it yet. Is... is that a problem?"

The clacking stopped.

"Mr. Vale, the protocol specifies administration between 8:00 AM and 9:00 AM. What time is it now?"

"It's 4:20 PM."

A silence followed. It was not a hostile silence, but it was a heavy one. Adrian could feel the weight of the question hanging in the air, a physical pressure against his eardrums.

"If you administer the dose now, you will be in violation of the protocol window," Sarah said, her tone careful, measured. "We will need to log this as a protocol deviation. Do you understand?"

"I... I don't want to be terminated," Adrian said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "I need this. I need the payments."

"Mr. Vale, I cannot make decisions regarding protocol deviations. That is the role of the Principal Investigator. However, I can put you through to Dr. Aris’s voicemail. He is not in the office, but he checks his messages every hour. You can leave a message. Or, you can submit a deviation report via the participant portal. Do you have your login credentials?"

Adrian closed his eyes. The portal. He had created an account when he signed up, but he hadn't used it. He didn't remember the password.

"I... I don't remember my password," he said.

"You can reset it via email. It will take up to twenty minutes to process. Once you reset it, you can submit the deviation report. In the meantime, I would advise you not to administer the dose until you have received clarification from Dr. Aris. The connection dropped, leaving Adrian with the sound of his own breathing and the low, rhythmic hum of the apartment’s faulty radiator. He stared at the phone, the screen glowing with the standby image of the hospital logo—a stylized serpent wrapped around a caduceus, rendered in a sterile, clinical blue. The battery icon ticked up to twenty-four percent. A small, fragile victory.

He set the device down on the desk, next to the cold coffee mug and the single orange capsule. The pill looked absurdly small in the harsh, fluorescent light of the room. It was a capsule of bright orange plastic, smooth and unblemished, sitting on the scratched surface of the laminate desk like a piece of candy left out for a child. It looked harmless. It looked like a multivitamin or a laxative. It did not look like the difference between having a place to sleep and sleeping under the bridge that crossed the university campus.

Adrian felt the knot in his stomach loosen, replaced by a hollow, grinding ache. He was hungry. He had been hungry since he woke up, a persistent, gnawing sensation that seemed to live just behind his ribs. He reached into the drawer, past the tangled charging cables and the loose change, and found the small, clear glass bottle containing his supply of Compound 7-B. There were thirty capsules inside, each one a small, vibrant orange dot. Thirty days of survival. Thirty days of dignity, purchased at the price of his privacy and his mornings.

He uncorked the bottle. The glass smelled faintly of nothing, a sterile, clean scent that belonged in a laboratory, not in his cramped, dust-mite-infested studio apartment. He shook the bottle gently. The capsules clinked against each other, a dry, plastic sound. He poured one out. It landed on his palm, light as a bead of plastic.

Adrian looked at the capsule in his hand. The instructions had been specific: *One (1) capsule of Compound 7-B orally, once daily between 08:00 and 09:00 AM.* He checked the time on his phone. It was 4:23 PM. He was well outside the window. Sarah had said not to take it without approval. But the approval was twenty minutes away, and his stomach was cramping.

He thought about the alternative. If he didn’t take it now, he would have to wait. He would sit in this cold, quiet room, staring at the peeling paint on the ceiling, feeling his energy drain away. He would be hungry, exhausted, and anxious. And in twenty minutes, if the email didn’t arrive, if the server was down, if the password reset failed, he would be back to square one. He would have to call again. He would have to explain. He would have to beg.

But if he took it now, it was a deviation. It was a protocol violation. It was a risk.

Adrian looked at the capsule again. It was just a pill. It was a promise. It was the only promise he had left.

He swallowed it.

He didn’t have water. He hadn’t bought water in three days. He used the last sip from his cold coffee mug, the liquid brown and bitter. He tilted his head back, tilting his throat to guide the pill down. It felt like a small, hard pebble sliding down his esophagus, settling somewhere in the dark, empty hollow of his stomach.

He lowered his head and breathed out. The air in the room was stale, smelling of old paper and the faint, chemical tang of the heating unit. He sat back in his chair, the springs creaking under his weight. The headache behind his temples was still there, a tight band of pressure that made it hard to focus. He blinked, his vision blurring for a second, then clearing.

He looked at the bottle. There were twenty-nine capsules left.

He felt a strange, quiet emptiness in the room. It wasn’t just the silence; it was the absence of expectation. For the last six months, his life had been a series of urgent, panicked reactions. The panic of the tuition bill. The panic of the rent notice. The panic of the empty fridge. Now, for the first time in a long time, he had done something. He had taken a step. It was a small, undignified step, taken in a drafty apartment with a broken chair, but it was a step.

The capsule was in his system. It was a chemical, a compound, a molecule. It was not magic. It was not a cure. It was a tool. A very expensive, very intrusive, very degrading tool. But it was a tool that paid the bills.

Adrian picked up his phone. The battery was at twenty-five percent. He opened the email app. The inbox was empty. He opened the calendar. He had a lecture in two hours. General Biology. He had to go. He had to show up. He had to sit in the back row and pretend to understand the mitotic spindle when his head felt like it was being squeezed in a vice.

He stood up. His joints popped. The room seemed slightly dimmer, the shadows in the corners of the ceiling stretching out like long, thin fingers. He put on his jacket, the denim rough against his skin. He checked his pockets. The wallet with the card. The bus pass. The keys.

He looked at the desk one last time. The bottle of orange capsules sat in the center of the clutter, a bright, defiant dot of color in the grey world. He felt a sudden, sharp hunger, a deep, resonant rumble that seemed to come from the core of his chest. The drug was in his blood, or perhaps it wasn’t, but the expectation was there. The promise was there.

He locked the door behind him. The click of the deadbolt was the only sound in the hallway, a final, definitive mark on the day. He walked to the stairs, the air outside colder, biting at his exposed skin. He needed to eat. He needed to survive. And somewhere in his stomach, a small, orange pill was beginning to do its work.

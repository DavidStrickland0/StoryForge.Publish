The bathroom stall was the only place in the clinic where the air didn’t smell like antiseptic and industrial floor cleaner. It smelled like cheap lemon disinfectant, which was a small mercy. Adrian leaned against the tiled wall, closing his eyes, and tried to breathe without his chest feeling too tight. The nausea was a low, persistent hum in his gut, a physical reminder that he had swallowed a pill that was supposed to make him more fertile.

He checked his phone. 10:15 AM.

He had about forty-five minutes before he had to be back in Room 4 for the final sign-off and the administrative processing of his daily attendance. It was enough time to do what he needed to do, if he was fast.

Adrian pushed off the wall and walked to the sink. He didn’t turn on the tap; he knew the water would be cold, and he was already shivering, partly from the draft in the hallway and partly from the way the medication seemed to make his skin feel thin. He washed his hands anyway. The friction of the soap against his palms was a grounding sensation, a way to feel his own body.

He looked at his reflection in the mirror above the sink. He looked tired. His skin was sallow, his eyes dark-rimmed. He was nineteen, but in the harsh light of the clinic bathroom, he looked older. He looked like someone who had already given up on things he couldn’t afford.

He needed to think about his schedule.

He pulled out his notebook. It was a battered spiral-bound thing, the kind he bought at the pharmacy for fifty cents. The pages were filled with cramped handwriting, equations, and dates. On the front page, he had written his semester goals.

*1. Pass Intro to Econ.*
*2. Pass Bio 101.*
*3. Pay Rent.*
*4. Eat.*

He looked at the list. It was a simple list, but it was a list he wasn’t sure he could keep.

The trial required him to be at the clinic every day from 8:00 AM to 12:00 PM. That was four hours. He had to take the bus, walk the last mile, go through intake, wait for his number to be called, take the medication, provide the sample, wait for the observation period, and then sign out. It was a rigid, unyielding block of time in the middle of his day.

He had classes at 9:00 AM.

He had classes at 1:00 PM.

He worked nights at the diner, from 6:00 PM to 2:00 AM.

He looked at the math. It didn’t work.

If he went to the 9:00 AM class, he would be late to the clinic. If he went to the clinic, he would be late to the 9:00 AM class. If he tried to do both, he would be exhausted by the time he got to the 1:00 PM class, and he would be too tired to work his night shift.

He had already missed two classes this week. He had told the professor he was sick. The professor had looked at him with a skepticism that Adrian felt in his bones.

He couldn’t keep doing that. He couldn’t afford to fail Intro to Econ. The tuition was a fixed cost, a debt that would hang over his head for years. If he failed, he would have to retake the course, which meant more money he didn’t have.

He had to make a choice.

He looked at the list again.

*3. Pay Rent.*
*4. Eat.*

He couldn’t skip the trial. The money was the only thing keeping him alive. The money was the only thing keeping him in the apartment, the only thing keeping him in the program.

He had to skip the classes.

He closed the notebook. The decision felt heavy, a weight in his stomach. He was choosing survival over education, but he was also choosing his own sanity over the illusion that he could do it all.

He walked out of the bathroom and into the hallway. The corridor was empty. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, a steady, irritating drone. He walked toward the main office, where the receptionist sat behind a high counter.

He didn’t want to talk to her. He didn’t want to look at the stack of forms waiting for his signature. He didn’t want to see Dr. Thorne’s face, even if she wasn’t looking at him.

But he had to.

He walked into the office. The receptionist looked up, her gray eyes scanning him. She didn’t smile. She didn’t ask how he was. She just pointed to a chair.

"Sit down," she said. "Thorne will be with you in a minute."

Adrian sat down. He placed his notebook on his lap. He opened it again. He looked at the list.

He took a pen from his pocket. He wrote a new line under *4. Eat.*

*5. Drop Bio 101.*

He stared at the words. It was a small act of rebellion, a tiny crack in the structure of his life. He was giving up on something he wanted, in order to keep something he needed.

It wasn’t fair. It wasn’t right. But it was real.

The door to Room 4 opened. Dr. Thorne walked out. She was tall, lean, and precise. Her dark hair was pulled back into a tight bun. She looked at Adrian, and for a second, he thought she might say something. But she didn’t. She just nodded, once, and walked back into the room.

Adrian stood up. His legs felt weak, a side effect of the hunger and the medication. He followed her.

The room was the same as before. The plastic chair, the examination table, the computer on the desk. It was a sterile, efficient space, designed to strip away everything that was personal and leave only the data.

Dr. Thorne sat down at her desk. She opened a file folder. She looked at the forms Adrian had brought in.

"You have the daily attendance log," she said. Her voice was flat, professional. "Sign here."

She pointed to a line at the bottom of the page.

Adrian picked up the pen. He signed his name. The ink was black, dry, and permanent.

"Next," she said. "The side effect log. Have you experienced any nausea, fatigue, or changes in libido?"

Adrian looked at her. He wanted to say yes. He wanted to say that he was miserable, that he was hungry, that he was tired, that his life was falling apart. He wanted to say that he was dropping a class because he couldn’t afford to keep it.

But he knew what she would do. She would write it down. She would file it. She would look at him with the same clinical detachment she had used during the examination.

He would be a data point. A variable. A subject.

So he said, "No."

Dr. Thorne looked at him. Her pale eyes were unreadable. She didn’t push. She didn’t question him. She just nodded, once, and wrote something in her file.

"Good," she said. "Then you are cleared for today’s dose."

She reached under her desk and pulled out a small vial. It was clear, colorless, and unassuming. It looked like water.

"Swallow it," she said.

Adrian took the vial. He uncapped it. He tilted his head back and let the liquid slide down his throat. It tasted like nothing.

"Sit," she said. "You will remain in the room for one hour. We need to monitor for any immediate adverse reactions."

Adrian sat down in the plastic chair. He placed his notebook on his lap. He opened it. He looked at the list.

*1. Pass Intro to Econ.*
*2. Pass Bio 101.*
*3. Pay Rent.*
*4. Eat.*
*5. Drop Bio 101.*

He looked at the vial in his hand. He looked at the clock on the wall.

One hour.

He had one hour to think. He had one hour to decide what he was going to do next.

He closed the notebook. He put his head down on the desk. He didn’t sleep, but he rested. He let the silence of the room wrap around him. He let the hum of the lights and the distant sound of the city fill his ears.

He was alive. He was hungry. He was tired. But he was here.

And for now, that was enough.

The hum of the fluorescent lights was a constant, low-frequency drone that seemed to vibrate in Adrian’s teeth. He kept his eyes closed, his forehead pressed against the cool laminate of the desk. The plastic chair was hard, molded to support a spine that he was beginning to feel less of. His body felt like a loose collection of parts, held together by habit and the desperate need to keep the rent paid.

Ten minutes passed. Then twenty.

The door to Room 4 clicked open. It was a soft sound, barely audible over the air conditioning, but Adrian’s head lifted from the desk. He blinked, adjusting to the harsh overhead light. Dr. Thorne stood in the doorway, her clipboard resting against her hip. She was looking at the wall-mounted clock, then at him.

She stepped inside, the door sliding shut behind her with a pneumatic hiss. She didn’t speak immediately. Instead, she walked around the small perimeter of the room, her movements precise and efficient. She checked the thermometer on the shelf, though it wasn’t connected to him. She glanced at the logbook on the desk, where his signature was still fresh ink.

Adrian straightened his back, trying to make himself look less like a man who had forgotten how to sit. He was aware of the sweat beading on his upper lip. He was aware of the way his hands trembled slightly when he placed them on the desk. He was aware that he hadn’t eaten since a half-eaten granola bar he’d found in his jacket pocket the previous night.

“You’re shivering,” Thorne said. Her voice was flat, devoid of inflection.

Adrian pulled his jacket tighter around his shoulders. “I’m fine.”

She stopped in front of him. She was taller than him, and she used that advantage to hover, her pale eyes scanning his face. She looked at the pallor of his skin, the faint blue veins visible through the thin fabric of his shirt. She looked at the way his jaw was set, the tension radiating from his neck.

“Your heart rate is elevated,” she stated. It wasn’t a question. She had likely checked the monitor before entering, or perhaps the sound of his breathing had been enough.

“It’s a warm room,” Adrian said, though the air conditioning was set to a brisk sixty-eight degrees.

Thorne’s gaze dropped to the vial he had just ingested. It was an empty, clear glass tube, sitting on the desk next to his notebook. She picked it up, turning it over in her fingers. Her expression remained unchanged, but there was a micro-second of pause in her movement, a fraction of a hesitation that Adrian caught because he was watching her too closely.

“The compound is new,” she said, setting the vial down with a sharp click. “The side effect profile is still being mapped. Nausea is common. Fatigue is expected. Skin sensitivity is… noted.”

She said the last word with a slight emphasis, as if testing the weight of it.

Adrian looked at the vial. He thought about the burning sensation in his stomach, the way his skin had felt prickly and raw against the desk. He thought about the way his thoughts had become sluggish, heavy, as if he were wading through water. He had told her ‘no’ on the log. He had checked the box for ‘no adverse reactions.’ He had done it because he knew what happened to people who reported problems. They were removed. They were data points. They were replaced.

He didn’t want to be replaced. He needed the money. He needed to drop Bio 101 so he could keep the other courses, so he could stay enrolled, so he could survive.

“I’m managing,” Adrian said.

Thorne studied him for a long moment. The silence stretched, thick and heavy. Adrian could see the reflection of his own face in the dark glass of the monitor on the wall. He looked thin. He looked tired. He looked like someone who was running out of time.

Thorne’s eyes narrowed slightly. There was a flicker of something in her face, a quick, sharp movement that vanished before Adrian could name it. Concern, perhaps. Or calculation. It was difficult to tell the difference with her.

“Stay here,” she said. Her tone was sharper now, the clinical detachment returning with force. “I’ll check on you in an hour.”

She turned and walked toward the door. Her hand rested on the handle, but she didn’t open it. She paused, looking back at him.

“Adrian,” she said, using his name for the first time since the intake process.

He looked up.

“If you feel faint, or if you experience any pain that is severe or persistent, you are to press the button on the desk.” She pointed to the red emergency call button, a small, unassuming device that looked out of place among the papers and the plastic chair. “Do not wait. Do not try to endure it. Press the button.”

Adrian stared at the button. It was a simple thing, a piece of plastic and wire. But in her hand, it felt like a lifeline. Or a trap.

“Okay,” he said.

Thorne nodded once, a stiff, mechanical motion. She opened the door and stepped out, letting the pneumatic mechanism close the gap behind her. The latch engaged with a soft click.

Adrian sat in the silence. The hum of the lights continued. The clock ticked. He looked at the red button on the desk. He didn’t touch it. He didn’t need to. He could endure this. He had to.

He leaned back in the chair, stretching his neck. The pain in his lower back was a dull ache, a reminder of the hours he had spent sitting in the diner, serving coffee to people who didn’t know his name. He closed his eyes and focused on his breathing. In. Out. In. Out.

He thought about the payment. It was due at the end of the week. It would cover the rent. It would cover the electricity. It would cover the food, if he was careful. If he skipped meals. If he endured the hunger.

He opened his eyes and looked at the clock. Forty-five minutes left.

He picked up his notebook. The paper was soft under his fingers. He looked at the list of goals. *1. Pay rent.* *2. Eat.* *3. Study.* *4. Sleep.* *5. Drop Bio 101.*

He picked up his pen. He didn’t write anything new. He just held the pen, feeling the weight of it. He thought about the classes he had missed. The lectures he had skipped. The professor who had looked at him with a mixture of pity and annoyance when he had finally shown up, looking pale and shaken.

He thought about the trial. The vial. The button. The silence.

He put the pen down. He put his head back on the desk. He waited.

The minutes crawled by. The light from the window shifted, the sun moving lower in the sky. The hum of the lights seemed to get louder, or perhaps his hearing was sharper. He could hear the distant sound of traffic, the wail of a siren, the murmur of voices in the hallway.

He was still there. He was still alive. He was still hungry.

When the door opened again, it was Thorne. She carried a small tray. On it was a glass of water and a small, sealed packet of crackers.

“Eat,” she said.

Adrian looked at the food. He hadn’t expected it. He hadn’t asked for it. But it was there.

He picked up the cracker. It was dry, salty. He chewed it slowly, swallowing the dry crumbs with difficulty. He drank the water, the cool liquid soothing the burning in his throat.

Thorne watched him eat. She didn’t say anything. She just stood there, her hands clasped in front of her, her eyes fixed on him.

When he finished, he set the glass down. His hands were steady.

Thorne nodded. She picked up the tray and carried it out. The door closed.

Adrian sat in the silence. He felt a strange, hollow warmth in his stomach. It wasn’t satisfaction. It was just the absence of pain.

He looked at the clock. Five minutes left.

He took a deep breath. He straightened his back. He picked up his notebook.

He was ready.

The fluorescent lights in the hallway hummed with a low, persistent buzz that Adrian felt in his teeth. He walked past the reception desk, where the gray-eyed woman was typing rapidly, her fingers a blur over the keyboard. She didn't look up. He didn't stop to say goodbye. There was nothing to say.

The automatic doors slid open, letting in a gust of cool air that seemed to suck the warmth straight out of his skin. Adrian shivered, pulling his jacket tighter around himself. The fabric felt rough against his arms, a tactile irritation that hadn't been there yesterday. He blinked, forcing his eyes to focus on the sidewalk. The pavement looked textured, every crack and pebble exaggerated by the sudden sensitivity of his nerves.

He had four hours until his next shift at the diner. Four hours that were supposed to be for studying.

Adrian checked his phone. The battery icon was a small red sliver. He had left it on his desk in Room 4, charging. He cursed under his breath, the sound harsh and foreign in his own ears. He would have to go back later. Or he would have to wait until the end of the day. Either way, it was a loss.

He began to walk. The campus was quiet in the late morning, the students who had morning classes already gone, and those who had afternoon classes not yet arrived. The leaves on the oak trees were turning a brittle yellow, dry and ready to shatter underfoot. Adrian stepped over a pile of them, the rustle loud in the silence.

He reached the library. The building was a concrete block, heavy and imposing. He swiped his ID card, the green light flashing. The interior smelled of dust and old paper, a scent that usually comforted him. Today, it smelled stale.

He found a small table in the corner, away from the windows. He pulled out his notebook, the pages dense with his own cramped handwriting. Organic Chemistry. The chapter on metabolic pathways. He opened to the section on cellular respiration.

The words swam.

Adrian blinked, rubbing his eyes with the heel of his hand. The letters remained blurry, drifting apart. He waited, forcing his breathing to slow. The air in the library was still, but his body felt like it was vibrating. A low thrum in his chest, a tingling in his fingertips.

He tried to read the first sentence again. *Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvate.*

He knew that. He had known it for weeks. But the connection between the word *glycolysis* and the concept of *conversion* felt distant, like trying to remember a dream after waking. He stared at the page until the letters burned into his retinas.

He picked up his pen. He wrote the first word of his notes. *Glyco.*

The pen slipped. It rolled off the edge of the table and clattered onto the floor.

Adrian froze. He stared at the pen, then at the empty space where it had been. His heart was hammering against his ribs, a frantic, irregular beat. He could feel the blood moving through his veins, hot and thick.

He bent down, his joints protesting with a dull ache. He picked up the pen. His hands were shaking.

He sat back down. He looked at the notebook. The word *Glyco* was smudged, the ink bleeding into the paper.

He closed the notebook.

The silence of the library pressed in on him. He could hear the hum of the ventilation system, the distant shuffling of feet, the faint creak of the building settling. He felt exposed. Vulnerable.

He stood up. His legs felt weak, the muscles in his thighs trembling. He walked to the exit, his steps slow and deliberate. He didn't look at the other students. He didn't want to see their faces. He didn't want to be seen.

He stepped out into the sunlight. The brightness was intense, a physical weight on his eyes. He squinted, his head throbbing in time with his pulse.

He leaned against the brick wall of the building, closing his eyes. The pain in his head was a dull, persistent ache, a low-frequency drone that he could not turn off. He thought about the money in his account. The small deposit. The promise of more to come.

He thought about the taste of the crackers. The bland, salty flavor. The way his stomach had clenched when he swallowed.

He opened his eyes. The world was sharp and clear, but it felt like it was happening to someone else. He pushed off the wall and began to walk again.

He had to get to the diner. He had to work. He had to earn the money that would keep him alive.

He walked until the campus was behind him, until the noise of the city replaced the silence of the library. The sounds of traffic, the chatter of pedestrians, the honking of horns. It was a chaos that was almost comforting in its indifference.

He reached the diner at 1:45 PM. The bell above the door jingled as he entered. The smell of grease and coffee hit him, rich and heavy. It made his stomach growl, a hollow, painful sound.

He walked to the back, to the changing room. He locked the door. He leaned against it, letting out a breath he didn't realize he had been holding.

He looked at himself in the mirror. His face was pale, drawn. His eyes were dark, hollowed out by fatigue. He looked like a ghost.

He pulled off his jacket. He rolled up his sleeves. His arms were thin, the bones of his wrists prominent. He flexed his hands, watching the tendons move under the skin.

He was still here. He was still breathing.

He tied his apron around his waist. He picked up his tray. He walked out onto the floor.

The diner was busy. The lunch rush was in full swing. Waiters moved between the tables, their voices raised, their movements quick and efficient. Adrian moved with them, his steps light, his eyes focused on the customers.

He approached a table in the corner. A man was sitting there, his head down, his hands wrapped around a coffee cup. He looked up as Adrian approached.

"Can I get you anything else?" Adrian asked. His voice was steady. Calm.

The man shook his head. "No, thank you. Just the check, please."

"Right away." Adrian wrote the order on his notepad. He walked to the register, punched in the amount, and printed the check.

He walked back to the table. The man was still looking at him. There was something in his eyes. A curiosity. A suspicion.

"Rough day?" the man asked.

Adrian smiled. It was a small, thin smile. "Just a long one," he said.

The man nodded. He took the check and put it on the table. "Thanks, kid."

Adrian turned and walked away. He felt the weight of the gaze on his back, a physical pressure. He kept his shoulders straight. He kept his head up.

He walked to the next table. He took the next order. He printed the next check.

He did this again and again, a machine, a function. He served the customers. He cleaned the tables. He wiped the counters.

He did not think. He did not feel.

He was just here.

The shift ended at 6:00 PM. The last customer left. The manager counted the register. The doors were locked.

Adrian stood in the back, leaning against the counter. He was tired. He was hungry. He was empty.

He looked at the clock. 6:15 PM.

He had to go back to the clinic. He had to provide the sample.

He picked up his bag. He walked out into the evening. The air was cool, the sky a deep purple. The city lights began to flicker on, one by one, a constellation of artificial stars.

He began to walk.

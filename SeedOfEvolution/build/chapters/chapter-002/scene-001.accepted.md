David swiped his finger across the tablet screen, the glossy display reflecting the sterile white ceiling lights. He didn’t look up. He was scrolling through a document, his movements precise and unhurried, as if he were organizing a bookshelf rather than handling a legal contract that would dictate Adrian’s bodily autonomy for the next three months.

Adrian leaned forward, his elbows hovering just above the polished surface of the reception desk. His knees knocked together, a small, involuntary tremor he couldn’t dampen. The lobby was warm, heated by some high-efficiency system that hummed so quietly he could only feel the vibration through the soles of his sneakers. It was a contrast to the chill that still clung to his bones, a residual cold from the autumn wind that had been biting at his thin jacket for the past hour.

On the screen, a table of figures materialized. Bold, sans-serif numbers.

*Monthly Stipend: $1,200.00.*

Adrian stared at the zero. The second zero. The decimal point.

"Is that per day?" he asked, his voice sounding hollow in his own ears. He cleared his throat, trying to push the rasp out, trying to sound like a person who had slept more than three hours in the last twenty-four.

"Per month, yes," David said. He finally looked up. His eyes were pale, a washed-out gray that seemed to match the clinical beige of the walls. He wore a crisp white shirt, the top button undone, a small, tasteful tie clipped at the collar. There was no sweat on his forehead. No shadow under his eyes. "Paid on the first of the month via direct deposit. You’ll need to provide banking information at enrollment."

Adrian’s mouth went dry. The hunger in his stomach had stopped being a sharp, nagging pain and had become a dull, constant ache, a heavy stone sitting in his gut. He looked at the number again. $1,200. It wasn’t just enough to cover the rent. It wasn’t just enough to pay the tuition balance that was currently glowing red on his email inbox. It was enough to buy food. It was enough to buy a coat that didn’t make him look like a scarecrow in the wind. It was enough to keep the lights on.

"Okay," Adrian said. The word felt heavy in his mouth.

David scrolled down. The table of figures disappeared, replaced by a dense block of text. Legal jargon. *Hereinafter referred to as the Participant.* *Consent to non-invasive sampling.* *Waiver of liability for incidental discomfort.*

Adrian squinted. The text was small. His vision blurred at the edges, a familiar symptom of the low blood sugar that had been his constant companion since he’d skipped lunch to save two dollars. He blinked, forcing his eyes to focus, but the words seemed to slide across the page, refusing to stick in his brain.

"I can’t read this," Adrian admitted. The honesty surprised him. He expected David to sigh, to offer a simpler version, to perhaps suggest he come back when he had had something to eat.

David didn’t sigh. He didn’t offer a simpler version. He turned the tablet slightly so the screen was angled toward Adrian, then he began to speak, his tone flat and procedural.

"The daily obligation is simple. You will take one oral tablet, provided by us, with a full glass of water. This must be done at the same time every day, within a two-hour window."

Adrian nodded, his hand unconsciously gripping the edge of the desk. The wood was cool and smooth under his fingers.

"Then," David continued, "you will provide a sample. Every day."

The air in the lobby seemed to thin. Adrian felt a sudden, irrational spike of heat in his face. He looked at David, waiting for the clarification, the euphemism.

"Urine?" Adrian asked. The word felt clumsy, crude, out of place in the sterile environment.

David’s expression didn’t change. "Semen."

The word hung in the air, sharp and clinical.

Adrian felt his blood drain from his face. The hunger in his stomach twisted into a knot of nausea. He looked at the ceiling, at the recessed lights, counting them to keep from looking directly at David. He counted the first recessed light. He counted the second recessed light. He counted the third recessed light. He counted the fourth recessed light.

"Every day?" Adrian asked. His voice was barely a whisper.

"Every day. For the duration of the trial, which is twelve weeks. The sample must be provided in a designated clinical room. We will provide the collection vessel. You are expected to complete the process within ten minutes of entering the room. If you are unable to do so, you will be asked to leave, and the day’s stipend will be forfeited."

David said it like he was describing a bus route. David described it with an efficient indifference. David was indifferent.

Adrian looked down at his hands. They were pale, the blue veins standing out sharply under the thin skin. He turned his hand over, looking at the palm, as if he could find a sign there, a warning, a way out. He couldn’t. He just saw the dirt under his fingernails, the faint callus on his thumb from gripping a pen.

It was humiliating. The thought of it, the routine of it, made his skin crawl. To stand in a room, alone, and perform this act for a camera, for a scientist, for a paycheck. To reduce his body to a data point, a source of fluid, a thing that produced samples.

But then he thought of the eviction notice taped to his door. He thought of the letter from the financial aid office, the polite, bureaucratic language that denied his request for emergency assistance. *Insufficient initial funding.* He thought of the granola bar he had eaten two days ago, the last piece of food he had owned, and how it had tasted like cardboard and regret.

He looked at David. The coordinator was watching him, not with sympathy, but with assessment. He was waiting. He was measuring the time it took for Adrian to process the information.

"How many people are in the trial?" Adrian asked.

"Currently, there are fourteen active participants," David said. "The trial is at capacity. We are not accepting new applications until a slot opens, unless a current participant withdraws. You have a slot because Mr. Henderson withdrew last week."

"Mr. Henderson?"

"Personal reasons," David said. He didn’t elaborate. He didn’t need to.

Adrian felt a strange, cold clarity settle over him. The nausea was still there, a low hum in his chest, but it was distant. It was a problem for later. Right now, there was a choice. He could walk out. He could go back to his apartment, which smelled like mildew and old sweat, and he could starve. He could fail his classes. He could be thrown out. He could be nothing.

Or he could sign the paper. He could swallow the pill. He could do the thing. And he could eat.

Adrian looked at the tablet. The legal text was still there, scrolling slowly, a river of words that didn’t matter anymore. He didn’t need to read every clause. He didn’t need to understand the fine print about liability or intellectual property. He knew what it was. He knew what it cost.

He didn’t sign it. He wasn’t ready to sign it. But he knew, with a certainty that felt like a physical weight in his bones, that he was going to.

He pushed his hand back from the edge of the desk, his fingers leaving faint, damp prints on the polished surface. He straightened his back, forcing the tension out of his shoulders. He looked David in the eye.

"I need to see the schedule," Adrian said.

David’s eyes flickered, a tiny shift in his posture. He had expected hesitation. He had expected a refusal. He had not expected a question about logistics.

"Of course," David said. He tapped the screen again. A calendar appeared. Days, marked in green and red. "The collection room is open from eight in the morning until eight in the evening. You can choose your slot. It is recommended that you choose a time that fits into your academic schedule, as the process takes approximately fifteen to twenty minutes, including preparation."

Adrian looked at the calendar. He saw the days of the week. He saw the days he had classes. He saw the gaps.

"Can I come in during the day?" he asked.

"Yes," David said.

Adrian nodded. He didn’t say thank you. There was nothing to thank him for. David had just handed him a leash and a bowl of food.

He looked at the tablet one last time. The numbers were still there. The text was still there. It was a contract. It was a cage.

But it was a cage with a door, and the key was in his own hand.

Adrian pulled his phone out of his pocket. The screen was cracked, the battery icon in the top right corner glowing a faint, pulsing red. He unlocked it, his thumb trembling slightly, and opened his email app. He needed to check his bank balance. He needed to see the number. He needed to see how far away the ground was.

David watched him, silent. He did not hover. He did not interrupt. He simply waited, his posture relaxed, his hands resting on the desk. He was patient. He was professional. He was a man who sold solutions to desperate people, and he knew that desperation was a slow, burning thing.

Adrian looked at the balance. He looked at the calendar. He looked at David.

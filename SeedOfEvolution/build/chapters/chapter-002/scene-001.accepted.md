The bathroom stall was the only place in the clinic where the air didn’t smell like antiseptic and industrial floor cleaner. It smelled like cheap lemon disinfectant, which was a small mercy. Adrian leaned against the tiled wall, closing his eyes, and tried to breathe without his chest feeling too tight. The nausea was a low, persistent hum in his gut, a physical reminder that he had swallowed a pill that was supposed to make him more fertile.

He checked his phone. 10:15 AM.

He had about forty-five minutes before he had to be back in Room 4 for the final sign-off and the administrative processing of his daily attendance. It was enough time to do what he needed to do, if he was fast.

Adrian pushed off the wall and walked to the sink. He didn’t turn on the tap; he knew the water would be cold, and he was already shivering, partly from the draft in the hallway and partly from the way the medication seemed to make his skin feel thin. He washed his hands anyway. The friction of the soap against his palms was a grounding sensation, a way to feel his own body.

He looked at his reflection in the mirror above the sink. He looked tired. His skin was sallow, his eyes dark-rimmed. He was nineteen, but in the harsh light of the clinic bathroom, he looked older. He looked like someone who had already given up on things he couldn’t afford.

He needed to think about his schedule.

He pulled out his notebook. It was a battered spiral-bound thing, the kind he bought at the pharmacy for fifty cents. The pages were filled with cramped handwriting, equations, and dates. On the front page, he had written his semester goals.

*1. Pass Intro to Econ.*
*2. Pass Bio 101.*
*3. Pay Rent.*
*4. Eat.*

He looked at the list. It was a simple list, but it was a list he wasn’t sure he could keep.

The trial required him to be at the clinic every day from 8:00 AM to 12:00 PM. That was four hours. He had to take the bus, walk the last mile, go through intake, wait for his number to be called, take the medication, provide the sample, wait for the observation period, and then sign out. It was a rigid, unyielding block of time in the middle of his day.

He had classes at 9:00 AM.

He had classes at 1:00 PM.

He worked nights at the diner, from 6:00 PM to 2:00 AM.

He looked at the math. It didn’t work.

If he went to the 9:00 AM class, he would be late to the clinic. If he went to the clinic, he would be late to the 9:00 AM class. If he tried to do both, he would be exhausted by the time he got to the 1:00 PM class, and he would be too tired to work his night shift.

He had already missed two classes this week. He had told the professor he was sick. The professor had looked at him with a skepticism that Adrian felt in his bones.

He couldn’t keep doing that. He couldn’t afford to fail Intro to Econ. The tuition was a fixed cost, a debt that would hang over his head for years. If he failed, he would have to retake the course, which meant more money he didn’t have.

He had to make a choice.

He looked at the list again.

*3. Pay Rent.*
*4. Eat.*

He couldn’t skip the trial. The money was the only thing keeping him alive. The money was the only thing keeping him in the apartment, the only thing keeping him in the program.

He had to skip the classes.

He closed the notebook. The decision felt heavy, a weight in his stomach. He was choosing survival over education, but he was also choosing his own sanity over the illusion that he could do it all.

He walked out of the bathroom and into the hallway. The corridor was empty. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, a steady, irritating drone. He walked toward the main office, where the receptionist sat behind a high counter.

He didn’t want to talk to her. He didn’t want to look at the stack of forms waiting for his signature. He didn’t want to see Dr. Thorne’s face, even if she wasn’t looking at him.

But he had to.

He walked into the office. The receptionist looked up, her gray eyes scanning him. She didn’t smile. She didn’t ask how he was. She just pointed to a chair.

"Sit down," she said. "Thorne will be with you in a minute."

Adrian sat down. He placed his notebook on his lap. He opened it again. He looked at the list.

He took a pen from his pocket. He wrote a new line under *4. Eat.*

*5. Drop Bio 101.*

He stared at the words. It was a small act of rebellion, a tiny crack in the structure of his life. He was giving up on something he wanted, in order to keep something he needed.

It wasn’t fair. It wasn’t right. But it was real.

The door to Room 4 opened. Dr. Thorne walked out. She was tall, lean, and precise. Her dark hair was pulled back into a tight bun. She looked at Adrian, and for a second, he thought she might say something. But she didn’t. She just nodded, once, and walked back into the room.

Adrian stood up. His legs felt weak, a side effect of the hunger and the medication. He followed her.

The room was the same as before. The plastic chair, the examination table, the computer on the desk. It was a sterile, efficient space, designed to strip away everything that was personal and leave only the data.

Dr. Thorne sat down at her desk. She opened a file folder. She looked at the forms Adrian had brought in.

"You have the daily attendance log," she said. Her voice was flat, professional. "Sign here."

She pointed to a line at the bottom of the page.

Adrian picked up the pen. He signed his name. The ink was black, dry, and permanent.

"Next," she said. "The side effect log. Have you experienced any nausea, fatigue, or changes in libido?"

Adrian looked at her. He wanted to say yes. He wanted to say that he was miserable, that he was hungry, that he was tired, that his life was falling apart. He wanted to say that he was dropping a class because he couldn’t afford to keep it.

But he knew what she would do. She would write it down. She would file it. She would look at him with the same clinical detachment she had used during the examination.

He would be a data point. A variable. A subject.

So he said, "No."

Dr. Thorne looked at him. Her pale eyes were unreadable. She didn’t push. She didn’t question him. She just nodded, once, and wrote something in her file.

"Good," she said. "Then you are cleared for today’s dose."

She reached under her desk and pulled out a small vial. It was clear, colorless, and unassuming. It looked like water.

"Swallow it," she said.

Adrian took the vial. He uncapped it. He tilted his head back and let the liquid slide down his throat. It tasted like nothing.

"Sit," she said. "You will remain in the room for one hour. We need to monitor for any immediate adverse reactions."

Adrian sat down in the plastic chair. He placed his notebook on his lap. He opened it. He looked at the list.

*1. Pass Intro to Econ.*
*2. Pass Bio 101.*
*3. Pay Rent.*
*4. Eat.*
*5. Drop Bio 101.*

He looked at the vial in his hand. He looked at the clock on the wall.

One hour.

He had one hour to think. He had one hour to decide what he was going to do next.

He closed the notebook. He put his head down on the desk. He didn’t sleep, but he rested. He let the silence of the room wrap around him. He let the hum of the lights and the distant sound of the city fill his ears.

He was alive. He was hungry. He was tired. But he was here.

And for now, that was enough.

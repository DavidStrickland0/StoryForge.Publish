The elevator was out again. It had been out for three weeks, ever since the main shaft cable frayed and the building manager decided the repair bill was higher than the rent he collected from the two empty units. Adrian took the stairs.

His legs were leaden things, dragged along by gravity rather than will. The fourth floor was a long, grinding climb. He counted the steps, a dull, rhythmic mantra to keep his breathing steady. *One, two, three.* The air in the stairwell was stale, carrying the scent of boiled cabbage from the second floor and the metallic tang of rust from the fire exit door. By the time he reached the landing, his chest felt tight, a physical warning that his body was running on fumes.

He fumbled for his keys. His fingers were cold, stiff against the metal teeth of the keyring. The lock stuck, as it always did, demanding a specific, fussy combination of turns to release. Finally, the deadbolt slid home.

The apartment was a single room, barely larger than a studio, with a kitchenette alcove that felt less like a space and more like an afterthought. The walls were painted a shade of beige that had turned slightly yellow with age, and the carpet was a matted, gray shag that had long since lost its pile. A single window looked out onto a narrow alleyway where a dumpster sat under a perpetual film of grime.

Adrian dropped his bag on the floor. It made a heavy, dull thud. He stood in the center of the room, listening to the silence. It wasn’t true silence; there was the constant, low hum of the refrigerator, a sound that had become the backdrop to his life. There was also the distant, muffled thump of bass from the apartment below, and the occasional shriek of a siren passing on the street.

He walked to the kitchenette. The counter was cluttered: a stack of unpaid bills, a calculator, a pen, and a small, empty bowl. The refrigerator was small, a compact unit that held little more than a single carton of orange juice, a half-depleted carton of milk, and a plastic bag containing four eggs. He had checked the inventory twice that morning before leaving. He knew exactly what he had.

He opened the fridge door. The cold air washed over his face, a sharp, clean contrast to the stuffy heat of the room. He took out the eggs and the milk.

There was no rice in the cabinet. No noodles. No lentils. Just a single, crumpled packet of saltines that he had forgotten to open, sitting on the top shelf.

Adrian leaned against the counter, his hands resting on the edge. He felt the hunger then, a sharp, acidic ache in his stomach. It was a familiar companion, a constant presence that had become part of his identity. He hadn’t eaten since yesterday afternoon, a small portion of cafeteria soup that he had saved from his shift at the library. He had skipped breakfast to save the money for the bus fare, a decision that had left him hollow and trembling by the time he arrived at the hospital.

He didn’t feel proud of it. He didn’t feel dignified. He felt tired.

He took the eggs into his hand. They were small, pale brown things, cold to the touch. He cracked them into the bowl, his movements automatic, practiced. The shells broke with a sharp, dry sound. The yolk was bright yellow, the white clear and runny. He added a pinch of salt, just a pinch, from the small shaker on the counter.

He heated a small amount of water in the kettle, the electric coil glowing red. He poured the hot water over the eggs, letting them steep. It wasn’t cooking, not really. It was a way of softening them, making them easier to swallow.

He sat at the small table by the window. The light was gray, diffuse, filtering through the dirty glass. He ate the eggs in small, careful bites, chewing slowly, trying to extract every bit of sustenance he could. The taste was bland, metallic, but it was food. It was protein. It was enough.

While he ate, he took the orange capsule out of his pocket. It sat on the table, a small, bright spot of color in the drab landscape. He looked at it for a long moment. It was a simple thing, a plastic shell and a white powder. But in that powder was his future. Or at least, a small, manageable piece of it.

He took a sip of water, washing the last of the eggs down his throat. He felt a small, fragile sense of relief. He had done it. He had signed the papers. He had the medication. He had the money, or at least, the promise of it.

He put the capsule in his pocket, right where it would be easy to find. He would take it every morning, as instructed. He would visit the clinic every day. He would provide the samples. And in return, he would survive.

The apartment was quiet again. The hum of the refrigerator, the distant bass, the sound of his own breathing. Adrian sat at the table, his hands resting on the cool surface of the wood. He felt the weight of the day ahead of him, the long, tedious cycle of visits and tests and waiting. But for now, in this small, cramped room, he was safe. He was fed. And he had a plan.

He closed his eyes for a moment, letting the exhaustion wash over him. It was a heavy, physical thing, pressing down on his shoulders, settling into his bones. He allowed himself to feel it, to acknowledge the toll it had taken. He had been running on empty for so long, stretching himself so thin, trying to hold everything together with sheer willpower. It had worked, barely. But it had cost him.

Now, he had a safety net. It was a thin, fragile thing, made of promises and paperwork and small doses of medication. But it was there. And that was enough.

He opened his eyes and looked at the window. The gray sky was darkening, the clouds thickening. It was going to rain. He knew that. The air had that particular, heavy quality, that sense of impending pressure that meant water was coming.

He stood up, his legs stiff from sitting. He washed the bowl and the spoon, drying them with a rag that was already damp. He put them away. He wiped down the counter, removing the few crumbs that had fallen. He was a neat person, or at least, he tried to be. In a small space like this, mess was a threat. It grew, it spread, it consumed. He couldn’t afford to let that happen.

He checked his phone. 10:45 AM. He had time. He could sleep, just for a little while. Just to rest, to let his body repair itself, even if only for a few hours.

He lay down on the bed, pulling the thin blanket up to his chin. The mattress was old, the springs worn, the fabric thin and rough. But it was warm. And it was his.

He closed his eyes, and for the first time in days, he felt his breathing slow. The hunger was still there, a low, persistent hum in his stomach, but it was manageable. He was not starving. He was not dying. He was just tired. And tiredness, at least, could be cured by sleep.

He slept, deeply and dreamlessly, the gray light of the afternoon filtering through the window, casting long, soft shadows across the room.
